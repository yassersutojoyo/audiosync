# AudioSync AirPlay sender specification

A behavioral specification for AudioSync's own AirPlay sender: a Rust crate that finds AirPlay speakers on the local network, opens a session with each one, and plays one shared audio stream on all of them in sync, every speaker with its own delay. It is written so the crate can be built by someone who reads only this document, the RFCs it cites and the documentation of the crates they choose.

AirPlay 1 (also called RAOP or AirTunes v2) is covered in full: enough for a sender that works with open and password-protected speakers, does Apple TV PIN pairing, and keeps several speakers in sync with a per-speaker delay. AirPlay 2 is covered for one receiver at a time, and for several at once on one PTP timeline the sender leads (P204 to P212, a later stage): HomeKit pairing, the encrypted channels, realtime audio, and NTP or PTP timing. Stereo pairs and the speakers an Apple TV plays through are covered as targets whose members each get a session of their own (P171). Section 11 says plainly what is still unknown.

Provenance. Written on 2026-10-19 from public sources: community protocol write-ups, project documentation pages, Apple's public documentation and codec description, and the RFCs. Facts are restated in our own words; the community write-ups carry no license, so none of their text is reused. The Apple TV pairing write-up ends with a short code example; only its prose description and its test vector were used. Revised the same day under a two-team clean-room: whoever writes this document may read open-source implementations, GPL code included, and turns what they learn into behavior-only clauses with sources; whoever writes the crate reads only this document, the RFCs it cites and crate documentation. The revision read the source of two open-source receivers and, for how a sender opens a connection to a device that requires verification, of two open-source senders; clauses that rest on them say what a receiver expects from a sender in our own words and carry no code from any of them. Nothing here describes how the engine AudioSync bundled before works inside. ## Conventions

- MUST, MUST NOT, SHOULD, SHOULD NOT and MAY are normative, in the sense of RFC 2119.
- Each clause states one behavior of the sender. It ends with *Source:* (the kind of source the fact comes from; "derived" means computed from values in a published example rather than stated in words) and, where AudioSync needs something the sources don't settle, *Required:* (the choice the crate MUST implement). "*Source:* AudioSync design" marks a clause that is a requirement only.
- A *Source:* of "observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders" reports what such a receiver or sender does, as read in its source. It is one implementation's reading of the protocol, not Apple's, and two such receivers agreeing doesn't make a behavior Apple's either.
- A *Source:* of "public user reports" cites TXT records and outcomes that users of open-source senders posted in public issue threads. It is what one network showed at one firmware version, not a rule; a clause resting on it names the hardware check that settles it.
- A *Source:* of "test-receiver run" reports what an open-source receiver, run by the project as a test receiver on its own network, did with this sender on the date given, as seen from outside (the receiver's log and the sender's debug trace), not as read in its source. It is one implementation at one version, not Apple's; its name and version are kept with the project's clean-room records.
- A *Source:* of "hardware trace" reports what one licensed receiver did with this sender, as AudioSync's debug trace recorded it on the date given. It is one model at one firmware version: it settles what a sender must do to work with that model, not what every receiver does.
- *Target*, *member* and *control device*: a stereo pair or home theater group (P173, P174) is one *target* for the host; each speaker that plays its audio is a *member*; the Apple TV of a home theater group is its *control device*.
- Terms: *sender* is the crate; *receiver* or *speaker* is the AirPlay device; *session* is one RTSP connection plus its UDP flows to one receiver, from connect to teardown; *frame* is one stereo sample pair at 44 100 Hz; *packet* is one RTP audio packet; *host* is the AudioSync app that drives the crate; *sender clock* is defined in P89.
- Multi-byte fields are big-endian (network order) unless a clause says otherwise. Single bytes are written in hex, such as 0xD4. "RTP header without SSRC" means the first 8 bytes of an RFC 3550 header.
- Example addresses use 192.0.2.0/24 (RFC 5737). Numbers quoted from captures come from the cited sources.
- Clause IDs run from P1 without gaps in this version. A clause added later takes the next free number and sits in the section it belongs to; a dropped clause keeps its number, marked withdrawn.

## 1. Scope and role

- **P1** FairPlay and MFi authentication are out of scope. The sender never implements FairPlay (encryption types 3 and 5, `POST /fp-setup`), MFi-SAP (encryption type 4) or the checking of a receiver's MFi certificate, and never uses RSA keys taken from Apple products. A receiver that accepts nothing else is reported as unsupported (P139). *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* also out of scope: video, photos, screen mirroring, AAC, AAC-ELD and Opus encoding, multichannel audio, the TCP media transport, and remote control through the MediaRemote stream (a stream SETUP of type 130). Remote control through DACP is in scope (P186 to P195).
- **P2** AirPlay 1 and AirPlay 2 are both target paths. Every speaker that advertises `_raop._tcp` with an encryption mode the sender supports (P45) is driven over AirPlay 1, including AirPlay 2 devices. A speaker goes to the AirPlay 2 path (sections 4 and 6) only when that path is enabled, the device is AirPlay 2 capable (P16), and either it has no `_raop._tcp` service or its AirPlay 1 attempt ended in Unsupported or Refused (P139, P138); PasswordRequired, WrongPassword, Busy and the pairing errors never switch paths. The outcome that moved a speaker is remembered for the device as P218 says, beyond its session and beyond the host's hold on the speaker, so a pairing the AirPlay 2 path then asks for is that path's own (P219, P220), never the legacy one of P46. A speaker the sender holds HomeKit credentials for (P152) never takes the AirPlay 1 path, whatever its `_raop._tcp` record says and however its last AirPlay 1 attempt ended: with the AirPlay 2 path on it goes to AirPlay 2, and without its `_airplay._tcp` service in view it is Unreachable and retried (P142); with the path off it is Unsupported with "AirPlay 2 only", and a PIN pairing for it fails the same way instead of taking the legacy pairing. The reason: a `_raop._tcp` record is unauthenticated, so any host on the LAN can publish one under the speaker's device id, and nothing on the AirPlay 1 path would notice, while the AirPlay 2 pair-verify proves the receiver holds the paired identity (P66). The rule ends only when the owner forgets the pairing: a pair-verify turned down keeps the credentials (P154), and so keeps the rule. With the AirPlay 2 path off, only a speaker without `_raop._tcp` is Unsupported with the reason "AirPlay 2 only"; every AirPlay 1 outcome, Unsupported and Refused included, is reported as it is. The AirPlay 2 path drives several AirPlay 2 speakers and targets (P177) at once (P204), each receiver placed on AudioSync's own timeline (P96) with its own delay like any AirPlay 1 speaker, all on one PTP timeline the sender leads (P205); a group leader, a group another sender made and audio passed on between receivers stay out of scope (P171). *Source:* public protocol write-ups; AudioSync design. *Required:* the crate MUST be complete for AirPlay 1 and for several AirPlay 2 speakers and targets at once (P204). The AirPlay 2 path stays behind a switch, off by default, until the checks on a real receiver in G17 have passed. The switch is an "AirPlay 2 (Experimental)" setting in the host app, off by default, kept across launches and applied at the next launch, or an environment variable of the host. While it is off, P170 holds.
- **P169** At most as many AirPlay 2 sessions as P204 allows run at once, each target member (P177) counting as one: while that many run, a request to start another gives Unsupported with the reason "too many AirPlay 2 speakers", and the running sessions and every AirPlay 1 session go on untouched. When a running session ends without a retry of its own (the host removed its speaker, or it failed for good), the first speaker refused only for this reason that the host still has starts a new session, through the restart of P142; a session that waits for its own retry keeps its place, so a speaker added meanwhile is refused too, until that speaker plays again, fails for good or is removed, or until its retry starts on AirPlay 1 or fails before its AirPlay 2 session runs, which hands the place on as an end for good does. *Source:* AudioSync design (P2).
- **P170** While the AirPlay 2 switch (P2) is off, no AirPlay 2 message leaves the sender: no `GET /info` of P41, no HomeKit `/pair-pin-start`, pair-setup or pair-verify (P61 to P66, P149), no session or stream SETUP with a binary-plist body (P43, P44), no RECORD, SETPEERS, FLUSH, `POST /feedback` or TEARDOWN of an AirPlay 2 session, no event connection (P70), no framed RTSP (P67) and no PTP message (P158 to P167); the PTP ports are not bound. Every AirPlay 1 request goes out as it does with the switch on, legacy pairing included, and every AirPlay 1 outcome is reported as it is; only a device without `_raop._tcp` is Unsupported ("AirPlay 2 only", P2). *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P214** A host may set the sender to prefer AirPlay 2, off by default. The preference acts only while the AirPlay 2 path is on (P2); with the path off it changes nothing and P170 holds. While it is on, a device that is AirPlay 2 capable (P16) and has its `_airplay._tcp` service in view takes the AirPlay 2 path whatever its `_raop._tcp` record says: no AirPlay 1 attempt comes first, and its pairing is the PIN pairing of P61, not the legacy pairing. A device that is not AirPlay 2 capable, or whose `_airplay._tcp` service is not in view, is chosen for as P2 says. While other speakers and targets hold every AirPlay 2 place (P169, P204), a device that AirPlay 1 can serve and that only the preference would send to AirPlay 2 takes the AirPlay 1 path instead of waiting for that place, so several such speakers keep playing together as they do with the preference off; such a device that would wait for PTP in use on another network (P205) takes the AirPlay 1 path the same way. A target (P177) plays over AirPlay 2 for all its members when the preference sends any member there. With the preference off, P2 alone chooses the path. Each session tells the host its path before its Connecting state, and a start that waits its turn in the queue of P222 tells it again when its connect starts (P224); the host keeps the last path it was told, so it names the path a speaker actually uses. *Source:* AudioSync design (advisor decision 2026-10-26).
- **P171** Stereo pairs and home theater groups made in Apple's apps are supported this way: the sender recognizes them from discovery (P172 to P175), reports each to the host as one target (P176), and plays to a target by driving its members, each with a session of its own on AudioSync's timeline (P177 to P181). This is not Apple's multi-receiver grouping, which P2 keeps out of scope: the sender never acts as or names a group leader, never joins a group another sender made, never asks one receiver to pass audio on to another, and leaves the pairing of the members, and which channel each plays, to the receivers. *Source:* AudioSync design (P2).
- **P3** Each speaker gets its own session: its own RTSP connection, its own UDP sockets (P82), its own RTP sequence numbers, timestamps and SSRC. A failure in one session never stops another session or the audio input. *Source:* AudioSync design; RFC 3550 §8 (one SSRC per source).
- **P4** All speakers of a stream play frames of one shared timeline (P96), each shifted by its own delay (P99). *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P5** The sender names itself AudioSync (`User-Agent: AudioSync/<version>`, the SDP `o=` and `s=` fields). Every identifier it sends (Client-Instance, DACP-ID, Active-Remote, session IDs, AirPlay 2 device ID) is random; an identifier that must persist is a random value created once and stored by the host, never a hardware MAC address, serial number or user name. *Source:* AudioSync design. Published captures carry other senders' names in these fields; no source says receivers check them (section 11, G11).
- **P6** The call through which the host hands audio to the crate MUST NOT allocate, lock, block, log or touch the file system. It copies frames into a preallocated single-producer, single-consumer ring and returns. All network work runs on the crate's own threads. *Source:* AudioSync design (the app's rule for audio callbacks and the PCM writer path).

## 2. Discovery

- **P7** The sender browses for `_raop._tcp` and `_airplay._tcp` in the `local.` domain over multicast DNS, following PTR records to SRV, TXT and address records. *Source:* RFC 6762; RFC 6763 §4–§6; public protocol write-ups.
- **P8** A `_raop._tcp` instance name has the form `<id>@<name>`: `<id>` is 12 hex digits (the device's MAC-style identifier without separators) and `<name>` is the speaker name shown to users. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P9** A `_airplay._tcp` instance name is the speaker name; its TXT key `deviceid` carries the identifier as six colon-separated hex bytes. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P10** The sender normalizes both identifiers to 12 uppercase hex digits, and records with the same identifier are one device. The display name is the `_airplay._tcp` instance name when that service exists, otherwise the part of the RAOP name after `@`. The device keeps the SRV target and port of each service it has. *Source:* AudioSync design. *Required:* a device seen on several interfaces or address families is still one device, and a device with only one of the two services is still listed.
- **P11** Each TXT string is `key=value`. Keys compare case-insensitively; a key without `=` is a boolean true; lists are comma-separated; booleans accept `true`, `false`, `1` and `0` in any case; integers accept decimal or `0x` hex. Unknown keys are ignored, and a malformed value drops only that key. *Source:* RFC 6763 §6.4 (keys are case-insensitive); public protocol write-ups.
- **P12** RAOP TXT keys the sender reads:

  | Key | Meaning | Use |
  |---|---|---|
  | `txtvers` | TXT record version (1) | informational |
  | `ch`, `sr`, `ss` | channels, sample rate, sample size | must be 2, 44100, 16 when present (P16) |
  | `cn` | codecs: 0 PCM, 1 ALAC, 2 AAC, 3 AAC-ELD (one write-up adds 4 Opus) | codec choice (P74) |
  | `et` | encryption types: 0 none, 1 RSA, 3 FairPlay, 4 MFi-SAP, 5 FairPlay SAPv2.5 | pairing mode (P45) |
  | `md` | metadata types: 0 text, 1 artwork, 2 progress | metadata gate (P123) |
  | `pw` | password required | P16 |
  | `da` | digest authentication supported | informational |
  | `tp` | transports, `UDP` and/or `TCP` | must include UDP |
  | `sv`, `sm` | receiver wants the sender to apply volume or mute to the samples | P121 |
  | `sf`, `ft` | status flags and features, hex | P15, P14 |
  | `am`, `vs`, `vn`, `fv`, `ov` | model, server version, protocol version, firmware, OS version | informational, `am` for P46 |
  | `pk` | receiver public key, hex | AirPlay 2 (P66) |

  *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P13** AirPlay TXT keys the sender reads: `deviceid` (P9), `features` (P14), `flags` (P15), `model`, `manufacturer`, `srcvers` (AirPlay version), `protovers`, `pw` (present or true when a password is set), `pk` (32-byte public key in hex), `pi` and `psi` (pairing identities), `gid`, `igl`, `gcgl` (group state), `acl` (access control level), `rsf` (required sender features), `fv`, `osvers`. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P14** `features` holds one or two hex numbers separated by a comma: the first is bits 0–31, the optional second is bits 32–63. RAOP `ft` is the same bitmask; `/info` reports it as one decimal integer. Bits the sender uses:

  | Bit | Meaning |
  |---|---|
  | 9 | audio supported |
  | 11 | audio redundancy |
  | 14 | FairPlay authentication |
  | 15, 16, 17 | metadata: artwork, progress, text |
  | 18–21 | audio formats; one write-up says 19 and 20 must be set for an AirPlay 2 connection |
  | 23 | RSA authentication |
  | 26 | MFi authentication in one write-up; "has unified advertiser info" in the other |
  | 27 | legacy pairing supported |
  | 30 | "has unified advertiser info" in one write-up; "RAOP on this port" in the other |
  | 38, 43, 46, 48 | pairing and encryption (CoreUtils, system, HomeKit, transient) |
  | 40 | buffered audio |
  | 41 | PTP |
  | 50 | binary-plist now-playing metadata |
  | 51 | unified pair-setup with MFi |
  | 52 | extended SETPEERS message |

  *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* the two sources disagree on bits 26 and 30, so no decision that can fail a session rests on either bit alone; bit 26 only adds the harmless request of P60.
- **P15** `flags` (AirPlay) and `sf` (RAOP) hold status flags in hex. Bits the sender uses: 2 audio cable attached, 3 PIN required, 7 password required, 9 one-time pairing required, 17 a receiver session is active. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P16** For each device the sender computes, and recomputes on every TXT change: *needs password* (RAOP `pw` true, AirPlay `pw` present and not false, or flags bit 7); *needs pairing* (flags bit 3 or 9); *busy hint* (flags bit 17); *AirPlay 1 capable* (has `_raop._tcp`; `et` absent or containing 0, or an Apple TV with legacy pairing (P46); `cn` absent or containing 0 or 1; `tp` absent or containing UDP; `sr`, `ss`, `ch` absent or equal to 44100, 16, 2); *AirPlay 2 capable* (any of features bits 38, 43, 46, 48, with bits 40 and 41 noted). The verdict is reported to the host with the device. *Source:* AudioSync design. *Required:* a missing `cn` means ALAC only, the codec of the oldest receivers; a missing `et` means "try unencrypted".
- **P17** The sender connects to the addresses of the SRV target in this order: IPv4, IPv6 global, IPv6 link-local (with its interface scope). It never uses an IPv4 link-local address (the 169.254/16 range) while another address exists. The address a session connected to is used for all of its UDP traffic. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; RFC 6762. *Required:* the order.
- **P18** A goodbye record (TTL 0) or an expired record marks the service unavailable for new sessions but never ends a running session; the session's own connection decides that (P141). *Source:* RFC 6762 §10.1; AudioSync design.
- **P19** Receivers change `flags`, `gid`, `igl`, `gcgl` and related keys with their state, for example while another sender plays to them. The sender updates the verdict (P16) and reports it, without touching running sessions. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P172** Group keys of the AirPlay TXT record (P13), read as P11 says and reported raw with the device: `gid` (group UUID); `igl` (is group leader; one third-party speaker sends `isGroupLeader` instead, read the same); `gcgl` (the group contains a discoverable leader); `pgid` and `pgcgl` (the same two for a parent group); `gpn` (group public name: the name the user gave a stereo pair, or the name of the Apple TV the speakers play for); `tsid` (tight sync UUID, shared by the members of a stereo pair); `tsm` (meaning unknown, 0 and 1 seen); `psgid`, `psgtp` and `psgsz` (seen on an Apple TV and on the speakers that play its sound: a group UUID, a type, 1 in every report, and the number of devices, 3 for an Apple TV with a stereo pair); `pgm` (meaning unknown, 0 seen); and `hgid` (home group UUID). Status flags (P15) bits read with them: 11 relaying supported, 12 silent primary, 13 the device leads its tight sync group, 14 its tight sync partner is unreachable. A `gid` is either one UUID or three fields joined by `+`: the pair's `tsid`, a small integer and another UUID (`<tsid>+0+<uuid>`). The *group key* of a `gid` is the part before the first `+`, compared case-insensitively. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (key and flag names); [mit-sender] protocol documentation; public user reports (the `+` form, `tsm`, `psgid`, `psgtp`, `psgsz`, `pgm`, and `isGroupLeader` on one third-party speaker). *Required:* no decision rests on `tsm`, `pgm`, `psgtp`, `hgid` or bits 11 and 12, and the middle field of `gid` serves only the leader rule of P173.
- **P173** A *stereo pair* is the set of devices that carry the same non-empty `tsid` together with a `gpn`. Its identity is that `tsid`: in the reports it stayed the same while `gid` switched between one shared UUID and a per-member `<tsid>+<n>+<uuid>`, and while other senders played to the pair. Its name is its members' `gpn` (when they differ, that of the member with the lowest normalized identifier, P10). Its leader is the one member with flags bit 13 set; when none or more than one has it, the member whose `gid` middle field is 0; when that doesn't decide either, the member with the lowest normalized identifier. The leader matters only for what the sender reports (P176) and reads back (P180). A device with a `tsid` and a `gpn` whose partner isn't in view is a pair with a member missing (P181) when it sets bit 14, and a single speaker otherwise, unless that partner was in view with it earlier in this run (P216). In the reports both members are the same model and advertise their own `_airplay._tcp` and `_raop._tcp` services. *Source:* public user reports (two pairs whose members share `tsid` and `gpn`, in both the left member with bit 13 and `gid` middle field 0 and the right member with neither; `gid` changing form over time; a HomePod with a `tsid` of its own and bit 14 set); [apple-homepod-pair] ("Creating a HomePod stereo pair requires two HomePod speakers of the same model"); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (a fork of the media server keys its pairs by `tsid`); public user reports (`gid` changing form while `tsid` stays the same). *Required:* nothing here depends on the model, so a device of another model that matches is a pair too.
- **P174** A *home theater group* is an Apple TV (`model` starting `AppleTV`) whose sound goes to HomePods. In the reports the Apple TV advertises `igl` 1, a `gid` and the same UUID as `psgid`; each of its speakers advertises `igl` 0, its `gid`, `pgid` and `psgid` equal to the Apple TV's group key, `pgcgl` 1 and the Apple TV's name as `gpn`, and the speakers of a stereo pair keep their own `tsid` (P173). The group's members are those speakers and its name is their `gpn`; the Apple TV is its control device. The speakers carried flags bit 17 in every report, so for them the busy hint of P16 holds nothing back. *Source:* public user reports (two Apple TVs, each with a stereo pair as its default audio output); [apple-tv-audio] ("On HomePod, this routes all sound, including system and game audio, from Apple TV 4K to your HomePod speaker"). *Required:* `psgid` marks the group, since the groups of P175 carry none: speakers with `igl` 0 whose `psgid` equals their `pgid` form the group even while their Apple TV is out of view.
- **P175** Devices that share a group key with no `tsid` or `gpn` linking them are in a group a sender made (an Apple sender's AirPlay menu, or another sender's session): an open receiver advertises as `gid` the `groupUUID` of the session SETUP it plays (P43), and reports show such groups changing, and falling apart when another sender starts playing to a member. The sender reports the shared key as information only, never treats such devices as one target, and drives each as any speaker, which can take it out of the other sender's group. A speaker group kept in the Home app that is neither a stereo pair nor a home theater group left no key in the reports; its members are listed one by one. *Source:* observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (an open receiver's `gid` comes from its session's `groupUUID`); public user reports; AudioSync design.
- **P176** The crate reports each stereo pair and home theater group to the host as one target, with its kind, its identity (the pair's `tsid`, or the group key of the Apple TV of a home theater group), its name, its members with their device identifiers and which one leads, the control device of a home theater group, and whether every member is in view. The members stay in the device list, each with a reference to its target, so the host can show the target as one row. Stored settings of a target (delay, volume, chosen or not) are keyed by its identity, so they survive the `gid` changes of P173 and P19. Membership is recomputed on every TXT change (P16, P19) and never touches running sessions; a change applies when the host next starts the target. *Source:* AudioSync design.

## 3. RTSP session

- **P20** A session is a TCP connection to the `_raop._tcp` SRV port. Requests are RTSP/1.0 text: a request line, header lines, a blank line, and a body sized by `Content-Length`; lines end in CRLF. One request is in flight at a time per connection (no pipelining). *Source:* RFC 2326 §6–§7; public protocol write-ups. *Required:* the connect times out after 5 s, and a response that doesn't arrive within 10 s is a timeout (P134), except while the sender waits for the user's PIN (P51).
- **P21** Every request carries `CSeq`, starting at 0 on a new connection and going up by one per request. A response MUST carry the CSeq of its request; any other value is a protocol error (P140). *Source:* RFC 2326 §12.17; public protocol write-ups.
- **P22** Besides `CSeq`, every request carries `User-Agent: AudioSync/<version>`, `Client-Instance` and `DACP-ID` (the same 16 uppercase hex digits) and `Active-Remote` (a decimal 32-bit number). DACP-ID and Active-Remote announce the sender's command server (P186 to P195). *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* Client-Instance and DACP-ID keep one random value for the sender's run and Active-Remote is fresh for each session, as P187 says (P5).
- **P23** The request URI is `rtsp://<address>/<session-id>`: `<session-id>` is a random 32-bit number in decimal, made once per session, and `<address>` is the sender's own local address on the connection. ANNOUNCE, SETUP, RECORD, SET_PARAMETER, GET_PARAMETER, FLUSH and TEARDOWN use it; OPTIONS uses `*`. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. Note: AirPlay 2 captures show the receiver's address in the URI, so receivers don't seem to check it. The IPv6 form is unverified (G16).
- **P24** A response starts with `RTSP/1.0 <code> <reason>`; header names are case-insensitive; a missing `Content-Length` means no body. The sender records `Server` and `Audio-Jack-Status` (`connected` or `disconnected`, optionally with `type=analog` or `type=digital`) and reports them; a disconnected jack is a warning, not an error. *Source:* RFC 2326 §7; public protocol write-ups.
- **P146** The sender bounds what it reads of an RTSP message, on every RTSP connection it reads, the pairing connection (P47) included: the head (the start line and the header lines, up to the blank line that ends them) may be at most 64 KiB (65 536 bytes), and the body at most 1 MiB (1 048 576 bytes). More than 64 KiB received without the end of a head, a head longer than 64 KiB, a `Content-Length` over 1 MiB, and a `Content-Length` that isn't a non-negative decimal number are each a protocol error (P140) as soon as the head shows it, before any of the body is read. A message within the limits is read whole, a 1 MiB body included, and the messages and errors read from a connection are the same however its bytes are split across reads, down to one byte at a time. *Source:* AudioSync design (the largest body the sender reads in AirPlay 1 is a pairing answer of a few hundred bytes, P148). *Required:* the sender reads each head once: while the body arrives it only checks whether all of it is there, and a search for the end of a head that hasn't ended yet goes on from where the previous search stopped, so the work of reading a message grows with its size and not with the number of pieces it arrives in.
- **P147** Lines of an RTSP head end in CRLF, and a bare LF ends a line too; empty lines and stray CR or LF bytes before a start line are skipped. A line that starts with a space or a tab continues the value of the header line above it, joined to it with one space. Head bytes that aren't valid UTF-8 are read with each invalid sequence replaced by U+FFFD, which alone doesn't make a message malformed. A header value, once the whitespace around it is trimmed, that still holds a control character (any byte below 0x20 other than tab, 0x09: a carriage return inside a line, NUL and ESC included) makes its line malformed, and the message is a protocol error (P140); every continuation line of a value is checked the same way. A tab inside a value is kept. Header names and the status reason are not checked for control characters, since the sender never sends them back. *Source:* RFC 2326 §4 (UTF-8 text, CRLF line ends, and receivers should also take a bare CR or LF as a line end) and §4.2, RFC 2616 §2.2 and §4.2 (a header value continued on lines that start with a space or a tab), RFC 2616 §4.1 (empty lines before a start line). *Required:* a bare CR is not a line end, and one left inside a value after trimming makes its line malformed, since values such as the session ID (P25) or the digest nonce (P36) go back out in the sender's own requests, where a CR, an LF or a NUL could end the header or start another; a value holding one is refused rather than split or passed on.
- **P25** The `Session` value from the SETUP response (on the AirPlay 2 path, the session SETUP's) goes, without its `timeout` parameter, on every later request of the session (RECORD, SET_PARAMETER, GET_PARAMETER, FLUSH, TEARDOWN); when the answer has none, no request carries one. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; hardware trace (2026-10-25: a Samsung 6 Series UT5500 TV, firmware srcvers 377.25.06, answered its AirPlay 2 session with no `Session` header).
- **P26** AirPlay 1 request order: the legacy pair-verify first when the sender holds credentials for the speaker (P55), then OPTIONS, then `POST /auth-setup` when it applies (P60), then ANNOUNCE, SETUP, RECORD, and SET_PARAMETER with the initial volume (P119); audio follows. No audio, sync or retransmission packet is sent before RECORD has been answered with 200. Verification goes before OPTIONS because an Apple TV that requires it may answer OPTIONS with 403 until the connection is verified (P46). *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* the gating on RECORD.
- **P27** OPTIONS is `OPTIONS * RTSP/1.0`; the response's `Public` header lists the receiver's methods. The sender records the list; a list that lacks ANNOUNCE, SETUP or RECORD marks the receiver unsupported for AirPlay 1, and a missing `Public` header is accepted. The sender sends no `Apple-Challenge` header, which belongs to the RSA mode (P59). This check is what catches receivers whose TXT record looks like AirPlay 1: two open receivers advertise `et` with 0, `cn` with 1 and an Apple TV model, yet list no ANNOUNCE; one answers ANNOUNCE with 501, the other answers any method it doesn't know, ANNOUNCE included, with 200 and does nothing, so a sender that skipped the check would fail later and less clearly. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders.
- **P28** ANNOUNCE carries `Content-Type: application/sdp` and an SDP body with these lines, in this order: `v=0`; `o=AudioSync <session-id> 0 IN IP4 <sender address>` (`IP6` for IPv6); `s=AudioSync`; `c=IN IP4 <receiver address>`; `t=0 0`; `m=audio 0 RTP/AVP 96`; `a=rtpmap:96 <encoding>`; and for ALAC `a=fmtp:96 <ALAC configuration>`. No key attributes (`rsaaeskey`, `fpaeskey`, `aesiv`), since the stream is not encrypted. *Source:* RFC 4566 §5; public protocol write-ups.
- **P29** For ALAC the encoding is `AppleLossless` and the configuration is `352 0 16 40 10 14 2 255 0 0 44100`: frames per packet, compatible version, bit depth, three tuning values (pb, mb, kb), channel count, maximum run, maximum frame size in bytes (0 for unknown), average bit rate (0 for unknown), sample rate. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; the field order matches Apple's ALAC configuration record ([alac]).
- **P30** For PCM the encoding is `L16/44100/2` and there is no fmtp line. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* PCM is used only as P74 allows. No source shows this exact SDP line from a working sender, so it is checked on hardware before PCM can become a default (G10).
- **P31** SETUP carries `Transport: RTP/AVP/UDP;unicast;interleaved=0-1;mode=record;control_port=<c>;timing_port=<t>`, with the session's local control and timing ports (P82). The response carries `Transport` with `server_port` (audio), `control_port` and `timing_port`, and a `Session` header. `timing_port` may be 0. A response without `server_port` or `control_port` is a protocol error. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* both local sockets are open and answering before SETUP is sent.
- **P32** RECORD carries `Range: npt=0-` and `RTP-Info: seq=<s>;rtptime=<t>`, where s is a random 16-bit value and t a random 32-bit value: the sequence number and RTP timestamp of the first audio packet. The response may carry `Audio-Latency: <frames>` (P110). *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P33** SET_PARAMETER carries the volume (section 8), track information, artwork or progress (section 9), with a `Content-Type` and, for metadata, `RTP-Info: rtptime=<t>`. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P34** FLUSH carries `RTP-Info: seq=<s>;rtptime=<t>`, naming the first packet that will be sent after the flush; the receiver drops audio queued before it. The sender ignores an `RTP-Info` header in the response. After a flush, numbering continues from s and t, the next sync packet carries the first-packet flag (P93) and the next audio packet the marker (P78). *Source:* public protocol write-ups (in a published capture, seq and rtptime in FLUSH have advanced by exactly 352 timestamp units per packet since RECORD: derived); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (two open receivers empty their buffer and wait for exactly the `seq` that FLUSH names as the next packet, reading only `seq`). Note: one write-up calls the values the last sequence number and timestamp; the derived arithmetic and both open receivers fit the next-packet reading, which the sender uses.
- **P35** TEARDOWN carries `Session` and no body in AirPlay 1. After the response, or after 2 s without one, the sender closes the TCP connection and stops all UDP traffic to that receiver. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* the 2 s limit.
- **P36** A `401` response with `WWW-Authenticate: Digest realm="<r>", nonce="<n>"` makes the sender send the same request again (with the next CSeq) adding `Authorization: Digest username="iTunes", realm="<r>", nonce="<n>", uri="<request URI>", response="<h>"`. This holds for the pairing and verification requests too (`/pair-pin-start`, `/pair-setup-pin`, `/pair-verify`), also when no earlier request on the connection was challenged: the resend carries the same body, and the 401 is never a pairing or verification answer (P58). *Source:* public protocol write-ups; RFC 2617 §1.2 (a 401 challenges the client to authorize); AudioSync design (pairing and verification requests, P58); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* realm and nonce are copied from the challenge; the user name is the fixed value that section documents for the audio service.
- **P37** Without `qop`, h = MD5(HA1 ":" nonce ":" HA2), with HA1 = MD5(username ":" realm ":" password) and HA2 = MD5(method ":" uri), each MD5 written as 32 lowercase hex digits; `uri` is the request URI exactly as on the request line (`*` for OPTIONS). If the challenge offers `qop=auth`, the sender follows RFC 2617 with `qop`, `nc` and `cnonce`. *Source:* RFC 2617 §3.2.2; public protocol write-ups.
- **P38** After a challenge, every later request on the same connection carries `Authorization`, recomputed for its own method and URI with the same nonce, until a new 401 brings a new nonce. A 401 whose challenge says `stale=true` means only the nonce expired: the sender retries once with the new nonce and does not report a wrong password. Any other 401 to a request that already carried `Authorization` with the current nonce means the password is wrong (P135). *Source:* RFC 2617 §3.2.1 (`stale`); AudioSync design.
- **P39** The host supplies the password per speaker. When a challenge arrives and the host gave none, the sender stops and reports PasswordRequired (P135). The password never appears in logs or diagnostics. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P40** In AirPlay 1 the receiver sends no requests on the RTSP connection; anything that is not the response to the outstanding request is a protocol error. *Source:* AudioSync design (no source shows receiver-initiated RTSP in AirPlay 1).
- **P41** On the AirPlay 2 path the sender first sends `GET /info RTSP/1.0` with `X-Apple-ProtocolVersion: 1`, `Content-Type: application/x-apple-binary-plist` and the body {`qualifier`: [`txtAirPlay`]}; the answer is a binary plist with the receiver's features, status flags, name, model, protocol and source versions, `pi`, `pk` and similar keys. A second `GET /info` without a body, after the session SETUP, returns further values such as `initialVolume`. AirPlay 2 request bodies are binary plists, and the published AirPlay 2 requests carry no `Session` header. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* the answer to the first request may be either the dictionary of values or a dictionary whose only useful entry is `txtAirPlay` (or `txtRAOP`), a data value holding the service's TXT record in DNS wire form (each string a length byte followed by `key=value`), which the sender parses as P11 says. In every `/info` answer, a value of an unexpected type is ignored as if it were absent.
- **P42** AirPlay 2 request order, as published from an Apple sender: `GET /info`; pairing (P63 to P66), with the encrypted channel from then on (P67); session SETUP (P43); `GET /info`; RECORD; SETPEERS (P106); SET_PARAMETER with the volume; stream SETUP (P44); FLUSH with `RTP-Info` naming the first packet; audio; and `POST /feedback` every 2 s throughout (P132). A stream ends with TEARDOWN carrying {`streams`: [{`type`: 96, `streamID`: <id>}]}; the session ends with TEARDOWN carrying an empty dictionary. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* this order on the AirPlay 2 path, with pairing where P153 places it, as two open senders do; SETPEERS only under PTP timing (P165); the volume SET_PARAMETER moves from its published place to after the FLUSH answer, as P183 says.
- **P43** The session SETUP body holds the sender's details and its timing choice: `deviceID`, `macAddress`, `sessionUUID`, `name`, `model`, `sourceVersion`, `timingProtocol` with its peer information (P104), the group keys `groupUUID`, `groupContainsGroupLeader` and `isMultiSelectAirPlay`, and in the published capture also `et`, `ekey` and `eiv` (with `et` 0). The answer holds `eventPort` (P70), `timingPort` and the receiver's `timingPeerInfo`. With "NTP" timing the body also carries `timingPort`, the sender's own timing port (P82): receivers send their timing requests there, and one open receiver logs an error and gets no clock offset without it. What `ekey` and `eiv` do with `et` 0 is not documented. In both open receivers, which come from the shairplay line, a session SETUP without `ekey` (72 bytes) and `eiv` (16 bytes) sets up no audio at all: there `ekey` is an audio key wrapped by FairPlay after `POST /fp-setup` (its first four bytes spell FPLY), and audio arrives AES-128-CBC encrypted under it. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* `name` and `model` are "AudioSync", and `deviceID` and `macAddress` both carry the random persistent identifier (P5); OS fields are left out unless a receiver turns out to need them (G11). The sender sends `et` 0 and leaves `ekey` and `eiv` out: with `et` 0 their role isn't documented, it has no key of its own to put there, and a FairPlay-wrapped key is out of scope (P1); a third open receiver, which implements HomeKit pairing, reads only `shk` (P156) for its audio key, and two open senders send no `ekey` or `eiv`; whether Apple's and licensed receivers want them anyway is checked on hardware (G17). When, after such a session SETUP, a receiver answers the stream SETUP by closing the connection or without a `dataPort`, the speaker is Unsupported (reason encryption), not Lost, and P142 doesn't retry it.
- **P44** The stream SETUP body is {`streams`: [one dictionary]} with `type` 96 (realtime audio; 103 is buffered audio, 130 remote control), `ct` (compression: 1 LPCM, 2 ALAC, 4 AAC, 8 AAC-ELD, 32 Opus), `audioFormat` (one bit of the format mask, such as 0x40000 for ALAC at 44 100 Hz, 16-bit stereo, or 0x800 for PCM at 44 100 Hz, 16-bit stereo), `spf` (frames per packet), `shk` (P156; a binary-plist data value of 32 bytes), the sender's `controlPort`, `latencyMin` and `latencyMax` in frames (11 025 and 88 200 in the capture), `isMedia` true, `audioMode` "default", `sr` 44100, `supportsDynamicStreamID` false and `streamConnectionID` (the session ID of P23). The answer gives the receiver's `dataPort` and `controlPort`: audio goes to `dataPort` over UDP, control packets to `controlPort`. A `controlPort` of 0 tells at least one receiver never to ask for retransmissions, so the sender always sends its real control port. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* `type` 96, `ct` 2 with `audioFormat` 0x40000, `spf` 352, `sr` 44100 (one open receiver reads `sr` and `spf` for type 96 and plays realtime audio as ALAC only), and the latencies as captured.
- **P177** To play a target, the sender opens a session to every member in view, each as P3 says and all at once (their connects spaced as P222 says), and none to the control device of a home theater group. All members use one path: AirPlay 1 when every member advertises `_raop._tcp` with a mode the sender supports (P2), otherwise AirPlay 2 for all; when a member's AirPlay 1 attempt ends in Unsupported or Refused, the whole target moves to AirPlay 2 for the rest of that start. With the AirPlay 2 switch off (P2, P170) nothing moves: every member plays over AirPlay 1 or is reported as P2 says, and P181 applies to the ones that can't play. For P169 a target counts as one AirPlay 2 speaker: its members' sessions start together, take one place, and hand it on when the last of them ends for good. While the host drives a home theater group, a request to drive its control device by itself gives Unsupported with the reason "plays through its speakers", and while the control device plays by itself, a request for its group gives the group the same. *Source:* public user reports (a sender that plays to both members of a pair, standalone or behind an Apple TV, gets stereo; one HomePod release dropped one member's session when the other started, which a later release fixed); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (a fork of the media server drives each member, keeps all members on one protocol, and sends a home theater group's audio to its speakers only, never to the Apple TV); [apple-tv-audio]. *Required:* whether one session to the leader alone plays on both members, and what a session to the Apple TV plays, are checked on hardware (G18); until then every member gets its own session.

## 4. Pairing and encryption

- **P45** Modes: (a) none: `et` contains 0 and neither password nor pairing is needed; (b) password: digest authentication (P36–P39); (c) Apple TV legacy pairing: a one-time PIN pairing plus a verification on every connection, with no encryption of later traffic; (d) RSA (`et` 1 only): unsupported (P59); (e) AirPlay 2 HomeKit-style pairing with an encrypted control channel (P61–P70, P149–P154). Password and legacy pairing can apply together. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P46** Legacy pairing applies when the receiver is an Apple TV (`am` or `model` starting with `AppleTV`) and its flags set bit 3 or bit 9, or when an Apple TV without stored credentials refuses ANNOUNCE with a 4xx status other than 401, 409 and 453, or when any receiver answers with 403 the OPTIONS that sets up a connection (P26, also when it is resent after a 401); these refusals are then reported as PairingRequired rather than Refused (P138). A 403 to the keep-alive OPTIONS of a running session is not a pairing answer (P131). An Apple TV that requires verification answers OPTIONS with 403 until the connection has been verified; an open sender reads that 403 as "verification required" from any receiver and starts PIN pairing. With stored credentials the sender verifies before OPTIONS (P26, P55); without them it reports PairingRequired and waits for the host to start pairing. Only a connection reports PairingRequired. Before any connection the sender MAY pass the host a pairing hint from discovery (P16's *needs pairing* verdict), as it passes the busy hint of P137, so the host can show the speaker as likely to need a code; the hint is not PairingRequired, and a pairing the host starts from the hint alone first runs the OPTIONS of the decision rule below, so it ends in Unsupported, with no code shown on the TV, when the receiver can't take AirPlay 1 audio, unless P220 takes the pairing on to the AirPlay 2 path. A model string is no proof of an Apple TV: both open receivers present Apple TV models (and set flags bit 3 or `pw` when their PIN option is on), answer OPTIONS with 200 and take no AirPlay 1 audio (P27). *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* the decision rule. Without credentials, a pairing candidate is asked OPTIONS first: a 403 gives PairingRequired; a 200 whose `Public` lacks ANNOUNCE gives Unsupported (P27), so the user is never asked for a PIN for a receiver that can't take AirPlay 1 audio; a 200 listing ANNOUNCE (or without `Public`) gives PairingRequired when the flags call for pairing and otherwise goes on to ANNOUNCE. A 401 to that OPTIONS, in a connection attempt or in a pairing the host started, is a password challenge (P36), not a pairing answer: with the speaker's password from the host the sender answers it, and every later request on the connection, pairing requests included, carries `Authorization` (P38); without one it reports PasswordRequired (P39) and stops before `/pair-pin-start`, so the TV never shows a code for a pairing that can't go on. Password and legacy pairing can apply together (P45). A 403 to OPTIONS can also mean access restricted by the Home app (G8); the pairing attempt then fails at `/pair-pin-start`, which gives PairingFailed (P58).
- **P47** Pairing runs over one TCP connection to the RAOP port (the start request may use its own); requests are `POST <path> RTSP/1.0`; the connection stays open between steps; bodies are binary property lists unless a clause says otherwise. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P48** `POST /pair-pin-start` with no body; on 200 the Apple TV shows a 4-digit PIN. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P49** `POST /pair-setup-pin` with `Content-Type: application/x-apple-binary-plist` and the dictionary {`method`: "pin", `user`: client ID}; the response dictionary holds `pk` (the 256-byte SRP value B) and `salt` (16 bytes). *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* the client ID is 16 uppercase hex digits of a random 64-bit value, made at pairing and stored with the credentials (P54).
- **P50** SRP-6a over the RFC 5054 2048-bit group (generator 2) with SHA-1, where I is the client ID, P is the PIN as typed (ASCII digits) and s is the salt; k, x and u are computed as RFC 5054 defines them. The private value a is a fresh random 32-byte secret that also seeds the client's Ed25519 key (a_pub is the Ed25519 public key of a). Two departures from common SRP libraries: the proof is M1 = H(H(N) XOR H(g) | H(I) | s | A | B | K), with g not padded, and the session key is K = H(S | 00 00 00 00) | H(S | 00 00 00 01), 40 bytes. In M1, M2 and K, A, B and S are written in their shortest big-endian form and s as the 16 bytes received; inside k and u, RFC 5054 pads values to the 256-byte length of N. *Source:* a public Apple TV pairing write-up gives a 2048-bit safe prime, SHA-1, the shared secret and the two departures; that the prime and generator are those of the RFC 5054 2048-bit group and that k, x and u follow RFC 5054 is derived: the published test vector's A, K and M1 come out of exactly this computation. RFC 5054 §2.5–§2.6 and appendix A; RFC 2945 §3 (shape of M1); RFC 8032 §5.1.5; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (two independent receiver implementations of this exchange: the user name is the client ID, the password the PIN, the salt 16 bytes; both hash s, A, B and S in their shortest big-endian form, without leading zero bytes, in M1, M2 and K, while u and k use the padded forms of RFC 5054). *Required:* the vector's A, B and S start with nonzero bytes, so it doesn't settle padding; the forms above follow both receivers. The sender also draws a again whenever A or S would start with a zero byte (together about once in 128 pairings), so only a B with a leading zero byte, the receiver's own choice, depends on that reading.
- **P51** Between the first and second `/pair-setup-pin` request the sender waits for the host to supply the PIN, with no timeout of its own. If the Apple TV closes the connection meanwhile, pairing ends with PairingCancelled (P136). *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P52** `POST /pair-setup-pin` with {`pk`: A (256 bytes), `proof`: M1 (20 bytes)}; the response holds `proof` (M2). A wrong PIN is answered with status 470. The sender SHOULD check M2 as RFC 2945 defines it, M2 = H(A | M1 | K) with A in its shortest form and the K above; the write-up treats the check as optional because the Apple TV rejects a bad exchange itself. Receivers differ after a 470: one keeps its SRP state so a corrected proof may follow, another discards it. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; RFC 2945 §3; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* after a 470 the sender never resends a proof; pairing ends (P58) and the host starts again from `/pair-pin-start`.
- **P53** `POST /pair-setup-pin` with {`epk`: 32-byte ciphertext, `authTag`: 16-byte tag}: a_pub sealed with AES-128-GCM and no additional data, where the key is the first 16 bytes of SHA-512("Pair-Setup-AES-Key" | K) and the IV is the first 16 bytes of SHA-512("Pair-Setup-AES-IV" | K) with 1 added to its last byte (modulo 256). A 200 response completes pairing. The IV is 16 bytes, so the GCM implementation MUST accept a 16-byte nonce. Two open receivers answer with the binary plist {`epk`: 32 bytes, `authTag`: 16 bytes}: the receiver's own Ed25519 public key sealed with the same key and an IV whose last byte is one higher than in the sender's IV (2 more than the hash's, modulo 256, with no carry into the other bytes). The sender MAY open that answer and keep the receiver's key with the credentials (P54); an answer that is missing, too large to read (P148) or fails to open never fails a pairing the 200 completed, since what an Apple TV sends here is unverified. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (one gives a larger tag size in its table than in its test vector, which has a 16-byte tag; the test vector wins); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders.
- **P148** The sender parses an answer to `/pair-setup-pin` (P49, P52, P53) only when its body is at most 4 KiB (4 096 bytes); a larger body is refused unread. The largest real answer, `pk` and `salt` (P49), is about 340 bytes as a binary property list and under 1 KiB as XML. A binary property list can refer to one object from many places, so a small body can stand for a very large value: while parsing, the sender reads at most 32 KiB (32 768 bytes) in total, counting every byte it reads again for an object referred to more than once, and an answer that needs more is refused like one over 4 KiB. A refused answer to the first or second step (P49, P52) is a protocol error (P140) and ends the pairing. A refused answer to the last step (P53) is ignored like one that fails to open: the 200 still completes the pairing, without the receiver's key. The 4 KiB cap also bounds how deeply an answer's values can nest, so reading or discarding one never exhausts the stack of the thread that reads it: an answer nested tens of thousands of levels deep, as a binary property list or as XML, ends the pairing with a protocol error, never a crash (P133). *Source:* public protocol write-ups (what the answers hold); AudioSync design. *Required:* the deepest binary answer within the cap is read and discarded on at most a quarter of a 2 MiB thread stack, the default for a spawned thread; the deepest answer of all within the cap, in the one-byte-per-level text form a parser may fall back to after XML (about 2,000 levels), on the 2 MiB stack itself.
- **P54** The credentials are the client ID and the 32-byte secret a, plus the receiver's 32-byte Ed25519 public key when P53 yielded one. The crate hands them to the host, which stores them securely per speaker; the crate never writes them to disk, and losing them means pairing again. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; AudioSync design.
- **P55** As the first request on every new RTSP connection to a paired Apple TV, before OPTIONS (P26), so again after each TEARDOWN: the sender makes a fresh X25519 key pair (v_priv, v_pub) and sends `POST /pair-verify` with `Content-Type: application/octet-stream` and a 68-byte body, 01 00 00 00 | v_pub | a_pub (not a plist). The 96-byte response body is atv_pub (32 bytes) | atv_data (64 bytes). Two open receivers take this first step only from a client key they paired with a PIN, or after an unauthenticated legacy `/pair-setup` on the same connection (a 32-byte Ed25519 public key each way, `application/octet-stream`, advertised by features bit 27); otherwise they answer 200 with an empty body. The sender doesn't use that legacy exchange for AirPlay 1. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; RFC 7748 §5; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* a 200 whose body isn't exactly 96 bytes is a failed verification (P58).
- **P56** The sender computes shared = X25519(v_priv, atv_pub) and sig = the Ed25519 signature by a over v_pub | atv_pub, then runs AES-128-CTR with key = first 16 bytes of SHA-512("Pair-Verify-AES-Key" | shared) and IV = first 16 bytes of SHA-512("Pair-Verify-AES-IV" | shared): it first encrypts atv_data and throws that output away, then encrypts sig with the continuing keystream. It sends `POST /pair-verify` with the 68-byte body 00 00 00 00 | encrypted sig; 200 means verified. Traffic after it stays unencrypted. atv_data is the receiver's Ed25519 signature over atv_pub | v_pub (its own key first), encrypted with the first 64 bytes of that same keystream, which is why the keystream runs over atv_data first; the receiver checks sig against the a_pub of the first step. The write-up describes no check of atv_data. When the stored credentials hold the receiver's Ed25519 key from pairing (P53), the sender checks atv_data before it signs, and the verification fails closed, without waiting for an Apple TV to confirm the check on hardware: the key P53 stores is the key the receiver signs pair-verify with, so a mismatch is never expected from the paired receiver, and failing closed costs only a speaker that stays off with its credentials kept. A signature that doesn't check out means the device at that address can't prove it is the receiver that was paired: the sender sends no second request and no signature of its own, closes the connection and reports ReceiverUnverified (P136). The stored credentials stay, so a device that can't sign can't wipe a pairing either. Credentials without the receiver's key skip the check. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; RFC 8032 §5.1.6; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders; AudioSync design.
- **P57** The write-up publishes a test vector for one pairing and one verification (its section 10.3); P145 holds its values. The crate's tests MUST reproduce every value that can be computed from the published inputs: for pairing, from the client ID, PIN, salt, B and a: A, K, M1, a_pub, the AES key and IV, epk and the tag; for verification, with a as v_priv: v_pub, the shared secret, the AES key and IV, the signature over v_pub | atv_pub and its encrypted form. The vector doesn't name v_priv, but its v_pub and shared secret are exactly what a gives as an X25519 private key, so the whole verification is reproducible. - **P58** A 470 response at any pairing step, or any other non-200 answer to a pairing request (`/pair-pin-start` or `/pair-setup-pin`) except a Busy answer of P137 and a 401, ends pairing with PairingFailed. A non-200 answer to a verification with stored credentials, again except a Busy answer of P137 and a 401, means they were rejected: the sender reports PairingRequired; the AirPlay 2 pair-verify narrows this to 470 and other 4xx answers (P154). A 401 to a pairing or verification request is a password challenge (P36), whether or not OPTIONS or another earlier request on the connection was challenged: with the speaker's password from the host the sender sends the same request again with `Authorization` and goes on, and every later request carries it (P38); without one it reports PasswordRequired (P39); a 401 without `stale=true` to a request that already carried `Authorization` with the current nonce is WrongPassword (P135). Sending the proof again after a 401 is not the resend P52 rules out, since the receiver hasn't judged it, and a 401 says nothing about the PIN or the stored credentials, which the host keeps. Two open receivers answer a wrong signature in the second verification step with 200 and then close the connection. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (status 470); AudioSync design; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (two open senders: a non-200 to either verification step means the stored key was rejected, and the second step's 200 completes verification). On the 401: RFC 2617 §1.2; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (two open receivers challenge only SETUP, never a pairing or verification request, and both turn a failed pairing down with 470; neither open sender answers a 401 to a pairing or verification request). *Required:* the 401 rule is AudioSync design, as no source shows a receiver that challenges a pairing or verification request: a 401 asks for credentials while 470 is the pairing verdict, answering it costs at most one resend (P38) and gives the receiver nothing a challenge to OPTIONS wouldn't, and a receiver that challenges only its pairing requests can then be paired with its password instead of failing every time. When the connection closes after the second verification step (P56) answered 200 and before the answer to the next request arrives, the sender opens one new connection at once and verifies again. If that verification ends the same way or is refused, it reports PairingRequired; if it succeeds, the session goes on; if the new connection can't be made, the speaker is Unreachable or Lost as usual (P134, P141), so a network drop in that window never forces a new pairing. The first step's 200 is no such sign: those receivers turn down a key they don't know with an empty 200 to the first step, which P55 already counts as a failed verification, so a close after the first step's 200 and before the second step is answered is a connection loss as usual (P134, P141), never PairingRequired.
- **P59** The RSA mode (`et` 1, early AirPort Express): the sender would put a random 16-byte `Apple-Challenge` on a request, wrap a random AES-128 key with RSA-OAEP under the receiver's RSA public key into SDP `a=rsaaeskey`, send the IV in `a=aesiv` (both base64 without padding), encrypt each packet's audio with AES-128-CBC over whole 16-byte blocks only (a shorter tail stays clear), and check the challenge signed back in `Apple-Response`. What's missing: no allowed source publishes the receiver's RSA public key, so this mode can't be built here. A receiver whose `et` lacks 0 and that isn't an Apple TV with legacy pairing is reported Unsupported. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P60** A receiver whose RAOP `et` contains 4, or whose features set bit 26 or 51, may refuse further requests until the sender has sent `POST /auth-setup` with `Content-Type: application/octet-stream` and a 33-byte body: 0x01 (unencrypted) followed by a fresh X25519 public key. The response (the receiver's key, an MFi certificate and a signature) is the receiver proving itself; the sender reads it and discards it without checking (P1). *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* sent once per connection, right after OPTIONS, for such receivers; a failed `/auth-setup` is not fatal and the session goes on.
- **P61** On the AirPlay 2 path the sender picks the pairing from `statusFlags` in the first `/info` answer (P41), an integer with the bits of P15, or from the TXT `flags` (P15) when the answer has no such integer, in this order: with flags bit 3 (PIN required) set and HomeKit credentials stored for the device (P152) that no answer turned down (P154), one pair-verify with them (P66), since that bit arrives in plain text before anything proves the receiver: a receiver that still holds them plays, and one that asks for a code every time refuses it (P182); else, with bit 3 set, a PIN pairing, and stored credentials stay (P154); else, with HomeKit credentials stored for the device (P152) that an answer turned down earlier in the sender's life (P154) and with bit 7 (password required) set, the password pairing below, whose success replaces them; else, with HomeKit credentials stored for the device, a pair-verify (P66), also after such a turn-down; else, with flags bit 9 (one-time pairing required) set, a PIN pairing (P65) after `POST /pair-pin-start`, which makes the receiver show a code; else, with bit 7 (password required) set, the same pairing with the speaker's password from the host in place of the PIN and without `/pair-pin-start`; otherwise a transient pairing (P64). A PIN pairing starts only when the host asks for it after a PairingRequired report, as the legacy one does (P46); with no password from the host, bit 7 gives PasswordRequired (P135). A 470 answer to a transient pair-setup means the receiver wants a PIN pairing and gives PairingRequired. Features bits 38, 43, 46 and 48 only mark the device AirPlay 2 capable (P16); one open sender offers a transient pairing whenever bit 38 or 43 is set, another chooses from the status flags as above. Who may play to a speaker is set in Apple's Home app: everyone, anyone on the same network, or only people sharing the home, optionally with a password; the last needs a home identity this sender doesn't have (G4). *Source:* public protocol write-ups; [apple-home] (the access settings); [mit-sender]; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders; AudioSync design (one pair-verify before a PIN under bit 3). *Required:* the order above; stored credentials never lead to a transient pairing.
- **P62** AirPlay 2 pairing data is TLV8: each item is a type byte, a length byte and the value. A value longer than 255 bytes is split into consecutive items of the same type, all but the last 255 bytes long, and received items are joined the same way. Type numbers seen: 0 method, 1 identifier, 2 salt, 3 public key, 4 proof, 5 encrypted data, 6 state, 10 signature; type 27 appears in a pair-setup reply with no known meaning. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (derived from a published example).
- **P63** Pair-setup is a series of `POST /pair-setup` requests (P149), each answered with a TLV8 body. M1 (sender) {method 0, state 1}, plus {flags 0x10} for a transient pairing; M2 (receiver) {state 2, salt (16 bytes), public key B (384 bytes)}; M3 (sender) {state 3, public key A (384 bytes), proof (64 bytes)}; M4 (receiver) {state 4, proof (64 bytes)}. P151 gives the SRP computation. Some receivers serve `/pair-setup` only in the legacy form of P55 (a raw 32-byte key) and answer a TLV8 M1 with 200 and an empty body. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (derived from a published example); [mit-sender]; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* an M1 answer with neither a TLV8 state item of value 2 nor an error item (P150) means the receiver has no HomeKit pairing; the speaker is Unsupported (reason encryption) on the AirPlay 2 path. The sender checks the M4 proof (P151) and treats a wrong one as an authentication error (P150).
- **P64** A transient pairing ends after M4: the SRP session key K (64 bytes, P151) is the session's shared secret, no M5, M6 or pair-verify follows, and nothing is stored. The code for this mode is 3939. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; [mit-sender]; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders.
- **P65** A PIN or password pairing continues after M4 with M5 and M6. The sender holds an Ed25519 key pair (RFC 8032) and an identifier (P152). From K it derives with HKDF-SHA-512 (RFC 5869), 32 bytes each, a signing prefix (salt "Pair-Setup-Controller-Sign-Salt", info "Pair-Setup-Controller-Sign-Info") and an encryption key (salt "Pair-Setup-Encrypt-Salt", info "Pair-Setup-Encrypt-Info"). It signs prefix | identifier | Ed25519 public key with its Ed25519 key and sends M5 {state 5, encrypted data}: the TLV8 {identifier, public key (32 bytes), signature (64 bytes)} sealed with ChaCha20-Poly1305 (RFC 8439) under the encryption key, with no additional data, the nonce being four zero bytes followed by the ASCII "PS-Msg05", and the 16-byte tag after the ciphertext. M6 (receiver) is {state 6, encrypted data}; opened with the same key and the nonce four zero bytes then "PS-Msg06", it holds the receiver's identifier, its 32-byte Ed25519 public key and a 64-byte signature over a prefix derived the same way with salt "Pair-Setup-Accessory-Sign-Salt" and info "Pair-Setup-Accessory-Sign-Info", then the receiver's identifier, then its public key. A pair-verify on the same connection follows (P153). *Source:* [mit-sender]; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders; RFC 5869; RFC 8032; RFC 8439. *Required:* the sender checks the M6 tag and signature (one open sender skips the signature check) and ends the pairing with PairingFailed when either fails; the receiver's identifier and public key from M6 become part of the credentials (P152).
- **P66** Pair-verify is a series of `POST /pair-verify` requests (P149) with TLV8 bodies. The sender makes a fresh X25519 key pair (RFC 7748) for each verification and sends M1 {state 1, public key (32 bytes)}. M2 (receiver) is {state 2, public key (32 bytes), encrypted data}: the sender computes the X25519 shared secret, derives a 32-byte key from it with HKDF-SHA-512 (salt "Pair-Verify-Encrypt-Salt", info "Pair-Verify-Encrypt-Info"), and opens the encrypted data with ChaCha20-Poly1305 and the nonce four zero bytes then "PV-Msg02"; inside are the receiver's identifier and an Ed25519 signature, which the sender checks with the stored receiver public key over receiver X25519 key | receiver identifier | sender X25519 key. M3 (sender) is {state 3, encrypted data}: the TLV8 {identifier, signature} with the sender's identifier and its Ed25519 signature over sender X25519 key | sender identifier | receiver X25519 key, sealed under the same key with the nonce four zero bytes then "PV-Msg03". M4 (receiver) {state 4} completes it, and the X25519 shared secret becomes the session's shared secret. The 120-byte encrypted items of the published capture fit a 36-character identifier, a signature and the tag. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (derived from a published example); [mit-sender]; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders; RFC 7748; RFC 8032. *Required:* an M2 whose tag or signature fails, or whose identifier differs from the stored one, ends the verification as rejected credentials (P154), and no M3 is sent.
- **P149** Pairing requests on the AirPlay 2 path go on the session's RTSP connection as `POST /pair-pin-start RTSP/1.0`, `POST /pair-setup RTSP/1.0` and `POST /pair-verify RTSP/1.0`, with the headers of P21 and P22, `Content-Type: application/octet-stream`, and `X-Apple-HKP` set to 4 on a transient pair-setup and to 3 on every other pair-setup, on `/pair-pin-start` and on pair-verify. `/pair-pin-start` carries no body. A 200 answer carries the TLV8 reply; 401, 409 and 470 answers are handled as P58 says. *Source:* [mit-sender]; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. Note: one open receiver dispatches on the path alone and reads neither header, so only a real receiver can show whether they matter.
- **P150** TLV8 types beyond P62: 7 error, 8 back-off (seconds to wait), 17 name, 19 flags (one byte; 0x10 marks a transient pairing). Method values: 0 pair-setup, 1 pair-setup with authentication, 2 pair-verify, 3 add, 4 remove and 5 list pairings; the sender sends 0 only. An answer with an error item refuses that step, whatever its state item says. In a pair-setup, error 2 (authentication: a wrong PIN or password, or a wrong proof) in a PIN pairing asks the host for the PIN again: the connection stays open, the receiver keeps showing its code, and the next PIN starts again from M1 on the same connection with no new `/pair-pin-start`, and never before the user typed it. When that PIN fails with error 2 as well, the sender sends `/pair-pin-start` again on the same connection and asks the host for the PIN again; the PIN after that starts from M1 as before. A receiver may keep showing the same code after that `/pair-pin-start`, so the sender and the host never assume that a new code appears. Error 3 (back-off) in a PIN pairing also asks the host for the PIN again on the same connection, and the next M1 goes out no sooner than the wait of item 8 (at most 600 s; none without the item). Error 2 gives WrongPassword in a password pairing and PairingRequired in a transient pairing; outside a PIN pairing 3 (back-off, with the wait from item 8), and in any pairing 4 (too many paired senders), 5 (too many attempts) and 6 (unavailable) give PairingFailed with that reason; 7 (busy) gives Busy (P137); 1 (unknown) and any other value give PairingFailed. A pair-verify reads the same table, except that error 2 there means rejected credentials, and its PairingFailed keeps the credentials and is retried (P154). Each time a PIN pairing asks the host for the PIN again, the sender first tells the host, for its debug log, which request the receiver answered and how (error 2, error 3 with its wait, or an M4 proof that didn't check) and whether `/pair-pin-start` goes out again, never the PIN. *Source:* [mit-sender] (the type, method and error values); AudioSync design (the mapping); hardware trace, Samsung UT5500, 24 Oct 2026 (the same code after a wrong PIN).
- **P151** The HomeKit SRP is SRP-6a (RFC 2945, RFC 5054) over the RFC 5054 3072-bit group with generator 5 and SHA-512 as H. The user name I is "Pair-Setup"; the password P is the PIN as typed (ASCII digits), "3939" for a transient pairing, or the speaker password from the host in UTF-8; s is the salt from M2. k = H(N | PAD(g)) and u = H(PAD(A) | PAD(B)), where PAD adds leading zero bytes up to the 384 bytes of N; x is as RFC 5054 defines it. The private value a is 32 random bytes, fresh for each pair-setup. The proof is M1 = H(H(N) XOR H(g) | H(I) | s | A | B | K) and the session key is K = H(S), 64 bytes; in these, g is the single byte 0x05 and A, B and S are written in their shortest big-endian form, as in the legacy pairing (P50). The receiver's M4 proof is H(A | M1 | K). *Source:* [mit-sender] (user name, group and hash); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders; RFC 2945 §3; RFC 5054 §2.5 and §2.6. *Required:* the sender draws a new a until A is a full 384 bytes, so A reads the same padded or not. The shortest forms of B and S in M1 and K rest on one implementation's reading, so a receiver that pads them computes another proof and answers M3 with error 2 rather than an M4. When B as received starts with a zero byte or S has fewer than 384 significant bytes (together about one pairing in 128), and the M3 answer is error 2 or an M4 whose proof doesn't check, the sender retries once from M1 on a new connection before P150 maps the answer: a transient pairing and a password pairing at once, with the same code or password; a PIN pairing doesn't retry by itself, since it needs the code from the user, and asks the host for the PIN again as P150 says. A retry that fails the same way is mapped as P150 says.
- **P152** HomeKit credentials, which the host keeps per speaker as P54 says for the legacy ones: the sender's identifier, a random UUID in its 36-character text form, created at the speaker's first PIN or password pairing; the 32-byte seed of the sender's Ed25519 key, also random and per speaker; and the receiver's identifier and 32-byte Ed25519 public key from M6. Nothing in them comes from a hardware address or a user name (P5). A transient pairing creates no credentials. *Source:* [mit-sender] (an identifier in UUID text form); AudioSync design.
- **P153** On the AirPlay 2 path a connection runs: `GET /info` (P41) in plain text; then the pairing P61 chose: a transient pair-setup (M1 to M4), a pair-verify with stored credentials, or, for a new PIN or password pairing, `/pair-pin-start` (PIN only), pair-setup M1 to M6 and a pair-verify on the same connection; from the first request after the M4 answer of the transient pair-setup or of the pair-verify, every byte is framed (P67); then the session SETUP (P43), the event connection (P70) and the rest of P42. *Source:* [mit-sender]; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (two open senders put pairing after `/info` and before the session SETUP). *Required:* no session SETUP goes out on a connection that isn't framed yet. A PIN pairing the host asks for outside a session (P61's PIN case, as the legacy pairing of P47 has its own connection) runs on a connection of its own: `GET /info`, `/pair-pin-start`, pair-setup M1 to M6 and the pair-verify, after which the connection stays open and framed with no session SETUP yet, and the host stores the credentials, unless flags bit 3 is set, when there is nothing to store (P182). When the host starts the speaker within 30 s, its session goes on over that connection with the session SETUP and no new pair-verify, since a receiver set to ask for a code every time refuses a pair-verify on a later connection. A connection the host doesn't claim within 30 s, cancels, or the receiver closes, or one whose address family differs from the session's PTP timing (P158), is closed, and the speaker's next session starts with the pairing P61 picks: a pair-verify with stored credentials that no answer turned down, bit 3 or not, or else, with bit 3 set, a new PIN pairing. *Source:* AudioSync design; behavior of a receiver set to ask for a code every time.
- **P154** A pair-verify answered with error 2 (authentication), or whose M2 fails the checks of P66, means the receiver no longer takes the stored credentials: the sender reports PairingRequired, as for the legacy verification (P58, P136), and the host decides whether to pair again. Nothing at that point proves the receiver: a reset receiver and an impostor answering at its address look the same, as the impostor can always fail these checks. So neither the sender nor the host deletes anything: the host keeps the stored credentials on disk and lists the speaker as needing its PIN, which it asks for as for any PairingRequired, and only a new pairing that succeeds replaces them; Forget Pairing still deletes them on the user's word. The same holds for every other answer that gives PairingRequired while stored credentials are in use: a 470 or other 4xx to the pair-verify, the legacy verification's failures (P55, P58, P136) and a 403 to the OPTIONS that sets up a connection (P46). Flags bit 3 (PIN required), from `/info` or from the TXT `flags` (P61), is never on its own an answer that turns stored credentials down: both arrive unauthenticated before anything proves the receiver, so anyone on the network can send them. With bit 3 set and stored credentials that no answer turned down, the sender runs one pair-verify with them (P61) before any PIN. When it succeeds the session plays with no PIN, nothing is stored or replaced, and the credentials stay unmarked. Only a rejection of that verify as this clause defines it (error 2, the M2 checks of P66, a 470 or other 4xx) marks them turned down and reports the one PairingRequired that offers the PIN (P182). Any other outcome follows this clause and P58 as on any receiver (errors 1 and 3 to 6 give PairingFailed, error 7 gives Busy, a 5xx gives Refused, a lost connection gives Unreachable or Lost as P134 and P141 say) and neither marks the credentials nor offers the PIN. The sender keeps its own copy too, marked as turned down: later sessions pair with the password when P61 allows it and otherwise verify with them again, so they work again once the impostor is gone. The mark lasts for the rest of the sender's life or until a session whose pair-verify with them succeeded plays, which proves the receiver holds them, the same proof that clears the host's mark below. The host lists the speaker as paired again once a session that proved the receiver holds them plays: on the AirPlay 2 path a pair-verify with them, on the AirPlay 1 path a verification with stored credentials that carry the receiver's key (P56), and never one a password the user typed carried, since an impostor that answers on AirPlay 1 only or meets credentials without that key plays without proving anything. It keeps offering Forget Pairing while they are stored, turned down or not. Errors 3 (back-off), 4 (too many paired senders), 5 (too many attempts) and 6 (unavailable), and 1 or any other value except 7, describe the receiver's state rather than the credentials: the sender reports PairingFailed, presented to the host as a refusal rather than a request to pair, keeps the credentials, and retries as P142 says, no sooner than the wait of item 8 when the answer carries one (at most 600 s); error 7 gives Busy (P137). A non-200 answer to any pairing request follows P58, except that a pair-verify counts only 470 and other 4xx answers (Busy and the 401 challenge aside) as rejected credentials: a 5xx or any other status is a passing fault on the receiver's side, reported as Refused (P138) and retried as usual (P142), and the credentials stay with the sender and the host. *Source:* AudioSync design; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (one open sender goes on after a failed verification so that the stored key can be deleted); [mit-sender] (the error values, P150); RFC 6762 §21 (Multicast DNS assumes cooperating hosts and authenticates nothing on its own). - **P182** A receiver with flags bit 3 (PIN required) set asks for a code on every connection: it refuses a pair-verify with credentials from an earlier connection. After M6 of a PIN pairing with it the sender stores nothing and reports the pairing as done for this session only, with no credentials, and the host stores none, keeping any it stored before (P154); the session goes on over the pairing connection (P153). Bit 3 is only what the receiver announces, unauthenticated (P154), so the hosts count such a receiver as paired while HomeKit credentials are stored for it that no answer turned down: they list it as paired, auto-connect turns it on and a switch restored as on stays on, and its session starts with the one pair-verify of P61, never with a PIN pairing. With none stored, or once that verify is rejected and marks them turned down (P154), the hosts list it as asking for a code every time; from a PIN pairing they list it as able to play until its session ends or the speaker leaves the sender, and as asking for a PIN again after that. Auto-connect and a restored switch never start a PIN pairing or open a PIN prompt for such a receiver: a rejected verify leaves the speaker off with a notice that it needs a PIN ([engine-spec] S223). A refused verify ends the attempt with one PairingRequired and no new connection on the sender's own. *Source:* behavior of a TV whose TXT `flags` read 0x4c set to ask for a code every time and 0x244 set to ask the first time only; RFC 6762 §21 (Multicast DNS authenticates nothing on its own); AudioSync design (stored credentials outweigh an unauthenticated flag). *Required:* no credentials are stored for a receiver with bit 3 set, and a pair-verify goes to one only with credentials stored before that no answer turned down (P61, P154); a host reads bit 3 as asking for a code only when no such credentials are stored, and never asks for the PIN of a receiver the user didn't turn on by a click (the hosts' part, [engine-spec] S223).
- **P67** Once a transient pair-setup or a pair-verify has completed, every byte on that RTSP connection, in both directions, travels in frames: a 2-byte little-endian length N, N bytes of ChaCha20-Poly1305 ciphertext, then the 16-byte tag. An RTSP message may span several frames. The first framed byte is the first byte of the request after the M4 answer that completed the pairing (P153); that answer and everything before it travel in plain text. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* the sender writes frames of at most 1024 plaintext bytes (the HomeKit limit, which AirPlay receivers are said not to insist on) and accepts any N up to 65 535.
- **P68** The sender-to-receiver key is HKDF-SHA-512 with the session's shared secret (P64, P66) as input, salt "Control-Salt", info "Control-Write-Encryption-Key" and 32 bytes of output; the receiver-to-sender key uses info "Control-Read-Encryption-Key". *Source:* public protocol write-ups; [mit-sender]; RFC 5869.
- **P69** Each direction keeps a 64-bit frame counter that starts at 0 and goes up by one per frame; the 12-byte ChaCha20-Poly1305 nonce is four zero bytes followed by the counter in little-endian; the additional data is the frame's 2-byte length field. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; [mit-sender]; RFC 8439 §2.8. *Required:* this layout, which an MIT-licensed sender uses on AirPlay 2 receivers; one session against a real receiver confirms it before release (G17).
- **P70** The first AirPlay 2 SETUP response names an `eventPort`. The sender opens a TCP connection to it and frames it like P67, with its own frame counters starting at 0 in each direction and keys derived as in P68 from the session's shared secret (P64, P66) with salt "Events-Salt" and infos "Events-Write-Encryption-Key" and "Events-Read-Encryption-Key", roles swapped: the sender encrypts with the Read key and decrypts with the Write key. The receiver sends RTSP requests on it (such as `POST /command` with a binary plist); the sender MUST answer each with `RTSP/1.0 200 OK`, the request's CSeq, `Content-Length: 0` and `Audio-Latency: 0`, and copies back a `Server` header when the request has one, or the receiver closes the connection after about 30 s. The sender connects after the session SETUP answer and before the stream SETUP, and retries a refused connect up to 5 times, 1 s apart. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; [mit-sender].
- **P71** *Withdrawn*: the trailer order it gave (nonce, then tag) is the reverse of what open senders and an open receiver use. P155 and P156 replace it.
- **P155** On the AirPlay 2 path each realtime audio packet is the 12-byte RTP header of P78, then the payload sealed with ChaCha20-Poly1305 (RFC 8439) under the stream key `shk` (P156), then the 16-byte tag, then an 8-byte nonce; the ciphertext is as long as the payload, so a packet is 36 bytes longer than its payload. The 12-byte nonce is four zero bytes followed by the 8 trailer bytes, and the additional data is header bytes 4–11 (timestamp and SSRC). A resent packet (P87) is the sealed packet exactly as first sent, never sealed again, so an AirPlay 2 session keeps its last 1024 sealed packets in a store of its own, allocated when its stream starts (P111). *Source:* [mit-sender]; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (two senders and one receiver agree on this layout; the receiver takes the nonce from the trailer and drops a datagram shorter than 36 bytes). *Required:* the 8 trailer bytes are a 64-bit counter in little-endian order: 0 for the first packet sealed under a key and one more for each packet sealed after it, as the MIT-licensed sender does. Another open sender puts the RTP sequence number there, which repeats under one key after 65 536 packets (about 8.7 minutes); the sender never does that, since a nonce used twice under one key breaks ChaCha20-Poly1305 (RFC 8439 §4).
- **P156** The stream key `shk` of each stream SETUP (P44) is 32 bytes from the operating system's secure random source, new for each stream SETUP, and the nonce counter of P155 starts again at 0 with it; a FLUSH keeps both. The key never appears in logs or diagnostics. *Source:* AudioSync design; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (one receiver ignores a `shk` that isn't 32 bytes long). Note: two open senders derive `shk` from the pairing secret instead; the receiver takes it as given either way.
- **P218** The AirPlay 1 outcome that moved a speaker to the AirPlay 2 path (Unsupported or Refused, P2) is remembered per device (P10), not per session or per speaker the host holds: the host removing the speaker, its session ending for good, a retry (P142) and a pairing all leave it in place. It is forgotten when the device's `_raop._tcp` service goes away (a goodbye or an expired record, P18), since a receiver that restarts or changes its settings may answer AirPlay 1 differently, and when an AirPlay 1 session with the device has its RECORD answered with 200 (P32), which shows the path works; otherwise it lasts until the sender stops. A TXT change forgets nothing, as receivers change their flags with their state (P19). Nothing of it is written to disk, so each run tries AirPlay 1 first again. While it is remembered, every new session of the speaker goes to the AirPlay 2 path with no AirPlay 1 connection first; a target's start still follows P177, which reads the outcomes of that start only. With the AirPlay 2 switch off nothing is remembered and every AirPlay 1 outcome is reported as it is (P170). The sender tells the host, for its debug log, when it remembers or forgets an outcome, with the outcome and the reason. *Source:* AudioSync design (P2): a fake-receiver run showed a speaker that refused AirPlay 1 and asked for a PIN on AirPlay 2 losing its outcome when the host let it go, after which the pairing the host started took the legacy path and the PIN prompt never came. *Required:* the lifetime above; neither the host's removal nor a pairing forgets the outcome.
- **P219** With the AirPlay 2 switch on, a pairing the host starts for a device with a remembered outcome (P218) is the AirPlay 2 path's PIN pairing (P61, P153), on a connection of its own to the `_airplay._tcp` address: `GET /info`, then what P61 chooses, `/pair-pin-start` and a PIN pairing for flags bit 9 or bit 3, or the password pairing for bit 7. It never runs the legacy pairing of P46 and P47 and opens no connection to the `_raop._tcp` port. What the user sees: the receiver shows its code after `/pair-pin-start`, and the host shows the PIN prompt it shows for every AirPlay 2 PIN pairing ([engine-spec] S223), asked again after a wrong PIN as P150 says; for bit 7 without bit 3 or 9 the host shows its password prompt instead (PasswordRequired, P135) when it has no password for the speaker this run (P61, [engine-spec] S223). A receiver with bit 3 asks for a code at every connection (P182), and when the hosts switch it on or ask for its code is theirs to decide ([engine-spec] S223); every pairing they start for it while P218 remembers the outcome goes to this path. A bit-9 pairing that succeeds stores HomeKit credentials (P152), after which P2's rule for paired speakers keeps the device on the AirPlay 2 path whatever P218 remembers. *Source:* AudioSync design (P2, P46, P61); [mit-sender] (the PIN start on the AirPlay 2 connection). *Required:* no request of the pairing reaches the `_raop._tcp` port, and the host never shows a second prompt before the user answered the first; it asks again only as P150 says.
- **P220** With the AirPlay 2 switch on, a pairing the host starts for an AirPlay 2 capable device (P16) that advertises `_raop._tcp` and has no remembered outcome, as one started from discovery's hint (P46) or after the outcome was forgotten (P218), first runs P46's decision rule on the `_raop._tcp` address. When that rule ends in Unsupported, or its OPTIONS gets an answer that gives Refused (P138), before any `/pair-pin-start` went out (both at the OPTIONS step, the only AirPlay 1 request a pairing sends before it may show a code), the sender remembers the outcome (P218) and goes on, within the same pairing as the host sees it, with the pairing of P219 on a new connection to the `_airplay._tcp` address. The host gets no Unsupported or Refused in between and shows at most one prompt. A legacy pairing that sent `/pair-pin-start` never switches paths, so the receiver never shows two codes for one pairing; PasswordRequired, WrongPassword, Busy, PairingRequired and PairingFailed never switch paths either (P2). With the switch off, or for a device that isn't AirPlay 2 capable, P46's outcome is reported as it is. A refusal that comes only at ANNOUNCE (a 4xx such as 406 from a device that isn't an Apple TV, P138) is out of this clause's sight: the pairing never sends ANNOUNCE, and the sender sends no ANNOUNCE or other probe before a pairing to find one. On a fresh run such a device therefore takes the known path: a pairing from the hint goes the legacy way as P46 says; the first stream attempt gets the ANNOUNCE refusal, which moves the speaker to the AirPlay 2 path and is remembered (P2, P218); the AirPlay 2 session then reports PairingRequired, and the next pairing the host starts is P219's, with one prompt. *Source:* AudioSync design (P2, P46). *Required:* the switch happens only before `/pair-pin-start`, only on Unsupported or Refused at the OPTIONS step, and with no ANNOUNCE sent by the pairing.
- **P221** The fake-receiver check for P218 to P220. The fake advertises `_raop._tcp` and `_airplay._tcp` under one device id, with features bits that make it AirPlay 2 capable (P16) and a model that isn't an Apple TV; on AirPlay 1 it either answers OPTIONS with 200 and a `Public` without ANNOUNCE (Unsupported, P27) or answers ANNOUNCE with 406 (Refused, P138); on the AirPlay 2 port it answers `/info` with status flags bit 9 set, or bit 3 in a second run, takes `/pair-pin-start` and pairs with a fixed PIN. *Required:* with the switch on: (a) the host adds the speaker: one AirPlay 1 attempt, then the AirPlay 2 session reports PairingRequired with no `/pair-pin-start` sent; (b) the host removes the speaker and then starts a pairing: nothing reaches the `_raop._tcp` port, `/pair-pin-start` reaches the AirPlay 2 port once, the host is asked for the PIN, the fixed PIN pairs, and a start within 30 s plays over that connection (P153); (c) with bit 3, in the Unsupported variant, nothing is stored, and a pairing started after that session ended goes to the AirPlay 2 port again (P182); (d) in the Unsupported variant only, with no session before, a pairing started from the hint sends one OPTIONS to the `_raop._tcp` port and then (b)'s sequence, with no Unsupported or Refused reported in between (P220); (e) after a goodbye for the `_raop._tcp` record and a new announcement, the next start begins with one OPTIONS to the `_raop._tcp` port (P218); (f) in the ANNOUNCE 406 variant, on a fresh run: a pairing from the hint goes the legacy way, its OPTIONS gets 200 listing ANNOUNCE and, as the TXT flags call for pairing, it sends `/pair-pin-start` to the `_raop._tcp` port, which the fake answers with 404, since a receiver that turns AirPlay 1 audio down at ANNOUNCE has no reason to carry the Apple TV legacy pairing there and keeps its PIN on the AirPlay 2 side; the pairing ends in PairingFailed (P58), with no prompt shown, no code asked for and no ANNOUNCE sent, and P220 doesn't switch on it; the host then starts a stream, whose attempt gets the 406, the AirPlay 2 session reports PairingRequired, and the next pairing sends nothing to the `_raop._tcp` port and asks for the PIN once on the AirPlay 2 port (P220's known path). With the switch off, (b)'s pairing takes the legacy path, nothing reaches the AirPlay 2 port (P170), and the speaker's AirPlay 1 outcome is reported as it is. A speaker of this kind on the network is checked by hand only when one is at hand: no such receiver is known yet. *Source:* AudioSync design.

## 5. Audio

- **P72** The stream is 44 100 Hz, 16-bit signed, 2 channels, left then right. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P73** The host hands the crate interleaved stereo frames at 44 100 Hz, as float32 or signed 16-bit, already resampled. The crate converts float to 16-bit by multiplying by 32 767, rounding to nearest and clamping to the 16-bit range; a NaN or infinite sample becomes 0. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P74** Codec per speaker: ALAC when `cn` contains 1 or is absent, since that is the path the published captures show (P29); PCM only when `cn` contains 0 and either the host enabled PCM for that device or `cn` lacks 1; otherwise the speaker is unsupported. A receiver might accept an ANNOUNCE for PCM and still drop the packets, so PCM stays opt-in until G10 is closed. If an ANNOUNCE offering PCM is refused with a 4xx status other than 401 and 453 and `cn` also contains 1, the sender retries once with ALAC on a new connection and remembers the choice for that device. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; AudioSync design (G10).
- **P75** Every packet holds 352 frames (about 7.98 ms) with either codec. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P76** A PCM payload is 352 frames × 2 channels × 2 bytes = 1408 bytes, each sample big-endian, left before right. With the 12-byte header the datagram fits a 1500-byte MTU over IPv4 and IPv6. *Source:* RFC 3551 §4.5.11 (L16 is in network byte order); public protocol write-ups.
- **P77** An ALAC payload is one ALAC frame of exactly 352 frames, produced by an ALAC encoder set up as P29 (16-bit, 2 channels, 44 100 Hz, frame length 352); the encoder may emit compressed or uncompressed frames. The ALAC bitstream is defined by Apple's open-source codec, not here. *Source:* [alac]; public protocol write-ups.
- **P78** The RTP header is 12 bytes: byte 0 is 0x80 (version 2, no padding, no extension, no CSRC); byte 1 is 0x60 (payload type 96), or 0xE0 (marker set) on the first packet after RECORD or FLUSH; bytes 2–3 the sequence number; bytes 4–7 the RTP timestamp; bytes 8–11 the SSRC, random per session and fixed within it. The payload follows. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; RFC 3550 §5.1.
- **P79** The first packet uses the sequence number and timestamp announced in RECORD; each later packet adds 1 to the sequence number (modulo 2^16) and 352 to the timestamp (modulo 2^32). Comparisons are modular. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; RFC 3550 §5.1.
- **P80** The shared timeline (P96) is cut into packets at frame indices that are multiples of 352. Every speaker gets the same packet contents, encoded once per packet, each with its own header; a speaker that joins late starts at a packet boundary. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P81** For speaker s, frame n has RTP timestamp (k_s + n) modulo 2^32, so packet j (frames 352·j to 352·j + 351) carries (k_s + 352·j) modulo 2^32. k_s is chosen at RECORD so the first packet gets the announced rtptime, and chosen again at each FLUSH so numbering continues from the value the FLUSH names (P34), and again at a resume from an explicit pause so the first whole packet after the resume carries the value its FLUSH named (P213). *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P82** Each session opens two local UDP sockets on ephemeral ports, in the address family of its RTSP connection: control (announced as `control_port`) and timing (announced as `timing_port`). *Source:* AudioSync design; public protocol write-ups.
- **P83** Audio packets go to the receiver's `server_port`; sync packets and retransmission replies go to its `control_port`; all three are sent from the session's control socket. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* the sending socket.
- **P84** Packet j is due to leave for speaker s at c(352·j + 351) + M + d_s: when its last frame has arrived from the host (P96), plus a safety margin M, plus the speaker's delay (P99). M covers the host's block size and arrival jitter: at least 20 ms, and at least one host block plus the largest positive residual of the rate fit (P101) over the last 30 s, capped at 100 ms; it may rise at once and falls by at most 1 ms per second. M moves only send times, never P_s(n): L absorbs it, so a receiver's lead shrinks from about L to about L − M. The sender sends each packet within 5 ms of its time and never more than 20 ms early; a packet already past its time is sent at once (P112). *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P85** While a session plays, packets never pause; when the host has no audio, they carry digital silence. The one exception is an explicit pause (P213). *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P86** Retransmission requests reach the session's control socket from the receiver: 8 bytes, 0x80 0xD5, a 2-byte sequence field, then the first missing sequence number (2 bytes) and the number of missing packets (2 bytes). Receivers address them to wherever the sender's control packets come from (one open receiver records the source address and port of the first packet that reaches its control port; the other never asks for retransmissions at all), so they reach the control socket because sync packets leave from it (P83), and none can arrive before the first sync. The sender takes them only from the receiver's address, from any source port, and drops a datagram from any other address unread and counts it (P144): nothing in the protocol keeps another host on the network from writing to the control socket, and a forged request would draw resends. The receiver's address is the one the session connected to (P17); an IPv4-mapped IPv6 source counts as its IPv4 address, and for an IPv6 address that isn't link-local any address in the same /64 prefix counts too, since a receiver may send from one of its temporary addresses (RFC 8981), which keep the prefix. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders; RFC 8981 §3; Microsoft's Winsock documentation of SO_RCVTIMEO (a timed-out socket's state is indeterminate); AudioSync design (the source address rule, the wake-up). *Required:* the control and timing sockets are read by a receive that waits with only a long backstop timeout, 1 to 5 s, since on Windows a receive that times out as a datagram arrives can drop that datagram, losing a retransmission or timing request; a timed-out receive just looks at the stop flag again. Closing such a socket wakes its receive with an empty datagram the socket sends itself; an empty datagram is dropped unread and not counted, from any address. A thread whose wake-up can't be sent (its local address went away with the network) still ends within the backstop.
- **P87** For each requested packet still held, the sender sends to the receiver's control port 0x80 0xD6, a 2-byte sequence field holding that packet's sequence number, then the complete RTP packet exactly as first sent (header and payload). Packets no longer held are skipped without a reply. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* the value of the sequence field.
- **P88** Each session can resend its last 1024 packets (about 8.2 s); since payloads are shared (P80), one payload store serves all sessions, and each session keeps only its headers, except that an AirPlay 2 session keeps its own sealed packets (P155). A request for more than 128 packets is clipped to the first 128, and a packet is resent at most 3 times. The cap matters: one open receiver asks again for the gap at the head of its buffer every time any packet arrives, so one lost packet can bring many identical requests. A receiver's buffer can be small (256 packets, about 2 s, in one open receiver, 64 in the other), and a sequence number more than a buffer ahead of the oldest packet held makes both discard what they hold, so numbering never jumps (P79). *Source:* public protocol write-ups; AudioSync design; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders.
- **P168** The sender never sets up buffered audio (stream type 103), so it never sends `SETRATEANCHORTIME` or `FLUSHBUFFERED`. For the record: buffered audio flows over a TCP connection to the `dataPort` the receiver names. One open receiver answers its stream SETUP with `type` 103, `dataPort` and `audioBufferSize` (8 MiB), reads `audioFormat`, `ct`, `spf` and `shk` from the request, starts playback at the `rtpTime` that `SETRATEANCHORTIME` ties to `networkTimeSecs`, `networkTimeFrac` and `networkTimeTimelineID` (a PTP clock), with bit 0 of `rate` meaning play, and drops the audio between `flushFromSeq` and `flushFromTS` and `flushUntilSeq` and `flushUntilTS` (no `flushFromSeq` means at once). Buffered audio needs the TCP media transport and in practice AAC, both out of scope (P1), and PTP timing; live capture doesn't need it. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders.
- **P178** Every member of a target gets the same packet contents as any speaker (P80): the full stereo mix, never one channel, a mono downmix or a pan. Which channel a member plays is the receivers' choice, made where the user set up the pair (the pair's audio settings in the Home app swap left and right); the host processes the target's audio once, the same for every member. *Source:* [apple-homepod-pair] (left and right are switched in the paired speakers' Audio Settings); public user reports (a sender that plays the same stream to both members of a pair gets stereo, standalone or behind an Apple TV; an older desktop sender that has only AirPlay 1 plays to both members too, after a HomePod release fixed selecting both). *Required:* which channel each member plays on each protocol, and what one member plays while the other is missing, are checked on hardware (G18).

## 6. Timing

- **P89** The sender clock is one monotonic clock for all sessions, never stepped or slewed by wall-clock changes, read as a 64-bit NTP-format timestamp (32-bit seconds, 32-bit fraction). By convention its seconds count from 0x83AA7E80 (the Unix epoch in NTP seconds) plus the time since the clock's origin. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; RFC 5905 §6. *Required:* the host schedules its local outputs on the same clock, so the crate either exposes its clock reads or takes the host's clock.
- **P90** Once a session is set up, the receiver sends 32-byte timing requests to the sender's timing port, about every 3 s: 0x80 0xD2, a 2-byte sequence number, four zero bytes, then three NTP timestamps (origin, receive, transmit), of which only transmit (the receiver's send time) is filled in. Receivers vary in the details, and the sender relies on none of them: two open receivers put 0x0007 in the sequence field, one asks every 3 s and the other every second, and one fills origin and receive of later requests with the previous exchange's values (the sender's last transmit time and its own arrival time). *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* the sender reads only the sequence field and transmit, whatever the other bytes hold.
- **P91** The sender answers each request with 32 bytes to the request's source address and port: 0x80 0xD3, the request's sequence number, four zero bytes, origin = the request's transmit timestamp copied unchanged, receive = sender clock when the request arrived, transmit = sender clock just before sending. One open receiver waits 300 ms for a reply, discards anything later (it empties its socket before each request), and keeps the exchange with the lowest round-trip delay among its last 8, so a slow or uneven reply costs accuracy and a late one is lost. The sender answers only requests from the receiver's address as P86 defines it, whatever their source port, since the reply goes back to that port and SETUP may name none; a datagram from any other address is dropped unanswered and counted (P144), which screens out stray datagrams, for example from a stale session or another device; it is not a security boundary, since a host in the same IPv6 /64 or one that forges its source address still gets through (P131). *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders; AudioSync design (the source address rule). *Required:* both clock reads sit as close to the socket calls as possible, on a thread that does nothing slow, so processing stays under 1 ms and a reply never leaves more than 100 ms after its request arrived; the reply goes to the request's source even when SETUP returned `timing_port` 0.
- **P92** In AirPlay 1 the sender sends no timing requests of its own and ignores anything on the timing socket that isn't a request. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; AudioSync design.
- **P93** A sync packet is 20 bytes to the receiver's control port: byte 0 is 0x90 on the first sync after RECORD or FLUSH (extension bit set) and 0x80 otherwise; byte 1 is 0xD4; bytes 2–3 a sequence field; bytes 4–7 the RTP timestamp of the frame due to be heard at the time in bytes 8–15; bytes 8–15 a sender-clock time in NTP format; bytes 16–19 the RTP timestamp of the next audio packet the sender will send to this receiver. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* the sequence field counts up from 0 per session; the published capture shows a small value and the AirTunes 2 write-up's pseudocode uses a constant 7, so receivers aren't expected to read it, and neither open receiver studied does.
- **P94** One sync goes right before the first audio packet after RECORD and after each FLUSH, and then one before the first packet that starts at or after each multiple of 44 100 frames counted from the stream's first frame (about once a second); each goes just before the packet whose timestamp it carries in bytes 16–19. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* the packet-boundary rule.
- **P95** The receiver lines up its playback so that the frame named in bytes 4–7 is heard at the sender-clock time in bytes 8–15, using the timing exchange to translate sender time into its own; bytes 16–19 minus bytes 4–7 is the latency the sender asks for, in frames. In the published capture that difference is 77 175 frames (1.75 s). One open receiver works exactly this way: at every sync packet it ties the timestamp in bytes 4–7 to the time in bytes 8–15, places every audio frame on that line at 44 100 frames per second, translates the result into its own clock with the offset the timing exchange gave when the first audio packet arrived (later changes of the offset aren't followed), and plays it then; it ignores bytes 16–19, holds back audio until the first sync arrives, and adds nothing for the `Audio-Latency` it reports unless its user configures an extra delay. Its notes give 77 175 frames as the difference its senders use for ALAC (and 7 497 for AAC-ELD). The other open receiver ignores sync packets and plays audio as it arrives. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (derived from a published example); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* this reading is the model the sender follows. How Apple's receivers apply it (for example whether they add their own `Audio-Latency`) is checked on hardware (G9).
- **P96** The frames from the host, plus any silence the crate inserts (P112), are numbered n = 0, 1, 2, … from the start of the stream. c(n) is the sender-clock time at which frame n arrived from the host, as given by the rate model (P101). Speaker s with delay d_s hears frame n at P_s(n) = c(n) + L + d_s, where L = Lf / 44 100 s is the base latency (P98). *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P97** For speaker s, with j the next packet to send: bytes 16–19 = the RTP timestamp of frame 352·j; bytes 4–7 = the RTP timestamp of frame 352·j − Lf (P81, modulo 2^32); bytes 8–15 = P_s(352·j − Lf) as the model gives it, not the moment the packet happens to leave. Because packet j leaves at about c(352·j + 351) + M + d_s (P84), the stated time lies within about M + 10 ms of the send time. *Source:* AudioSync design, applying P95.
- **P98** The base latency Lf defaults to 77 175 frames (1.75 s), the value in the published capture. The host may set any value from 11 025 to 88 200 frames (0.25 to 2.0 s), the range an Apple sender announced in an AirPlay 2 SETUP. All speakers of a stream use the same Lf; changing it flushes every session, as a timeline restart does (P143). The host sets it from what the receivers report (P200), and P201 says when a new value takes effect. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (derived from a published example). *Required:* the default and the range.
- **P99** Each speaker has a delay d_s from 0 to 20 000 ms, set by the host, the same range as the host's own delays ([engine-spec] S77), so a calibrated offset or a Google Cast hold-back up to it plays in full. The sender applies it in two places at once: it sends that speaker's packets d_s later (P84) and states times d_s later in its sync packets (P96). The latency field (bytes 16–19 minus bytes 4–7) stays Lf for every speaker, so a receiver's buffer fill doesn't change with its delay. Shifting the send time as well keeps the delay working on a receiver that plays audio as it arrives and ignores sync packets, as one open receiver does, though such a receiver can't be held in step to better than its own network and buffer jitter. Since the receiver's buffer fill and lead don't depend on d_s, a long delay costs only the sender's store (P111), about 3.5 MB of audio per codec at 20 s. *Source:* AudioSync design; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders.
- **P100** A delay change for a playing speaker takes effect through a FLUSH of that speaker: the sender stops sending to it, sends FLUSH, and resumes with the next packet due under the new delay. That speaker is silent for about L; the others are untouched. Changes arriving within 250 ms of each other are merged into one flush. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P213** An explicit pause flushes every session of the stream. When the host tells the crate the stream is paused (on the host's side: Pause, or a PlayPause that pauses, from a receiver's remote through DACP (P192, [engine-spec] S274); the app's own pause button; or the source player seen going from playing to paused in the host's now-playing readings, whatever paused it, a media key included ([engine-spec] S292)), the crate stops sending audio to every speaker of the stream at once and sends each session a FLUSH as P100 does, on both paths; on the AirPlay 2 path it is the FLUSH of the realtime stream (P42), never `FLUSHBUFFERED` (P168). The FLUSH carries `RTP-Info: seq=<s>;rtptime=<t>` (P34), where s is the sequence number after the last packet sent to that speaker and t the RTP timestamp that packet would have carried: the flush point is the first packet not sent. Packets already prepared but not yet sent (the margin of P84 and the speaker's delay d_s, P99) are dropped, never sent after the FLUSH; requests to resend packets before s get no answer; the frames the host delivers while paused are counted (P96) but never sent. While paused the session stays up: no audio or sync packets (P93, P166) go out, and timing replies (P91), keep-alives (P131, P132), volume (P118), metadata and the command server (P186) go on. A delay change (P100) or a new Lf (P201) set during a pause sends no FLUSH and applies at resume. On resume (Play or a PlayPause that resumes through DACP, the app's play button, or the source player seen going from paused to playing) the crate sends no new FLUSH, RECORD or SETUP: numbering continues from s and t (P81), with k_s chosen again at the resume so the first whole packet of the shared timeline (P80) that starts at or after the first frame the host delivers after the resume carries timestamp t. The frames between that first frame and the packet boundary, fewer than 352 (under 8 ms), are dropped, not sent as silence: payloads are cut once on the shared 352-frame grid for every speaker (P80), so a partial packet would need a payload of its own, and the dropped frames fall in the first milliseconds of a resume whose sound starts about L later anyway; the next sync packet carries the first-packet flag (P93, P94) and the next audio packet the marker (P78); that frame plays at its time under P96, about L + d_s after it arrives, in step with the other speakers (P102), as after a start. A resume that comes before a session's FLUSH answer waits for it; a pause while paused and a resume while playing do nothing. A speaker lost during a pause (P141) is retried as P142 says. A host that paused may resume on its own once its capture carries sound again, at least 3 s after the pause ([engine-spec] S292). Silence never pauses: neither the crate nor the host infers a pause from the audio (digital silence, a quiet passage, a source app that stops making sound, an underrun of P112), so those keep the packets of P85 flowing. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (FLUSH as the request with which a sender stops the stream when playback pauses, its `RTP-Info` naming the position); project documentation pages (an open receiver's metadata documentation reports a FLUSH as a pause or flush of the stream and the first audio after it as a resume); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (receivers empty their buffer on FLUSH and wait for the sequence number it names, P34; an open media server sends FLUSH to its speakers when its user pauses). *Required:* a flush on explicit pause only, resume without new requests, and silence never a trigger are AudioSync design.
- **P101** c(n) is piecewise linear: c(n) = c(n_u) + (n − n_u) / R̂ from the last update at frame n_u. R̂ starts at 44 100 Hz and follows the host's actual frame rate measured on the sender clock, as a least-squares fit of (frames delivered, arrival time) over the last 30 s or an equivalent delay-locked loop. R̂ changes only at sync points, stays within ±1000 ppm of 44 100 Hz, and each change moves the predicted time of any frame up to the next sync point (44 100 frames ahead) by at most 0.5 ms. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P102** All speakers share one c(n): for every frame n and any two speakers s1 and s2, P_s1(n) − P_s2(n) = d_s1 − d_s2 at all times, within one frame. This is the multi-room guarantee. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P103** A speaker that joins a playing stream starts, after its RECORD, with the next packet in its regular schedule (P84), preceded by a first-flagged sync; it is heard about L − M after it is sent, in step with the others. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P104** In AirPlay 2 the first SETUP names `timingProtocol`: "PTP", with `timingPeerInfo` (`Addresses`, `ID`, `SupportsClockPortMatchingOverride`) and `timingPeerList`, or "NTP". Under PTP the receiver answers with `timingPort` 0 and its own `timingPeerInfo`; under NTP it opens a timing port and the AirPlay 1 exchange applies. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P105** *Withdrawn*: P158 to P163 say what PTP the sender runs for one receiver; what stays unknown is in G1.
- **P106** SETPEERS has `Content-Type: /peer-list-changed` and a binary-plist array of address strings (every group member's addresses, the receivers' and the sender's); it is sent after RECORD and whenever the group changes. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* the sender sends it only under PTP timing, as P165 says.
- **P107** *Withdrawn*: P166 replaces it. Its note stays: one write-up gives the timing request and reply as 211 and 212, one more than the 0xD2 and 0xD3 that the others give; the sender uses 0xD2 and 0xD3.
- **P108** *Withdrawn*: P157 replaces it.
- **P157** The AirPlay 2 path asks for "NTP" timing in the session SETUP (P43, P104) and then applies this section as for AirPlay 1. When a receiver answers that SETUP with a status other than 200 (one open receiver answers 501, since it handles only PTP), its features (P14) set bit 41 and the PTP service (P158) is running, the sender tries once more on a new connection with "PTP" timing (P164) and remembers that choice for the device, across launches as P217 says; otherwise the speaker is Unsupported with the reason timing. The sender never asks for "None", which one open sender's notes say leaves an Apple TV silent at the stream SETUP. *Source:* observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders; [mit-sender] (a sender that asks for NTP); AudioSync design (the order). *Required:* NTP first, since it keeps one timing model for both paths.
- **P217** The timing choice of P157 outlives the launch. Once a receiver answers a session SETUP with 200, the sender reports to the host the timing it took ("NTP" or "PTP") with the receiver's `deviceid` (P9) and the `features` and `srcvers` values (P13) its TXT record showed at that SETUP; the host keeps that entry in its settings store, on both hosts, keyed by `deviceid` and never by address or name, so a receiver that moves to a new address or is renamed keeps it. At a session start the host hands the sender the entry for that `deviceid`, if any. An entry that says "PTP", while the TXT record still shows the same `features` and `srcvers`, bit 41 is set and the PTP service (P158) is running, makes the first session SETUP ask for "PTP" at once (P164), with no "NTP" attempt first; an entry that says "NTP" changes nothing, since P157 asks for "NTP" first anyway. When the PTP service is not running, the sender acts as P157 says and leaves the entry as it is. An entry is forgotten, and the next session start runs P157 from the beginning, when the receiver's `features` or `srcvers` differ from the stored values (a firmware update or a changed setting the TXT record shows), and when the host forgets the device's pairing. When the session SETUP with the remembered "PTP" gets an answer other than 200, the sender drops the entry and runs P157 once on a new connection, "NTP" first, and the timing that then gets 200 becomes the new entry; when none does, the speaker is Unsupported with the reason timing, as in P157, and no entry is kept. A connection that fails or closes before the SETUP is answered is not an answer and leaves the entry as it is. Members of a target (P177) each keep an entry of their own. A receiver with no entry, one never seen before included, runs P157 exactly as without this clause. *Source:* AudioSync design; measured against an open receiver in a Linux virtual machine (26 Oct 2026): the first session SETUP of a fresh launch asks for "NTP", gets 501 and takes PTP on the retry, on every launch. *Required:* the timing round trip runs once per receiver, not once per launch: a fake-receiver test that answers "NTP" with 501 and sets bit 41 sees one "NTP" session SETUP across two sender launches that share one host store [emulated]; a change of `srcvers` between the launches brings the "NTP" attempt back once.
- **P158** The PTP service is one per sender and serves its one AirPlay 2 session (P169; every AirPlay 2 session on PTP once P205 is built) while that session uses PTP timing: two UDP sockets bound to port 319 (event messages) and port 320 (general messages) for the address family in use. The sender is the PTP grandmaster for its receiver, sends to the receiver's ports 319 and 320 by unicast, and never follows another clock. When either port can't be bound (another PTP stack holds it, or the system reserves ports below 1024), PTP is unavailable: the sender tells the host once, and P157 leaves receivers that need PTP Unsupported. When a later session binds both ports after that report, the sender tells the host the ports are free (warning 12 at the C interface), and it reports a clash after that again. A session that takes PTP over an address outside the service's family while no other session uses the service is closed without a TEARDOWN and started again with the service bound in the family it reached; while no other session uses the service, that speaker's later sessions, a reconnect after a loss included, bind it in that family first as long as the speaker still has an address in it, and otherwise pick the family as if it never moved. A later session that falls outside that family moves the service again the same way; only a fallback in the session started again after a move starts the speaker on NTP with the warning of P179. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (UDP ports 319 and 320); [mit-ptp]; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (an open receiver's PTP helper needs both ports for itself too).
- **P159** Every PTP message the sender sends is an IEEE 1588-2008 version 2 message with transportSpecific 1 (the upper four bits of byte 0), domain 0, correctionField 0, the flags field 0x0408 (unicast, PTP timescale) or 0x0608 on Sync and Delay_Resp (two-step added), and a sourcePortIdentity of the sender's clock identity and port number 1. The clock identity is 8 bytes, 0xFF 0xFF followed by 6 random bytes made when the PTP service starts; it is not an EUI-64 and comes from no hardware address (P5). Each message type keeps its own 16-bit sequenceId, from 0 up by one per message (once P205 is built, one such counter per message type for each receiver). *Source:* [mit-ptp]; IEEE 1588-2008 (message header). *Required:* port number 1 (AudioSync design).
- **P160** Announce goes to the receiver's port 320 once a second (logMessageInterval 0), with originTimestamp 0, currentUtcOffset 0, grandmasterPriority1 128, grandmasterClockQuality of clockClass 6, clockAccuracy 0x21 and offsetScaledLogVariance 0x436A, grandmasterPriority2 128, grandmasterIdentity the sender's clock identity, stepsRemoved 0, timeSource 0x20, and a path trace TLV holding the clock identity. IEEE 1588-2008 ranks class 6 (a clock synchronized to a primary reference) ahead of the default class 248 in the best-master choice. Announce messages from other clocks are ignored. *Source:* [mit-ptp]; IEEE 1588-2008; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (an open receiver's PTP helper picks its master from Announce: grandmaster identity, priorities and clock quality). *Required:* whether a receiver with a better clock of its own yields to these values is checked on hardware (G17).
- **P161** Sync goes to the receiver's port 319 eight times a second (logMessageInterval −3) as a two-step clock, with originTimestamp 0. A Follow_Up with the same sequenceId follows on port 320; its preciseOriginTimestamp is the sender's PTP time (P163) read as close as possible to the moment the Sync left. The Follow_Up carries two TLVs, in this order: the IEEE 802.1AS Follow_Up information TLV (type 3, length 28, organization 00-80-C2, subtype 1, all other bytes zero), then an organization-extension TLV (type 3, length 14) with Apple's organization 00-0D-93, subtype 4 and the 8-byte clock identity. An open receiver's PTP helper takes its offset from the Follow_Up alone (its time plus correctionField, minus the arrival time, smoothed), ignores the Sync's time, accepts only a first TLV of exactly that kind, and its receiver plays only after the clock has been its master for 400 ms. *Source:* [mit-ptp]; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders.
- **P162** A Delay_Req from the receiver's address (the address rule of P86) is answered with a Delay_Resp to port 320 of the request's source address, carrying the request's sequenceId, logMessageInterval −3, receiveTimestamp the sender's PTP time when the request arrived, and requestingPortIdentity copied from the request's sourcePortIdentity. Pdelay_Req, Signaling and Management messages, and Sync or Follow_Up from another clock, get no answer and change nothing. A datagram from any address other than a served receiver's is dropped by the event socket's thread before it reaches the driver, so a flood from another host on the network queues nothing ahead of the session's audio. The event socket is read and woken on close as P86 says for the control socket, backstop included, so no Delay_Req is lost to a timed-out receive. *Source:* [mit-ptp] (which also answers Pdelay_Req, marked untested there); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (an open receiver's PTP helper sends no Delay_Req at all).
- **P163** The sender's PTP time is the sender clock of P89 read as seconds and nanoseconds since the clock's origin (the NTP-format seconds minus 0x83AA7E80), so one clock serves the host's local outputs, NTP timing and PTP. Every PTP timestamp, and the time in a time-announce packet (P166), comes from it. *Source:* AudioSync design; [mit-ptp] (a sender's PTP clock that runs on a monotonic clock).
- **P164** A session SETUP with "PTP" timing carries `timingProtocol` "PTP" and no `timingPort`; `timingPeerInfo`, a dictionary of `Addresses` (the sender's own addresses of the connection's family), `ID` (a random UUID string made when the PTP service starts), `DeviceType` 0, `ClockID` (the clock identity of P159) and `SupportsClockPortMatchingOverride` false; `timingPeerList`, an array holding that same dictionary; and `groupUUID` (random per session) with `groupContainsGroupLeader` false. The answer holds `timingPort` 0 and the receiver's own `timingPeerInfo`, which the sender records. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (P104); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* `ClockID` is a binary-plist integer with the same 64 bits as the clock identity, which a plist reads as signed (a clock identity starting 0xFFFF is negative); the sender sends its PTP messages to the address the session connected to (P17), never to an address taken from the answer.
- **P165** Under PTP the sender sends SETPEERS (P106) after RECORD and before the stream SETUP, with the receiver's address first and then the sender's own addresses of the connection's family; under NTP it sends none. One open receiver answers it with 200 and reads nothing from it, and two open receivers take NTP-timed audio without it. *Source:* observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders.
- **P166** Under PTP, 28-byte time-announce packets take the place of the sync packets of P93, on the same schedule (P94) and to the same port: byte 0 is 0x90 on the first after RECORD or FLUSH and 0x80 otherwise; byte 1 is 0xD7; bytes 2–3 a sequence field counting up from 0 per session; bytes 4–7 the RTP timestamp of the frame due to be heard at the time in bytes 8–15, as P97 computes it; bytes 8–15 that time as a 64-bit count of nanoseconds of the sender's PTP time (P163); bytes 16–19 the RTP timestamp of the next audio packet minus 11 025; bytes 20–27 the clock identity. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (the second byte 0xD7); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* this layout, which is one open sender's. One open receiver takes bytes 4–7 and 8–15 as its anchor but places the anchor frame 11 025 frames (0.25 s) before the one bytes 4–7 name, and reads bytes 16–19 only to log their distance from bytes 4–7; so on some receivers the sound may land a constant 0.25 s away from where P96 puts it, which the host's per-speaker delay absorbs until a hardware test measures it (G17).
- **P167** The PTP service sends Announce, Sync and Follow_Up to a receiver from the moment its session SETUP with "PTP" timing is answered with 200 until the session ends (TEARDOWN, loss or failure), and to no other address. The first Announce goes at once and Sync runs every 125 ms from then on; with no PTP session it sends nothing. *Source:* [mit-ptp]; AudioSync design. Note: an open receiver waits until the clock has been its master for 400 ms (P161), and a packet is heard about L after it is sent (P98), so the sender adds no wait before the first audio.
- **P179** All members of a target share one delay d_s, set by the host for the target, so P102 holds between them as between any two speakers, and a member that starts late joins as P103 says. Under NTP each member's session runs its own timing exchange (P90, P91) against the one sender clock. Under PTP the PTP service (P158) serves every member of the target, each as P167 says for one receiver; each member's session SETUP is that of P164, with a `groupUUID` of its own; and each member's SETPEERS (P165) is the list of P207. A target may mix address families: a member with no address in the running service's family plays on NTP, and the sender tells the host once that PTP is unavailable for it; the service never moves to another family while a member uses it. The service starts in IPv4 when any member in view has an IPv4 address, so only members reachable over IPv6 alone fall to NTP; a member that comes into view with IPv4 addresses only, after a member with IPv6 addresses only started the service in IPv6, stays on NTP too, since the service does not rebind, until the service is free when that member's session starts again. A member whose session takes PTP over an address outside that family while another member's session uses the service gets the same warning; that session is closed without a TEARDOWN and the member starts again with a session that asks for NTP only, the close opening the 10 s window of P181 as the sender's own TEARDOWN does. *Source:* AudioSync design (one delay; mixed families); public protocol write-ups (an Apple sender's SETPEERS to one receiver of a group lists that receiver, a second receiver and the sender, in that order); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (the media server and its fork give each session its own random `groupUUID` and play stereo pairs that way, and the fork lists a pair's other member in each member's SETPEERS). *Required:* how the members keep in step with each other (through the sender's timing or a link of their own), and whether one `groupUUID` shared by all members, as receivers in an Apple sender's group suggest by advertising the same `gid` (P175), changes anything, are checked on hardware (G18).

### Several AirPlay 2 receivers

- **P204** Several AirPlay 2 speakers at once. Once built, this replaces the limit of P169: the AirPlay 2 path drives every AirPlay 2 speaker and target the host asks for at the same time, up to 16 AirPlay 2 sessions per sender (each member of a target counts as one), next to any number of AirPlay 1 sessions. Each is a session of its own (P3) on AudioSync's timeline (P96) with its own delay (P99), whatever its path and timing protocol, so P102 holds between any two of them. Volume (P118, P180), metadata (P123), artwork (P185), feedback (P132), the event connection (P70) and remote commands (P193) stay per session as they are. A start that would make a 17th AirPlay 2 session gives Unsupported with the reason "too many AirPlay 2 speakers"; such a speaker waits as P169 says for its own reason: the first speaker refused only for this reason that the host still has starts, through the restart of P142, when an AirPlay 2 session ends for good. The sender still never acts as or names a group leader, never joins a group another sender made and never asks one receiver to pass audio on to another (P171). *Source:* AudioSync design (scope set on 2026-10-24); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (the media server plays to several AirPlay 2 receivers at once, each with a session and a random `groupUUID` of its own, all served by one PTP clock of its own). *Required:* the limit of 16, which bounds the PTP service's traffic and matches the 16 connections of the DACP server (P194).
- **P205** One PTP timeline for every receiver. The PTP service of P158 serves every AirPlay 2 session that uses PTP timing, standalone speakers and target members alike, each as P167 says for one receiver: Announce, Sync and Follow_Up go to that receiver's address only, from the answer to its session SETUP until its session ends, and a Delay_Req from any served receiver's address is answered as P162 says. Every receiver gets the same clock identity (P159) and times from the same clock (P163), so one timeline spans them all; an NTP-timed AirPlay 2 session (P157) and an AirPlay 1 session read the same sender clock (P89), so no receiver's timing protocol moves it off P96. Each receiver has its own sequenceId counter per message type, from 0 when the service starts sending to it, so a receiver that joins sees what a lone receiver sees; its first Announce goes at once and its Sync every 125 ms from then, and no other receiver's schedule moves. The service binds one address family and never moves while a session uses it, as P179 says for a target's members, now for every PTP session: it starts in IPv4 when any receiver about to take PTP has an IPv4 address; a receiver with no address in the family in use is started on NTP with P179's warning, and one that takes only PTP (P157) is refused with Unsupported and the reason "PTP in use on another network", which, unlike P157's final reason timing, is a wait: the speaker waits as one refused under P204's limit does, and starts once no PTP session holds the service, which then binds in its family (P158). *Source:* IEEE 1588-2008 (unicast messages, a sequenceId per message type); [mit-ptp]; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (the media server's PTP clock serves every AirPlay 2 receiver it plays to, one unicast destination per receiver, added when that receiver's SETUP is answered and removed when its session ends); AudioSync design (the counters per receiver).
- **P206** The sender leads every receiver. It is the grandmaster for every receiver it serves and never follows another clock (P158); every receiver gets P160's Announce values. An Announce that arrives on port 320 from a served receiver's address still changes no timing (P160), but when IEEE 1588-2008's dataset comparison ranks its grandmaster ahead of the sender's (priority1, then clockClass, clockAccuracy, offsetScaledLogVariance, priority2 and the identity), the sender gives one warning per session naming the speaker, since that receiver may follow its own clock or another receiver's instead of the sender's. Nothing else changes. *Source:* IEEE 1588-2008 (best master clock algorithm, dataset comparison); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (an open receiver's PTP helper takes timing only from the address of the sender that set up its session, whatever SETPEERS lists). *Required:* the warning; whether Apple's receivers that are named to each other (P207) keep following the sender is checked on hardware (G20).
- **P207** SETPEERS names every receiver on the timeline. Under PTP each session's SETPEERS (P165) lists, in this order: that receiver's address; the address of every other receiver whose session takes PTP timing from the service and has had its RECORD answered, standalone speakers and target members alike, each by the address its session connected to (P164); and the sender's own addresses of the service's family. A receiver is listed from the answer to its RECORD until its session ends: one still pairing, waiting for a PIN or for its RECORD answer is not listed yet and enters through P209's update; a target's member in view but not yet that far is not listed either. NTP-timed AirPlay 2 receivers, a target's member that fell back to NTP (P179, P205) included, and AirPlay 1 receivers are never listed, since they don't take the sender's PTP clock. This is the one rule for every session, a target's members included (P179). Every session keeps its own random `groupUUID` and `groupContainsGroupLeader` false (P164, P179). *Source:* public protocol write-ups (an Apple sender's SETPEERS to one receiver of a group lists that receiver, a second receiver and the sender, in that order); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (the media server's SETPEERS names only the receiver and the sender; an open receiver reads nothing from it, P165). *Required:* every PTP receiver in the list, in the Apple sender's order. Whether Apple's receivers act on the other receivers' addresses is checked on hardware (G20); should naming them pull a receiver off the sender's clock (P206), this clause changes to the single list P165 sends to a lone receiver: that receiver and the sender.
- **P208** A receiver added while others play. An AirPlay 2 session that starts during a stream (a speaker switched on, a target member coming into view, a retry of P142, a restart of P210) runs its own pairing, SETUPs, RECORD and SETPEERS and joins as P103 says, heard in step about L later. Its start never flushes, pauses, sets up again or delays another session, never changes the Lf of a stream that sounds (P201) and never changes another speaker's delay ([engine-spec] S284). Once its RECORD is answered 200 under PTP, every other running PTP session gets the update of P209. A session that fails before that point was never named to the others, so its failure sends nobody an update. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P209** SETPEERS when the timeline changes. When a receiver enters or leaves P207's list (a PTP session's RECORD is answered, or a PTP session ends for any reason), each other running PTP session whose first SETPEERS has been answered gets a new SETPEERS with the list as it now stands, on its own connection and in turn with its other requests. Changes within 1 s of each other go out as one request per session. A missing answer never ends the session (P134 doesn't apply to it); a 2xx answer completes the update, and anything else follows P210. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (SETPEERS is sent whenever the group changes, P106); AudioSync design. *Required:* nothing public shows how a receiver takes a list that changes while it plays (G2), so P210 says what follows a refusal, and what an update does to a receiver's sound is checked on hardware (G20).
- **P210** A refused update. An update of P209 answered with a status other than 2xx, or unanswered after 10 s (P134's wait, which here never ends the session), leaves that session playing with the list it had and gives one warning per session naming the speaker and the status. When the refused update added a receiver and the session's first SETPEERS was answered 2xx (a receiver that takes a list at the start but not a change), the sender sets up that receiver again, alone: TEARDOWN, then a new session whose first SETPEERS names every current receiver and which joins as P208 says. That speaker is silent for about L, and no other session is set up again or flushed: they get P209's update for its leaving and another for its return once its new RECORD is answered, merged into one when both fall within 1 s. A speaker is set up again this way at most once per run, and a later refused update leaves it playing with its old list. An update that only removed a receiver never causes this, since a list that still names a receiver that left changes nothing the sender sends. When the speaker is a target's member, the TEARDOWN opens P181's 10 s window as any TEARDOWN of the sender's own does; that window covers target members only, and a standalone speaker has none. *Source:* AudioSync design (setting up only the affected receiver again is the fallback for G2). *Required:* whether any receiver refuses an update, and whether one that answers 200 plays on in step, is checked on hardware (G20).
- **P211** One receiver failing. A failure of one AirPlay 2 session, at its start or later, belongs to that speaker alone: PasswordRequired, WrongPassword, PairingRequired, PairingFailed, PairingCancelled, Unsupported, Refused, Busy, Unreachable, Timeout and a loss (P134 to P141) are reported for that speaker, its retries follow P142, and every other session plays on with its packets, its PTP messages and its delay untouched. A receiver that asks for a PIN (P46, P61) waits for it on its own connection and is on nobody's list (P207) until its RECORD is answered; the others play on while the user types the code, and the receiver then joins as P208 says. A cancelled or wrong code ends only that session. A session that fails for good frees its place under P204's limit as P169 says for an end for good, and leaves the list as P209 says. A target's member follows P181 within these rules. *Source:* AudioSync design (P3, P141).
- **P212** What receivers settle. Whether Apple's receivers stay on the sender's clock when they are named to each other (P206, P207), how they take a SETPEERS changed while they play (P209, P210, G2), and whether they play in step with each other and with AirPlay 1 or NTP-timed receivers on one timeline (P205) stays open (G20). An open receiver run twice on one computer checks the protocol side and settles nothing for Apple's receivers. *Source:* AudioSync design. *Required:* on hardware, with the AirPlay delay at 0 and the debug log on, two AirPlay 2 receivers that take PTP play at once and are in step with each other, by ear and by microphone against a local output, within calibration's error; a third switched on and off while they play leaves both playing without a gap, with each update's answer in the log; no warning of P206 appears. A failure is recorded with the receiver models before P207 or P210 changes.

## 7. Buffering and latency

- **P109** Receivers buffer about the latency the sender asks for. Classic AirPlay sources set about 2.0 to 2.25 s and AirPlay 2 can go down to about half a second; AirPlay 2 realtime audio plays about two seconds late, while buffered audio starts sooner and may arrive faster than real time. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P110** *Superseded in part by P196 to P203*: the sender now also reads `arrivalToRenderLatencyMs` and reports one R_s per session, and the host sets Lf from it (P200); the crate still never folds a report in by itself. Receivers report their own output latency: in AirPlay 1 as the `Audio-Latency` header of the RECORD response, in frames (captures show 2205 and 3035); in AirPlay 2 as `audioLatencies` entries with `outputLatencyMicros` in `/info` (400 000 in one capture). One older write-up treats `Audio-Latency` from some receivers as unreliable. The two open receivers report 11 025 frames (0.25 s), one as a fixed value and one as a setting that changes nothing in its own playback (P95), and one writes `outputLatencyMicros` as a boolean rather than a number. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* the sender records and reports these values and never folds them into Lf or the timeline; per-speaker compensation stays with the host, through d_s. A value that isn't a non-negative number is reported as unknown.
- **P111** Per stream the sender keeps an input ring of at least 1 s (host to crate) and one payload store that covers the resend history (P88) plus the largest delay (20 s, P99); a speaker with its own encoding (P121) gets its own store of the same size. At 44.1 kHz, 16-bit stereo, 28 s of audio is about 5 MB. All buffers are allocated when the stream starts. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P112** When a packet's send time (which includes the margin M of P84) comes before the host has delivered all its frames, the sender fills the missing frames with silence, sends on time and counts the silent frames; frames for that range that arrive later are dropped, so the timeline never stalls or shifts. When the input ring is full, the host's write keeps what fits, and the dropped frames are counted. The host's first frames are never late: when they arrive after the stream already sent silence for their range (a capture that opens after the stream starts), they start the audio as if they had just arrived on time, and none is dropped. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P113** For each speaker the crate reports the delay from a frame's arrival to its sound, L + d_s, and separately the receiver's reported latency; on request it gives c(n) for any n, so the host can line local outputs up with the AirPlay speakers. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P114** *Superseded in part by [engine-spec] S176, S178, S179 and S180*: the host's local outputs now get L plus a stored adjustment of the user's (0 by default) plus their offset relative to a reference output, which may be negative and isn't held to 0–20 000 ms, and the one-time migration below is replaced by those clauses; the timing contract (c(n), L, re-anchoring) is unchanged. A frame the crate receives at c(n) sounds on speaker s at c(n) + L + d_s (P96). The host lines a local output up with the AirPlay speakers by giving it an effective delay of exactly L + d_local, counted on the sender clock from c(n), so the time a frame spends between capture and the crate is accounted for; d_local is that output's own trim on the same 0–20 000 ms scale as d_s. This is the value the host uses as the effective local delay while an AirPlay speaker is chosen ([engine-spec] S78, S79): under this sender it replaces S78's rule that the effective delay equals the configured delay, and the stored delay setting ([engine-spec] S77, 0–20 000 ms, default 2000) is migrated once to d_local = clamp(configured − L, 0, 20 000) rather than reused as d_local, which would play local outputs about 2 s late. When Lf changes (P98) or the timeline restarts (P143), the crate reports the new L and mapping and the host re-anchors every local output to them. *Source:* AudioSync design; [engine-spec].
- **P115** A speaker's first packet is heard about L − M after it is sent (P84 sends each packet M ahead of its fitted arrival). The crate reports, per speaker, the sender-clock time at which that first packet is due to be heard. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P196** On either path a RECORD answer may carry `Audio-Latency`: a decimal count of frames at the stream's 44 100 Hz. Published captures show 2205 (50 ms) and 3035; one open receiver sends 11 025 (0.25 s) on AirPlay 1 and 0 on AirPlay 2, the other open receiver 11 025 on AirPlay 1. Nothing public says what the value covers or whether a receiver adds it to the time a sync packet states (G9): its name and size fit the receiver's own output delay, not the buffer the sender fills (that is L, P95). One open AirPlay 1 sender takes the larger of this header and its own latency setting as the latency it asks for, and states its times 11 025 frames later still, noting that receivers seem to play everything that much later than stated; a commercial sender only logs the header. The sender reads the header on both paths. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders; observed behavior of a commercial sender (behavior only, team A). *Required:* a value that isn't a decimal integer from 0 to 2^32 − 1 gives no value (P110), and neither does a missing header.
- **P197** On the AirPlay 2 path a `/info` answer (P41) may carry `audioLatencies`, an array of dictionaries with `type` (an integer; one open receiver sends one entry with 100 and one with 101, whose meaning nothing public gives), `audioType` (the string "default" there), `inputLatencyMicros` and `outputLatencyMicros`, both in microseconds. One published capture shows `outputLatencyMicros` 400 000 (0.4 s); that open receiver writes it as the boolean false and `inputLatencyMicros` as 0; the other open receivers send no `audioLatencies` at all. The sender reads it from every `/info` answer of the session, the later answer replacing the earlier, and takes the largest `outputLatencyMicros` among the entries where it is a finite, non-negative integer or real; `inputLatencyMicros` and `type` aren't used. An entry or key of another type is skipped as P41 says; with no usable entry, as with a receiver that writes false or sends no `audioLatencies`, it gives no value and no warning. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* the largest entry, since nothing says which `type` applies to realtime audio.
- **P198** On the AirPlay 2 path the answer to the stream SETUP (P44) may carry `arrivalToRenderLatencyMs`, at the top level or in its `streams` entry: an integer or real number of milliseconds, by its name the time from a packet's arrival to its sound. A commercial sender logs this value from that answer and uses it for nothing else; no open receiver sends it and no write-up describes it. The sender reads it when it is a finite, non-negative number, the `streams` entry winning over the top level, and otherwise it gives no value. *Source:* observed behavior of a commercial sender (behavior only, team A); AudioSync design.
- **P199** A speaker's reported latency R_s for one session is the largest of the values P196, P197 and P198 gave in that session, in microseconds (frames × 1 000 000 / 44 100, rounded; milliseconds × 1000), or 0 when none gave a value, with no source named and no warning: an AirPlay 2 receiver that writes `outputLatencyMicros` as false or sends nothing, or an AirPlay 1 receiver without the header, plays as one that reports 0. The crate reports it with the latency event of P113, which goes once the session has its last report (after the RECORD answer on AirPlay 1, after the stream SETUP answer on AirPlay 2) and before the session's first audio packet, together with which of the three sources gave a value. Each session reports afresh; the crate keeps nothing across sessions. The crate itself never folds R_s into Lf or into the timeline (P110); the host uses it twice, for the shared Lf (P200, P201) and as that speaker's own arrival estimate ([engine-spec] S283). *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P200** The host sets one Lf for the stream, shared by every network speaker in it (P98; every member of every target, P177; [engine-spec] S237): `Lf = clamp(max(77 175, R_max + 22 050), 11 025, 88 200)` frames, where R_max is the largest R_s, in frames, among the speakers in the stream (0 for one that reported nothing). With no report Lf stays 77 175 (1.75 s). The margin of 22 050 frames (0.5 s) covers the send margin M (up to 100 ms, P84) plus room for resend round trips (P88), so a receiver gets at least about R_s + 0.4 s of lead. The default stays the floor: the published capture and a commercial sender use 1.75 s, nothing public shows a receiver playing in step at a smaller L, and a smaller L shrinks the time left for resends on a busy network while the local outputs wait for the network speakers anyway ([engine-spec] S176). When R_max + 22 050 exceeds 88 200, Lf is 88 200 and the host gives one warning per session naming the speaker; the open receivers' buffers end near 2 s (P88), so no larger value would play anyway. Reading R_s as a lower bound covers both readings of G9: a receiver that adds its own latency on top of the stated time loses nothing but a longer wait, and one that needs R_s of buffer gets it. *Source:* observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (one open AirPlay 1 sender raises its latency to the receiver's `Audio-Latency`); public protocol write-ups; AudioSync design. *Required:* the floor, the margin and the clamp. Lf is the only use the crate makes of R_s; the host's per-speaker use ([engine-spec] S283) never changes Lf.
- **P201** The crate takes a new Lf from the host at once only while no speaker of the stream has reached its first-sound time (P115): the speakers already sending get the flush of P98, but none has sounded, so nothing audible is cut and the first sound comes later by the change. Otherwise it keeps the latest value as pending, never changes the Lf of a stream that sounds, and applies the pending value when the stream next has no speaker, at the next timeline restart (P143) or at a resume from an explicit pause (P213), all of which already silence every speaker; the timeline event of P113 reports L whenever Lf changes. A speaker whose R_s would need more than the stream's Lf joins at that Lf, in step with the others (P103), and the crate gives one warning for it per session. Reason: a change of Lf in a stream that sounds silences every speaker for about L (P98, P100) and moves every local output ([engine-spec] S177), so one joining speaker would interrupt the room, which P103 rules out. Before it adds speakers to a stream that has none, the host sets Lf from the R_s it already holds from this run (P200), so a receiver it has seen starts at its own Lf with no change at all. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P202** The crate's interface: the speaker latency event of P113 carries R_s in microseconds (0 with no source when nothing was reported) and its sources; the sender's latency setter takes Lf in frames and applies it as P201 says; and a pure function of the crate computes P200's rule from a list of R_s, so the rule is written and tested once. The C interface mirrors them: the latency event (`ASC_AIRPLAY_EVENT_LATENCY`) gains `receiver_latency_us` (signed 64-bit, R_s in microseconds, 0 when no source gave a value) and `receiver_latency_sources` (a bit set: 1 `Audio-Latency`, 2 `audioLatencies`, 4 `arrivalToRenderLatencyMs`, 0 when none), and keeps `receiver_latency_frames` as the raw header for logs. `asc_airplay_set_latency(airplay, frames)` takes Lf in frames, clamps it to 11 025 to 88 200 and applies it as P201 says; a null handle does nothing. `asc_airplay_session_latency_frames(reported_us, count)` wraps the crate's function for both hosts, over an array of R_s in microseconds where a negative entry counts as 0; a null array or a count of 0 gives 77 175. The shortfall of P201 is a warning event with the speaker's ID and a detail naming R_s and Lf. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P203** What a receiver's reported value means stays open (G9), and so does what a television with AirPlay 2 only reports: its 0.7.0 log said "the receiver reports nothing", since the AirPlay 2 path read no value, and its measured AirPlay offset was 1970 ms on one run and 1981 ms on another at L = 1750 ms, about 220 to 230 ms more than L. *Source:* hardware trace (2026-10-24, the television of P182). *Required:* on hardware, with the AirPlay delay at 0, the debug log's R_s and its source are read and Calibrate All measures the receiver: an excess over L within 30 ms of R_s means the receiver adds its reported latency on top of the stated time, which the host's starting estimate ([engine-spec] S283) already carries; anything else means it doesn't, and calibration corrects the estimate. Either way L follows P200.

## 8. Volume

- **P116** Volume is a SET_PARAMETER with `Content-Type: text/parameters` and the body `volume: <dB>` followed by CRLF. −144 means muted; otherwise the value runs from −30 to 0. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P117** The number is written in fixed point with six decimals ("-11.123877", "-20.000000"), with a period as the decimal separator whatever the system locale, and no exponent. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* six decimals.
- **P118** The host sets a speaker's volume as a dB value from −30 to 0, or mute; values outside the range are clamped and NaN is ignored. How the app's slider maps to dB is the host's choice. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P119** On the AirPlay 1 path the initial volume goes out right after RECORD succeeds, before or together with the first audio; on the AirPlay 2 path P183 places it. AirPlay 2 receivers report their current level as `initialVolume` (−144 to 0) in `/info`. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **P120** At most one volume request is in flight per session; a newer value replaces one still queued; at most 10 volume requests per second go to one session; volume requests go ahead of queued metadata. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P121** A receiver whose RAOP TXT has `sv=true` wants the sender to apply the volume to the samples: the sender multiplies that speaker's samples by 10^(dB/20) (0 when muted) before encoding and sends it no volume requests. `sm=true` asks the same for mute alone. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* such a speaker gets its own encoding outside the shared payloads (P80), with its own resend history (P88) and delay store (P111); this rests on a single source.
- **P122** The sender MAY read a receiver's volume with GET_PARAMETER (`Content-Type: text/parameters`, body `volume`, answer body `volume: <dB>`, possibly after an empty line) to show it to the host; the answer never changes the sender's own state. On the AirPlay 1 path it reads when the host asks, behind a volume set still waiting on P120's rate limit, so the answer is never the level that set replaces; on the AirPlay 2 path it reads as P184 says. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (two open receivers answer it on both paths, one of them with an empty line before the value; no open sender asks for it on the AirPlay 2 path); hardware trace (2026-10-24: a Samsung television with AirPlay 2 only answered it on an AirPlay 2 session with its level in the six-decimal form of P117). *Required:* whether an Apple receiver answers it on an AirPlay 2 session stays a hardware check (G17, G18); P184's stop rule covers one that doesn't.
- **P180** A target has one volume, set by the host as for a speaker (P118). The sender sends it to every member's session as P116, P117 and P120 say, and a member that starts late gets it where P119 and P183 place it on its path. A read-back (P122) asks the leader (P173), or the first member in view when the leader isn't, on both paths; on the AirPlay 2 path only that member's session reads as P184 says, its answers are reported as the target's, and once its reads stop (P184) the target reports none. *Source:* AudioSync design; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (a fork of the media server sends a pair's volume to each member, at the same absolute level). *Required:* whether a volume set on one member moves the other, and whether the leader reports a level the user sets on the other member, are checked on hardware (G18); either way every member gets the same value.
- **P183** On the AirPlay 2 path the initial volume SET_PARAMETER goes out once the FLUSH that follows the stream SETUP (P42) is answered: before any metadata request (P123) and before or together with the first audio. A host value set earlier waits until then, the latest replacing an older one (P120). The body is that of P116 and P117, unchanged. A volume SET_PARAMETER that gets an error status on the AirPlay 2 path gives a warning, at most one per session, and never ends the session or changes its state; the sender sends the latest value once more alongside the next feedback request (P132), so at most 2 s later, and after a second error it sends nothing until the host sets a new value. Before that warning and resend, a volume read (P122) sent right after the error decides, even in a session whose host never asked for reads (P184), which then reports no level and, when its reads stop, gives P184's warning only at the host's first request for a read: a level within 0.5 dB of the value sent means the receiver applied it, and the error gives neither warning nor resend; only another level, a failed read or reads already stopped give them. *Source:* hardware trace (2026-10-25: a Samsung 6 Series UT5500 TV, firmware srcvers 377.25.06, answers 500 with an empty body to a volume SET and applies it, its read-back giving the value on its 0.6 dB grid, -28.110378 read as -28.200001); hardware trace (2026-10-24: a Samsung television with AirPlay 2 only answered the volume SET_PARAMETER sent after RECORD and before the stream SETUP with 500, while the text, artwork and progress SET_PARAMETERs it got after the FLUSH were answered 200); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (the media server's AirPlay 2 output sends the volume last in its start sequence, after the stream SETUP, notes that some speakers ignore a volume sent earlier, and goes on when the volume request fails; [mit-sender] sends it after RECORD and FLUSH, before the audio; both write six decimals; an open AirPlay 2 receiver answers every volume SET_PARAMETER 200 and applies it only to the connection that holds playback). *Required:* this place, which rests on one receiver's answers and two open senders; that it ends the 500 is checked on the same television (G17).
- **P184** On the AirPlay 2 path the sender reads the volume (P122) once after the initial volume of P183 is answered, then, while the stream plays, at most once a second, on a 1 s tick of its own, when the host has asked since the last read ([engine-spec] S271, S291). A read counts as a volume request for P120 and goes behind a waiting volume set. The answer to a read sent before the latest volume set went out is dropped as stale; any other answer with a level is reported to the host as a level the user set on the speaker. An answer with an error status or without a level is a failed read; after three failed reads in a row the session sends no more reads and gives one warning, and a host request for it then reports nothing. A failed read never ends the session, never counts toward P132's loss rule and never changes the sender's state; a read still unanswered at the first read tick at least 1 s after it went out counts as one failed read and never ends the session: P134's close on a missing response doesn't apply to it, and no new read goes out while it waits. *Source:* hardware trace (2026-10-24: the same television answered every read on the AirPlay 2 session, -27.000000, then -26.700001 and on in 0.3 dB steps as its remote moved the level, while its event channel (P70) carried nothing; a read every 2 s caught every change); AudioSync design (three in a row mirrors P132's loss count).

## 9. Metadata and artwork

- **P123** The sender sends only the metadata types a receiver lists: RAOP `md` 0 (text), 1 (artwork), 2 (progress); on the AirPlay 2 path, features bits 17, 15 and 16 (P14), the same three types, whether or not the device also has a `_raop._tcp` record with `md`. On the AirPlay 2 path the requests are those of P124 to P126, with the same content types and `RTP-Info`, where t is an RTP timestamp of that session's audio packets; they go on the session's RTSP connection, framed like every request after verification (P67, P153), and only once the initial volume of P183 has gone out after the FLUSH answer; P127 applies as on AirPlay 1. Features bit 50 doesn't change this: a receiver that sets it together with bit 17, 15 or 16 gets these requests for the bits it sets, and one that sets bit 50 alone gets none (P128). *Source:* public protocol write-ups (`md` 0, 1 and 2 listed as features bits 17, 15 and 16); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (two open receivers that implement AirPlay 2 take these three requests on an AirPlay 2 session through the same handling as on AirPlay 1, one of them ignoring them until an audio stream is set up; the engine an earlier AudioSync ran as a separate process decides on the AirPlay 2 path from features bits 15 to 17 alone and sends its first metadata after the stream SETUP); [mit-sender] (one metadata path for both protocols, after the stream SETUP); hardware trace (2026-10-25: a Samsung television with AirPlay 2 only answered these text, artwork and progress requests of an AirPlay 2 session 200, a 76 775-byte JPEG among them, and showed the title and cover through a 5-minute pause). *Required:* one open receiver notes that an Apple sender sends a receiver that sets bit 50 its metadata only as binary plists; whether an Apple receiver that sets bit 50 shows these requests is checked on hardware (G13, G17).
- **P124** Track information is a SET_PARAMETER with `Content-Type: application/x-dmap-tagged` and `RTP-Info: rtptime=<t>`, where t is the RTP timestamp from which it applies. The body is DMAP: each chunk is a 4-character code, a 4-byte big-endian length of its data, then the data. It holds an `mlit` container with `minm` (title), `asar` (artist) and `asal` (album) as UTF-8 strings. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* the `mlit` container, which the sources don't show for this request.
- **P125** Artwork is a SET_PARAMETER with `Content-Type: image/jpeg`, `RTP-Info: rtptime=<t>` and a JPEG body. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* JPEG only, at most 512 KiB; the host scales or drops larger images. Which image a session sends, and what follows a refusal: P185.
- **P185** Artwork size per path. With each track the host hands the sender two JPEG encodings of the same picture: a *large* one of at most 1024 px on its longest side and 128 KiB, and a *small* one of at most 600 px and 40 KiB (the same bytes for both when the picture fits the small limits). An AirPlay 2 session sends the large one; an AirPlay 1 session sends the small one; each member of a target (P177) follows its own path. When a receiver answers an artwork request with anything but 2xx, or doesn't answer within the sender's usual wait for a request's answer (which then never ends the session as P134 would), the session sends the small one once, with the same `RTP-Info`, unless the refused one was already the small one, and sends only the small one for the rest of the session; a refused small one ends artwork on that session, and the next session starts again from its path's image. A refusal is never Refused or Lost (P138, P141): track information and progress still go, the audio plays on, and the host gets one warning per session (P127). *Source:* public protocol write-ups (an Apple sender's artwork request carries a 34 616-byte JPEG; no size limit is stated); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (an open receiver that serves both paths reserves 256 KiB for a request when cover art is wanted, grows the buffer for a longer body, takes any `image/*` body without a byte or pixel check and answers 200 on both paths; two open receivers of one lineage collect a request body of any length; an open media server sends artwork of at most 600 px a side on both paths, unchanged when it already fits, with no byte limit); public user reports (a 2017 news report and forum thread: AV receivers with a display failed on AirPlay 1 artwork of about 1600 px from a desktop Apple sender, and played once a smaller image replaced it); a public protocol page of an open sender (Apple's remote protocol asks for now-playing artwork at 1024 × 576 for a TV screen). *Required:* no source gives a receiver's limit, and the sender learns nothing about one before it sends, so the sizes are AudioSync's choice: the AirPlay 1 image stays about the size Apple's and the open senders' artwork measures, since the receivers with reported trouble are AirPlay 1 AV receivers, and the AirPlay 2 image is large enough for a TV-sized cover while staying within the buffer an open receiver reserves. A refusal is read as "too large" because size is the one thing the sender can change. A receiver that takes an image and shows it poorly is not detected; whether a TV on the AirPlay 2 path shows the large image sharper is checked on hardware.
- **P126** Progress is a SET_PARAMETER with `Content-Type: text/parameters` and the body `progress: <start>/<current>/<end>`, three absolute RTP timestamps. *Source:* public protocol write-ups. *Required:* AudioSync plays live audio without track bounds, so it sends progress only when the host supplies a track's start and length.
- **P127** Metadata is optional and never delays audio or volume: the host supplies now-playing information when it has some; metadata requests wait while a volume request is pending; a failed metadata request is a warning and never ends the session. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P128** Binary-plist now-playing metadata (features bit 50) is not documented and not sent. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.

### Remote control from the receiver

- **P186** A receiver can send commands back to the sender: the play, pause and track buttons and the volume of its own remote or controls. The sender takes them through DACP on both paths: it announces a command server in its RTSP requests (P22, P187) and over DNS-SD (P188), answers the receiver's HTTP requests (P189 to P192), and hands each accepted command to the host as an event naming the speaker (P193), which the host carries out ([engine-spec] S273 to S279). *Source:* public protocol write-ups (the receiver finds the sender's DACP service and sends it HTTP commands with the session's Active-Remote as the token, no pairing needed); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* no stream SETUP of type 130 ever goes out (P1), so DACP is the only way commands arrive.
- **P187** The DACP ID is chosen once per run of the sender, when the command server first starts: 64 random bits written as 16 uppercase hex digits whose first digit is not 0; Client-Instance carries the same value (P22). Each session gets its own Active-Remote: a random 32-bit number in decimal, different from every Active-Remote the sender issued earlier in the run. Both headers go on every request of both paths, on the AirPlay 2 path from `GET /info` before pairing on, framed requests included. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (a 64-bit DACP ID; Active-Remote as the command token); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (one receiver reads the headers on the AirPlay 1 SETUP and, on the AirPlay 2 path, on `GET /info` and the stream SETUP; another reads them from the first request of a connection that carries them, also when it plays for an AirPlay 2 sender; an open sender sends them on every request and keeps Active-Remote to 32 bits because a speaker it tried took no larger value; one receiver matches the service name to the DACP ID case-sensitively after dropping the name's leading zeros, so an ID that starts with 0 never matches). *Required:* an Active-Remote names exactly one session, and through it one speaker.
- **P188** While the command server runs, the sender registers a DNS-SD service of type `_dacp._tcp`, instance name `iTunes_Ctrl_<DACP ID>`, the server's port, and the TXT keys `txtvers=1`, `Ver=131075`, `DbId=<DACP ID>` and `OSsi=0x1F5`. It registers before the first RTSP request of the session that starts the server and withdraws the record with a goodbye (RFC 6762 §10.1) when the server stops. A failed registration is one warning: the sessions go on and still send the headers. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (service type, name form, the example TXT record); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (a receiver browses `_dacp._tcp` for that name, takes only the port and sends to the address its RTSP connection came from; an open sender records that a speaker ignored its service under any other name); RFC 6763. *Required:* the TXT values are the write-up's example; no source shows a receiver reading them.
- **P189** The command server speaks HTTP/1.1 over TCP. It starts with the first session of the run, on either path, runs while any session runs, and stops 10 s after the last one ends; it keeps its port for the run when that port is still free. A connection may carry several requests one after another and is closed after 5 s idle, after an answer to a request with `Connection: close`, or after a malformed request. Every request is answered within 100 ms of its last byte, before the command takes effect. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (HTTP on the advertised port); observed behavior of open-source receivers (one opens a new connection for each command, sends `GET`, `Host` and `Active-Remote` only, and gives up after 0.5 s for the send and 0.5 s for the answer); AudioSync design for the limits.
- **P190** The server is reachable from the LAN only, and only from the receivers it plays to. It listens on the local addresses of the running sessions' RTSP connections, all on one port, never on a wildcard address: a session on a new local address adds a listener there, and the listener goes when the last session using that address ends; when the port can't be bound on the new address, that session plays without remote control and the host gets one warning. A connection is accepted only from an address discovery reported for a speaker with a running session (for a target, any member); any other is closed before a byte is read. The sender asks no router for a port mapping. IPv4 and IPv6 are treated alike; a link-local IPv6 address keeps its scope. *Source:* AudioSync design; observed behavior of open-source receivers (the command goes to the address the RTSP connection came from).
- **P191** A request is checked in this order. A request line or headers that don't parse, or exceed P194's limits, get 400 and the connection closes. A method other than `GET` gets 405. A missing `Active-Remote`, one that isn't a decimal 32-bit number, or one that names no running session or a session whose speaker doesn't have the connection's peer address gets 403 and no action, and the connection closes; a peer that gets 8 such answers within 5 s of its first has every connection closed unread until those 5 s have passed. A path outside `/ctrl-int/<n>/`, where n is a decimal number (the sources show 1), gets 404. A command of P192 gets `204 No Content`; any other path under `/ctrl-int/<n>/` (`getproperty`, `getspeakers`, `playstatusupdate`, `nowplayingartwork`, the play-queue requests and the rest) gets 400 and no action. Every answer has `Content-Length: 0` and `DAAP-Server: AudioSync/<version>` and no body. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (a command answered with `204 No Content`, `Content-Length: 0` and a `DAAP-Server` header); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (an open sender answers an unknown path or an unknown Active-Remote with 400 and its commands with 204; one receiver reads 400 as "the sender is there but serves no extended control" and keeps sending commands, and polls `getproperty`, `getspeakers` and `playstatusupdate`). *Required:* 403 for a wrong Active-Remote: no receiver of ours sends one, since each session's value reaches only its receiver. The close after a 403 and the cap of 8 per 5 s are AudioSync design.
- **P192** The commands, with the event each gives the host: `playpause` gives PlayPause; `play` and `playresume` give Play; `pause` and `stop` give Pause; `nextitem` gives Next; `previtem` gives Previous; `volumeup` gives VolumeUp; `volumedown` gives VolumeDown; `mutetoggle` gives MuteToggle; `setproperty` with `dmcp.device-volume=<dB>` gives DeviceVolume with that level. `beginff`, `beginrew`, `shuffle_songs` and `setproperty` with any other key (`dmcp.volume`, `dmcp.device-prevent-playback`, `dmcp.device-busy`, `dacp.shufflestate` and the rest) get 204 and no event. The query is split at `&`, each pair at the first `=`, both percent-decoded (RFC 3986 §2.1); keys are case-sensitive, and when `dmcp.device-volume` appears more than once the last counts. Its value is a decimal number with a period: −144 means muted, a value from −30 to 0 is a level, any other number is clamped to −30 to 0 (P118), and one that doesn't parse or isn't finite gives no event, still with 204. *Source:* public protocol write-ups (the thirteen commands); observed behavior of open-source senders (`dmcp.device-volume` as the receiver's own level in dB from −144 to 0; the busy and prevent-playback properties as 0 or 1; a GPL-3.0 sender library documents the same volume form). *Required:* Play, Pause and the volume events are new to the host; nothing about the play queue is served.
- **P193** An accepted command reaches the host as an event on the crate's event channel, in the order requests arrived, carrying the speaker of the session its Active-Remote names (for a member of a target, the target, P176) and the event of P192. It never runs on the audio path (P6). A command whose session ended between the answer and the delivery is dropped. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P194** Limits: at most 16 open connections (a new one beyond that is closed at once); a request line and headers of at most 8 KiB, read within 2 s of the connection opening or of the previous answer; a body (`Content-Length` above 0) of at most 4 KiB, read and ignored, a larger one giving 400 and a close; at most 10 events per second for each speaker, a request beyond that getting 204 and no event. Nothing the server does or receives ends, delays or changes a session or the audio: a bind, accept or registration failure is a warning, an accept error waits 100 ms before the next accept, and a fault while handling a connection closes that connection only. *Source:* AudioSync design (P133, P141).
- **P195** The crate logs one debug line for each request it doesn't hand to the host: the speaker's name, or the peer address when no session matches; the command (the path without its query, plus the `dmcp.device-volume` value when there is one); and the status code. A request handed on is logged by the host, once, with what it did ([engine-spec] S279). Requests over the rate limit are summed into one line per speaker per second. When another device claims the service's instance or host name and the record is announced under a changed name (RFC 6762 §9), the crate logs one line per conflict naming the network interface and whether the host or the service name was claimed, never the names themselves. The DACP ID and Active-Remote values never appear in the log. The counters of P144 gain, for the server: commands accepted, 403 answers, 400 and 404 answers, and requests over the limit. *Source:* AudioSync design.

## 10. Teardown, keep-alive and errors

- **P129** Stopping a speaker: stop its packets, send TEARDOWN (P35), wait at most 2 s for the answer, close the connection. Stopping the stream does this for every speaker in parallel and finishes within 2 s overall. A speaker stopped before its session exists on the receiver just closes the connection, but one stopped while its SETUP (AirPlay 1) or session SETUP (AirPlay 2) waits for an answer waits for it within the same 2 s, because the receiver may already have made that session: an answered SETUP gets the TEARDOWN of that session (on AirPlay 1 with the `Session` the answer gave), a refused or unanswered one closes the connection. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; AudioSync design for the limits and for the SETUP in flight.
- **P130** The crate never ends a session on its own because the audio is silent; the host decides when a speaker stops. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P131** In AirPlay 1 the continuous audio and the receiver's timing requests keep a session alive; no keep-alive request is documented. The sender SHOULD send `OPTIONS *` after 30 s without any RTSP request on the connection, to notice a dead connection early. A session that has received at least one timing request from its receiver (P91: requests from other addresses don't count) and then receives none for 10 s is lost (P141); a session that never received one is judged by its connection alone. The keep-alive checks the connection, not the session's rights: any answer shows the connection alive, and a missing one is a timeout (P134). A 4xx or 5xx answer to it, 403 included, is a warning only, as for SET_PARAMETER (P138): the session goes on, the stored credentials are kept, and it is neither Refused nor PairingRequired; a 401 follows P38 as on any request. The 403 of P46 means a connection that hasn't been verified yet, which can't describe a connection that verified, or needed no verification, and is streaming. If the receiver has in fact dropped the session, the connection closing or the timing requests stopping end it as Lost (P141), and the next connection's verification decides whether the pairing still holds (P55, P58). *Source:* public protocol write-ups; AudioSync design; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (one open sender sends OPTIONS every 25 s while streaming and ignores the answer's status; another keeps the session alive with a progress SET_PARAMETER every 25 s, logs a non-200 answer and goes on, and ends the session only when no answer comes).
- **P132** On the AirPlay 2 path the sender sends `POST /feedback` on the RTSP connection every 2 s for as long as the session lasts; the answer is 200, with a binary plist or with no body, and the sender doesn't read it. One open receiver warns after a few seconds without it and drops the session after 15 s (its default). *Source:* public protocol write-ups; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders. *Required:* the loss rule: three missing or failed answers in a row mean the speaker is lost.
- **P133** Errors are typed events per speaker. None of them panics, ends another session or stops the input. Each carries the RTSP method and status code when there is one. Kinds: Unreachable, Timeout, PasswordRequired, WrongPassword, PairingRequired, PairingFailed, PairingCancelled, ReceiverUnverified, Busy, Refused, Unsupported (with the reason: encryption, codec, transport, methods, format or AirPlay 2 only), ProtocolError, Lost. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P134** A TCP connect that is refused, unreachable or unanswered for 5 s gives Unreachable; a response missing after 10 s gives Timeout and closes the session. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P135** A 401 when the host gave no password gives PasswordRequired; a 401 without `stale=true` to a request that carried `Authorization` with the current nonce gives WrongPassword. Neither is retried automatically. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; RFC 2617; AudioSync design.
- **P136** A 470, or another non-200 answer to a pairing request, gives PairingFailed (P58); a failed verification gives PairingRequired (the stored credentials are no longer accepted, and the host keeps them until a new pairing replaces them, P154), except a receiver signature that fails the check of P56, which gives ReceiverUnverified and leaves the stored credentials in place; in both cases a Busy answer of P137 is Busy instead, and a 401 is a password challenge, which gives PasswordRequired or WrongPassword only when it can't be answered (P58, P135); the receiver closing the connection while the sender waits for a PIN gives PairingCancelled. *Source:* public protocol write-ups; AudioSync design.
- **P137** Flags bit 17 seen before connecting is reported as a busy hint, and the sender still tries. A 453 answer to ANNOUNCE, SETUP or RECORD gives Busy, and so does a 409 answer to any request sent before RECORD has been answered (where the open receiver below sends it), pairing and verification requests included, ahead of P58 and P136, so a busy receiver never makes the sender ask for a new pairing; a 409 after that follows P138. No public write-up documents which status Apple's receivers use while another sender holds them; 453 ("Not Enough Bandwidth" in RFC 2326) is an assumption to confirm on hardware (G8). Of the two open receivers, one answers the first request of a new sender's connection with 409 while another sender is connected (unless its user lets a newcomer take over, in which case the first sender's session is dropped without a TEARDOWN), and the other stops accepting TCP connections once its connection slots are full, which the sender sees as a timeout (P134). *Source:* public protocol write-ups; RFC 2326 §11.3; AudioSync design; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders.
- **P138** Any other 4xx or 5xx answer to OPTIONS, ANNOUNCE, SETUP or RECORD gives Refused, with method and code, and closes the session. Not "other" in this sense, and so not Refused: 401 (P36), the Busy answers of P137, the pairing answers of P46 (a 403 to the OPTIONS that sets up a connection, and an uncredentialed Apple TV's 4xx to ANNOUNCE), and a 501 to OPTIONS, which the sender treats as an answer without a `Public` header (P27). A 4xx or 5xx answer to SET_PARAMETER, GET_PARAMETER or FLUSH, or to the keep-alive OPTIONS of P131 other than a 401 (which follows P38), is a warning only. *Source:* RFC 2326 §7.1; AudioSync design; observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders.
- **P139** Unsupported is reported from discovery or on connect when there is no usable encryption mode (P59), no usable codec (P74), no UDP transport, an OPTIONS list without the needed methods (P27), a format other than 44 100 Hz, 16-bit stereo, or a device without `_raop._tcp` while the AirPlay 2 path is off (reason "AirPlay 2 only", P2; a device whose records only lapsed is unannounced instead, P215). *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P140** A malformed status line or header line (P147), a message over the limits of P146, a wrong CSeq, a missing required header or an unexpected request from the receiver gives ProtocolError and closes the session. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P141** A speaker is lost when its TCP connection closes or resets, when two UDP sends to it in a row fail with an unreachable error, or when the rule in P131 or P132 fires. The session's resources are released and the other speakers keep playing. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P142** While the host still wants a lost speaker, the crate retries after 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 s and then every 30 s, each attempt a complete new session (new identifiers, sequence numbers and timestamps). On success the speaker joins the running timeline (P103) and plays in step with the others. Retries stop on PasswordRequired, WrongPassword, PairingRequired, PairingFailed, ReceiverUnverified and Unsupported, which need the user, except a PairingFailed from a pair-verify error about the receiver's state, which is retried no sooner than its back-off (P154). An Unsupported that only means the speaker stopped announcing itself pauses the retries instead (P215). *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P215** *Added 2026-10-26, a correction to P142.* An unannounced speaker is not an unsupported one. An Unsupported whose only cause is that the speaker's own service record is missing or lapsed (a goodbye or an expired record, P18, for a service the sender saw for that device earlier in this run, or no record of the device in view at all; "this run" means since the sender started, so a discovery stop and start does not forget what was seen and a retry due before the speaker resolves again reports it as unannounced until its record returns) is reported as unannounced: nothing showed that the speaker lacks a capability, only that it stopped announcing itself. While a speaker the host still wants is unannounced, its retry schedule (P142) pauses: the crate makes no attempt and reports nothing further for it, so the host's notice stays "not in view" and never escalates to a failure that needs the user. The first announcement that makes it playable again (a record that, with the others in view, gives it a path under P2) starts a session at once, not at the next back-off slot: the start is a retry that falls due at that moment and takes its turn in the queue of P222, behind any host starts waiting there; that attempt is the first of a fresh schedule, so a failure after it is retried after 1 s. A target whose members are all unannounced is unannounced the same way (P181, P216). An Unsupported with a capability cause keeps P142 as it is, with no retries and the user needed: no usable encryption mode, codec, transport or format (P139), AirPlay 2 only for a device whose `_airplay._tcp` is in view and that was never seen with `_raop._tcp` in this run (P2), the encryption failure of P43, and every other reason of P139; the waits of P169 and P204 stay as they are. *Source:* test-receiver run, 2026-10-26 (an AirPlay 2 receiver whose service records lapsed and came back while this sender wanted it); AudioSync design.
- **P143** *Rewritten 2026-10-26.* A capture switch is an underrun of P112 and nothing more. When the host stops one capture and starts another (a new source, a new channel pair of the input captured, a new capture rate), the timeline, the rate model and the frame count carry on, and the sender sends no session a FLUSH, TEARDOWN, SETUP, pair-verify or `GET /info` for it. While the host rebuilds its capture, the packets whose time comes are filled with silence on the shared timeline (P112). The host marks the start of the new capture on its input; the new capture's first write, when it comes after the stream already sent silence for its positions, is placed as a resumed capture's is (P112): its last frame at the n whose c(n) is its arrival, never before the first frame not yet cut. Its first frame thus follows the silence and sounds on every speaker about as long after the old capture's last frame as the rebuild took, and the crate reports the new mapping (P113) with the timeline not restarted. A new capture rate needs nothing of the crate either: both hosts resample every capture to the stream's 44.1 kHz before it reaches the input. The timeline starts over only when a hole outgrows L: when a packet is due more than L past its send time (the sender couldn't run), the crate starts the timeline over at the host's next frame, resets the rate model and the frame count, applies an Lf held under P201, and reports the new mapping with the timeline restarted. Each speaker that has sent audio gets a FLUSH naming the first packet it has not sent, as at a pause (P213), and no audio of the new timeline goes out to it before that FLUSH is answered, even when the FLUSH waits behind another request on the connection. Reason: a FLUSH and a new timeline left every speaker silent for about L (1.75 s by default, P98) plus the rebuild at each switch, about 2 s, while a track change within one source sends no FLUSH at all. Before 2026-10-26 every capture switch flushed every session and started the timeline over. *Source:* AudioSync design; advisor 2026-10-26 14.02.
- **P144** Counters the host can read without locks: per session, packets sent, retransmission requests received, packets resent, requested packets no longer held, timing requests answered, late packets, silent frames inserted, input frames dropped, datagrams dropped on the control and timing sockets because they came from another address (P86, P91), and the connects the queue of P222 let start with the moment the last of them started; per stream, the current R̂. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P181** A target plays on the members it can reach. A member not in view at start, or lost while playing (P141), is retried as P142 says while the host wants the target, and joins in step when it comes back (P103); the other members keep playing. While a member is missing, or a member in view sets flags bit 14 (its partner unreachable), the crate reports the target as incomplete and names the missing member (a member whose record lapsed: P216). When no member can be reached the target fails as a single speaker would. Within 10 s of the sender's own TEARDOWN to a member, a refused connection, or a transient pair-setup that P150 would map to PairingRequired, counts as a loss of that member and is retried once as P142 says. A member seen in a target is forgotten once it is in view outside that target, which only its `_airplay._tcp` record can show: the keys that place a device in a target (P172 to P175) are in that record, and the `_raop._tcp` record carries none of them (P12). A member in view through `_raop._tcp` alone, its `_airplay._tcp` record having lapsed (P18), is therefore not in view outside the target: it stays a member and is named missing. The two records of one device lapse independently, each with its own goodbye and its own TTL, so either can be the last one left. Without `psgsz`, a member seen once and then gone stays named missing until it is in view again, it is in view outside the target, or discovery stops (as when AudioSync quits); a pair whose members all lapsed stays a target that long too (P216). *Source:* public protocol write-ups (bit 14); observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (a fork of the media server retries pair members that refuse connections or transient pairing for a few seconds after a TEARDOWN); RFC 6762 §10 (each record carries its own TTL, 120 s recommended for an SRV record) and §10.1 (a goodbye names one record); test-receiver run, 2026-10-26 (a pair member's `_raop._tcp` record left while its `_airplay._tcp` record stayed in view, and the member came back with both 13 s later); AudioSync design. *Required:* what the remaining member plays is checked on hardware (G18).
- **P216** *Added 2026-10-26, a correction to P173 and P181.* A pair member whose record lapses stays in its pair. When one member of a stereo pair whose members were in view together in this run has its records lapse first (P18), or only its `_airplay._tcp` record while its `_raop._tcp` record stays (P181), the other member stays a pair with that partner named missing in the incomplete notice of P181, whether or not it sets flags bit 14; it never becomes a single speaker that drops the partner silently. "A single speaker otherwise" in P173 holds only for a device whose partner was never seen with it in this run. When the remaining member's records lapse too, the target stays, both members are named missing, and it is unannounced as P215 says. It stays a pair for the rest of the discovery run: it goes only when one of its members is in view outside it (P181) or discovery stops, never because no member has been in view for some time. Each member that announces again is retried at once (P215) and joins in step (P103). *Source:* test-receiver run, 2026-10-26; RFC 6762 §10 (each record lapses on its own TTL); AudioSync design.

### Several connects at once

- **P222** Connect spacing. The crate starts the connects of new sessions one at a time, in a queue: a connect (the TCP connect of P20, the first step of P26 or P42) starts at the later of the moment its start falls due and 0.5 s after the previous connect started, except that no connect waits more than 10 s after its start fell due. A start that falls due when no connect has started in the last 0.5 s and none is waiting connects at once, so a speaker started alone waits for nothing. Two kinds of start go through the queue. Host starts: a start the host asks for (a user's click, the speakers marked to connect at launch, a zone or a preset turned on), a start handed on by P169 or P204, a target's move to the AirPlay 2 path (P177), and a pairing connection the host asks for outside a session (the legacy pairing of P47 and the PIN pairing of P153), each of which opens a TCP connection. Retries: a retry of P142 or P181 whose time has come, and the start of P215 when an unannounced speaker announces again. Each turn goes to the waiting host start that fell due first, and to a retry only when no host start waits; retries keep the order they fell due in among themselves, and a connect already started is never overtaken. A retry delayed by the queue counts its next back-off of P142 from the moment its connect actually started, not from when it fell due. A session that goes on over a connection already open takes no turn: the session after a PIN pairing of P153 claimed within its 30 s, the case P182 relies on. Stops (P129) never wait and stay parallel. The spacing is between connect starts only: a session that is still connecting (up to P134's 5 s), pairing, waiting for an answer (up to 10 s) or waiting for the user's PIN (P51) holds no later start past its turn. The connect times are a pure function of the waiting starts (their due times and kinds) and the time the last connect started, so the schedule is tested on a fake clock, as the retry schedule of P142 is. *Source:* observed behavior of a commercial sender (behavior only, team A): when several AirPlay speakers connect at once, each connect waits until 0.5 s after the previous one, and none is put more than 10 s ahead; AudioSync design for the two kinds and their order, so a user's click is never queued behind speakers that are retrying. *Required:* the 0.5 s and the 10 s are taken from that sender unchanged. Why it spaces its connects (the receivers, the network or its own pairing work) is not known, and no connect storm has been measured with AudioSync, so the spacing is a bounded precaution: the sixteenth AirPlay 2 speaker of P204 starts its connect 7.5 s after the first, and the 10 s cap first binds on the twenty-second start of one burst, which then connects 10 s after it fell due, together with the twenty-first. A start waiting in the queue counts as started for P169 and P204: it holds its AirPlay 2 place from the moment it falls due, so a later AirPlay 2 start is refused or counted as those clauses say and can't take the place ahead of it.
- **P223** A start never holds up a speaker that plays or connects before it. While later sessions wait their turn, connect, pair (the key exchange of P151 included) or wait for answers, every packet of a session already playing still leaves within the 5 ms of P84, and its sync packets (P93), timing replies (P91) and keep-alives (P131, P132) keep their times. The queue of P222 never delays a start for one of the same kind that fell due after it, nor a host start for a retry: a host start's connect, pairing and first sound (P115) come as they would if no later start had been asked for, and only a retry may wait for a host start that fell due after it. A session that joins does so as P103 says, so nothing already playing is flushed or re-timed by it, and P201 is unchanged (a joiner whose R_s arrives after the stream sounds joins at that stream's Lf). *Source:* AudioSync design (P3: one session's failure never stops another; the send window of P84).
- **P224** What the user sees. A speaker waiting for its turn is reported Connecting (`ASC_AIRPLAY_STATE_CONNECTING`) from the moment its start falls due, the same as a speaker whose connect has begun, so both hosts show it as they already show an AirPlay speaker whose session is starting, with no text of its own. A waiting start reports the path it plans to take, then Connecting, and when its turn comes the path its session actually takes; if the AirPlay 2 place was taken while it waited (P169), the fallback of P214 sends it to AirPlay 1 and that path comes after Connecting. Each speaker sounds as soon as its own session is up, in step with those already playing (P102, P103): the first at its usual time, and the k-th of a burst starts its connect at most 0.5 s × (k − 1) after the first, then takes its own connect and handshake time. A speaker the host removes while it waits gives Stopped and opens no connection; the starts after it keep their order and connect by the same rule, never sooner than 0.5 s after the last connect that started. Stopping the stream drops every waiting start. The hosts don't space their own starts: they ask for every speaker at once (the speakers marked to connect at launch, a zone or a preset turned on) and the crate spaces them, the same on both hosts. *Source:* AudioSync design.
- **P225** The spacing is checked with loopback fake receivers as the target tests do, in six parts, on the connect starts the sender records (P144); each receiver's accept must follow its own start, within the 5 s of P134, since a loaded machine can schedule a fake receiver's accept seconds after the connect. (a) Five fake AirPlay 1 receivers and one fake AirPlay 2 receiver started in one call connect in the order asked, each connect at least 0.5 s and at most 0.75 s after the previous one started; the bound is per turn, not counted from the first, because P222 spaces each connect from the previous connect's actual start, so a turn the machine runs late moves every later turn with it. (b) When the second of them accepts the connection and never answers (a silent receiver, timing out under P134), the third still connects in its turn. (c) A receiver that already plays when the six are started receives, through the whole burst, every audio packet within 100 ms of the one before it, keeps its stated times within one frame of P96 for its delay (P102), and gets no FLUSH, SETUP or TEARDOWN. (d) With P169 in force, three fake AirPlay 1 receivers and then two fake AirPlay 2 receivers started in one call: the first AirPlay 2 speaker is reported Connecting at once and connects in its turn, the fourth, 0.5 s to 0.75 s after the third; the second gets Unsupported ("one AirPlay 2 speaker at a time") while the first still waits, and opens no connection. (e) Three playing fake receivers that close their connections together are retried after 1 s (P142); 0.2 s after those retries fall due, while two of them still wait, the host starts a fourth receiver: it connects at the next turn, ahead of the two waiting retries, which then connect 0.5 s apart in their order. (f) On a fake clock: starts due at 0, 0, 0, 0.2 and 3 s connect at 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 3.0 s; 22 starts due at 0 put the twenty-first and the twenty-second at 10 s; a host start due after three waiting retries takes the next turn and the retries keep their order; a retry whose connect the queue delayed, failing again, gets its next back-off counted from its actual connect; a start removed from the queue opens no connection and the next one connects 0.5 s after the last connect that started; a single start connects at once; a session that goes on over an open pairing connection takes no turn. *Source:* AudioSync design. *Required:* the tolerances: none under the spacing, since every connect start comes from the sender's own clock; 250 ms over it is half a turn, so a connect can't pass for the next turn; 100 ms is the cap on M (P84), so a longer gap would use up a receiver's whole safety margin.

## 11. Gaps

What a sender needs that no public source covered when this was written. Each gap names the clauses it blocks and what would close it.

The two open-source receivers studied for the revision settled points inside clauses rather than gaps: the FLUSH reading (P34), SRP padding (P50), the receiver's answers in Apple TV pairing (P53, P56, P58), the timing reply window (P91), and how the AirPlay 2 session SETUP names the timing port (P43); two open senders settled the connection order for a device that requires verification (P26, P46). The receivers narrow G8, G9 and G11 and close none: neither implements SETPEERS, PTP, buffered audio, HomeKit pairing or the AirPlay 2 packet encryption, and both take audio only with a FairPlay-wrapped key. For the AirPlay 2 revision a third open receiver (with HomeKit pairing, AirPlay 2 packet encryption, PTP timing through a helper, and buffered audio), the AirPlay 2 output of open senders, and two MIT-licensed libraries ([mit-sender], [mit-ptp]) were read; they close G3, G4, G6 and G7, narrow G1, and add G17 for what only a real receiver can show.

- **G1 PTP group timing.** Narrowed by P157 to P167, which say what a sender does for one receiver: NTP first, PTP when a receiver takes nothing else, with the sender as unicast grandmaster on domain 0 sending Announce, Sync and Follow_Up and answering Delay_Req, and 0xD7 time-announce packets in place of sync packets. Still unknown: which device Apple's senders let become grandmaster in a group, which is out of scope (P2), since the sender leads every receiver itself (P205, P206), and what Apple's receivers make of a peer list that names other receivers (G20); the meaning of the Signaling messages an open sender sends with Apple's organization 00-0D-93 (subtypes 1 and 5) and notes as used by iOS, which this sender leaves out until a receiver shows it needs them; and the 11 025-frame shift between how an open sender writes and an open receiver reads the time-announce packet (P166). The last two go to the hardware checks (G17).
- **G2 SETPEERS.** Only the address list is documented (P106), and the sender sends it only under PTP (P165). Unknown: what the extended message (features bit 52) adds, whether the sender's addresses must come first or last, and what a receiver does when the list changes mid-stream. None of the open receivers studied acts on it (two answer 501 or 200 and ignore it, a third answers 200 and reads nothing), so a receiver can take audio without it. Parked: the extended message's body and a list changed mid-stream stay open until a capture of an Apple sender is public, since a receiver alone can't show them; one open AirPlay 2 sender that plays to HomePods sends no SETPEERS of its own and puts its peers only in the session SETUP timing keys (P43). A public features list names bit 52 without describing it. For several receivers at once, P209 sends a list changed mid-stream and P210 says what follows a refusal; how receivers take it is part of G20. *Source:* public protocol write-ups.
- **G3 Buffered-audio SETUP.** Closed by P168: buffered audio is out of scope, and the clause records what an open receiver expects. Its TCP framing and its AAC format stay undocumented here, since the sender never uses them.
- **G4 HomeKit pairing details.** Closed by P61, P63 to P66 and P149 to P154: user name, transient flag, headers, M5 and M6, pair-verify labels, nonces and signed data, and the error values now rest on an MIT-licensed sender and open receivers and senders. Still out of reach: a speaker the Home app restricts to people sharing the home, which needs a home identity this sender doesn't have; such a speaker refuses the pairing and is reported as P150 maps the refusal. How Apple's and licensed receivers use the error values is checked on hardware (G17).
- **G5 Apple TV Companion pairing.** Parked: the protocol documentation of [mit-sender] describes Companion, with its own HomeKit-style PIN pairing and OPACK frames, as covering apps, remote buttons, touch and power, and ties audio streaming to an Apple TV to AirPlay pairing alone; no public sender streams audio over Companion. The PIN an Apple TV asks for during AirPlay is the AirPlay one (P46, P61), so the sender needs no Companion pairing; an Apple TV that plays after AirPlay PIN pairing in the G17 session confirms it.
- **G6 Encrypted framing details.** Closed by P67 to P69 and P153: an MIT-licensed sender uses the inferred nonce layout and additional data with AirPlay 2 receivers, and frames from the request after the pairing's last answer. The first session with a real receiver confirms it (G17).
- **G7 AirPlay 2 audio packet trailer.** Closed by P155 and P156: two open senders and an open receiver put the tag before the 8-byte nonce and fill the 12-byte nonce with four leading zero bytes, the reverse of the one diagram P71 rested on. The first session with a real receiver confirms it (G17).
- **G8 Busy and access statuses.** No public write-up documents the status code Apple's receivers use for "another sender is playing" (P137) or for a speaker restricted to people sharing a home. Two open receivers show the range: a 409 to a second sender's first request, or no longer accepting connections. Hardware tests settle the mapping for Apple's receivers.
- **G9 Receivers' reading of sync packets.** That receivers play the frame in bytes 4–7 at the time in bytes 8–15 (P95) follows from field names and descriptions, not from a statement by Apple. One open receiver does exactly this and adds nothing for its reported `Audio-Latency`; the other ignores sync packets. Whether Apple's receivers add their own `Audio-Latency` on top is still not documented. A two-speaker ear test with a known delay settles it, and the host's per-speaker delay covers any constant offset meanwhile.
- **G10 PCM over AirPlay 1.** The exact SDP for PCM (P30) isn't shown by any capture, and one write-up sets the packet's payload type to 10 (the static L16 stereo type of RFC 3551) for raw PCM instead of 96. ALAC is the default (P74); PCM, when the host enables it, uses 96 with `L16/44100/2` and falls back to ALAC if refused. Hardware tests decide whether PCM can become the default.
- **G11 Sender names.** No source says whether receivers check `User-Agent` or the SDP `o=` and `s=` names (P5). One sender's changelog records switching to an AirPlay-style user agent for Apple TVs and HomePods, which suggests some receivers look at it. Of the two open receivers, one reads `User-Agent` only to spot one old Windows sender, whose audio key it then treats differently, and the other ignores it; neither rejects an unknown name. If hardware tests confirm that, using such a token is a decision for the maintainer, since it would mean presenting as another sender.
- **G12 Features bits 26 and 30.** The two community sources give different meanings (P14); no decision rests on them alone.
- **G13 Binary-plist metadata.** Features bit 50 metadata (P128) has no public format. Whether a receiver that sets bit 50 next to bits 15 to 17 shows the requests of P123 on the AirPlay 2 path is part of G17.
- **G14 TCP-only receivers.** A receiver whose `tp` lists only TCP needs RTP over TCP, whose framing for AirPlay 1 isn't documented in these sources; such receivers are unsupported.
- **G15 RSA receivers.** The RSA mode (P59) needs a receiver public key that no allowed source publishes; such receivers are unsupported by design.
- **G16 IPv6 request URIs.** The one IPv6 capture writes the address without brackets in the URI (P23); RFC 3986 would bracket it. The sender prefers IPv4 (P17); the IPv6 form is checked on hardware.
- **G17 AirPlay 2 checks on a real receiver.** The AirPlay 2 path rests on open implementations, not on Apple. One session with a licensed AirPlay 2 receiver settles, before the path's switch (P2) can default to on: whether it takes "NTP" timing or needs PTP (P157); which status flags it sets for each of its code or password settings, and whether a transient pairing works (P61, P150); the request headers of P149; the framing (P67 to P69) and the audio sealing (P155, P156), which are confirmed when it plays; whether it wants `ekey` and `eiv` (P43); under PTP, whether it follows the sender as grandmaster (P160) and where the 0.25 s of P166 goes; whether it shows the now-playing requests of P123, bit 50 set or not (G13); whether it takes the initial volume where P183 places it and answers the reads of P184; and, by ear or measurement against a local output, the constant offset of its sound from P96, which the host's per-speaker delay then covers. When all of these pass, the last step is to flip the switch's default to on. A Samsung television with AirPlay 2 only (2026-10-24, debug trace) answered the volume read of P122 on an AirPlay 2 session and refused a volume set sent before the stream SETUP with 500, which P183 and P184 now follow; on 2026-10-25 it showed the title and cover the requests of P123 carried on an AirPlay 2 session. It settles none of these points for Apple's receivers.
- **G18 Stereo pairs and home theater groups.** P171 to P181 rest on public user reports and open senders, not on Apple, and no pair was at hand when they were written. A HomePod stereo pair, and where one is available an Apple TV with a pair as its default audio output, settles: whether a session to each member plays stereo with each member on its own channel, over AirPlay 1 and over AirPlay 2 (P177, P178), checked with a test signal that has sound in the left channel only; whether a session to the leader alone plays on both members, which would let P177 address one member; what the remaining member plays while the other is unplugged, and which flags it then sets (P178, P181); whether the members stay in step with each other under NTP, by microphone against a local output with a known delay (P179), and whether a shared `groupUUID` or the other member's address in SETPEERS changes that under PTP; whether a volume set on one member moves the other (P180), whether the leader answers the reads of P184 on the AirPlay 2 path, and whether its answer follows a level the user sets on the other member; what a session to the Apple TV of a home theater group plays, and whether it disturbs its speakers' sessions (P177); whether the middle field of `gid` always marks the left member, which would let the host label the sides (P172); and whether a speaker group kept in the Home app advertises any key (P175).
- **G19 Which receivers send DACP commands.** The open AirPlay 2 receiver studied reads the remote-control headers on both paths and sends its commands through DACP, and a second open receiver hands them to remote-control apps; a GPL-3.0 sender library's documentation says HomePods and Apple TVs send DACP commands when used on the device, without saying what was tested. No source covers a licensed AirPlay 2 television such as a Samsung TV, and Apple's own receivers may use the MediaRemote stream instead (the open receiver logs a stream SETUP of type 130 marked remote control only and does nothing more with it). A Samsung TV traced on 2026-10-24 applied its remote's volume presses itself, which the read-back of P184 and [engine-spec] S271 catches whether or not the TV also sends DACP. Closed on hardware by `hardware_dacp_receivers`: with that TV and, where available, a HomePod or Apple TV playing, press play/pause, next, previous and volume on the device's own remote and read which requests arrive (P195).
- **G20 Several AirPlay 2 receivers.** P204 to P212 rest on IEEE 1588, one Apple sender's SETPEERS in a write-up and the media server, which leads several receivers from one PTP clock but names none of them to another; no two AirPlay 2 receivers were at hand. Two or more receivers that take PTP (HomePods or Apple TVs) settle: whether each keeps following the sender's clock when the others are named in its SETPEERS (P206, P207); whether a SETPEERS changed while it plays is answered 200, refused, or changes what it plays (P209, P210, G2); and whether they play in step with each other and with an AirPlay 1 or NTP-timed receiver (P205). Closed on hardware by `hardware_several_ap2_receivers`.

The clauses rest on public protocol write-ups, project documentation pages, Apple's public documentation, the observed behavior of open-source receivers and senders (restated as behavior, no code taken), and the RFCs below. A clause that cites none of these is AudioSync's own design. The detailed list of sources is kept with the project's clean-room records.

- [mit-sender]: an open-source Python client library for Apple TV and AirPlay devices (MIT); its source, mainly the HomeKit pairing, the encrypted channels and the AirPlay 2 audio path, and its protocol documentation pages. Name, URL and version are kept with the project's clean-room records.
- [mit-ptp]: an open-source PTP library for AirPlay 2 senders (MIT), kept in its own MIT-licensed directory of a GPL repository; read for its behavior. Name, URL and version are kept with the project's clean-room records.

- Open-source receivers and senders read for behavior only, no code taken (cited as observed behavior): for the AirPlay 2 revision, an AirPlay 2 receiver under an MIT-style license, its PTP helper (GPL-2.0-or-later), and the AirPlay 2 output and pairing code of a media server (GPL-2.0-or-later, its pairing directory MIT). For the receiver-group revision (P171 to P181), also a GPL-2.0 fork of that media server that drives stereo pairs and the speakers behind an Apple TV, read for behavior only.

- Public user reports: issue threads of open-source sender projects in which users posted the TXT records of their stereo pairs and home theater groups and said what played. Restated as facts; no text reused. The threads are listed with the project's clean-room records.
