# AudioSync engine specification

A behavioral specification of AudioSync's audio engine: capture, equalizer, the shared delay buffer, per-output delay and drift correction, local output rendering, volume, the PCM feed into the AirPlay engine, and the control policies around them. It is written so the engine can be reimplemented (for example in Rust) by someone who reads only this document.

It describes AudioSync 0.4.1 plus the unreleased changes on `main` as of 2026-10-18. It was written from observable behavior only: the public docs (`README.md`, `docs/DEVELOPMENT.md`, `docs/BENCHMARKS.md` for the diagnostic counters, `CHANGELOG.md`, `SECURITY.md`, `THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.md`), the names, values and assertions of the test suites (`Tests/logictest.swift`, `Tests/fifotest.swift`, `Tests/enginetest.swift`, and the printed checks of the ring buffer suite), black-box runs of the delay reader and the FIFO feed through the same calls those suites make (only their output was read), AudioSync's own policy sources outside the core audio modules (`Volume.swift`, `Equalizer.swift`, `AudioSource.swift`, `CaptureCheck.swift`, `SpeakerState.swift`, `SpeakerLayout.swift`, `AirPlayReconcile.swift`, `AirPlayPairing.swift`, `EngineLifecycle.swift`, `AirPlayEngine.swift`, `AirPlayEngineConfig.swift`, the engine's client and player sources, `PipeMetadata.swift`, `Notices.swift`, `main.swift`, the `AppDelegate*.swift` files, `EqualizerWindow.swift`), and Apple's public Core Audio documentation. The implementation of the capture, ring buffer, renderer, sync engine, FIFO feed, device and diagnostics modules was not consulted.

Since 2026-10-19 the Mac app has one audio engine, the Rust core in `core/`: the Swift engine it replaced (`RingBuffer.swift`, `FifoFeed.swift`, the equalizer's filters in `Equalizer.swift`, the Swift paths of `OutputRenderer.swift` and `SyncEngine.swift`, the sync rule in `SpeakerState.swift`) and its suites (`Tests/ringtest.swift`, `Tests/paritytest.swift`, and the logic suite's checks of those files) are gone. Citations of them below record the evidence each clause was written from, at the 2026-10-18 snapshot; every clause they back still stands and is now covered by the core, whose tests name the clauses they cover (`core/tests/spec_coverage.rs`). Those clauses are marked *Now covered by the core.*

Since 2026-10-20 the Mac app has no bundled AirPlay engine: AudioSync's own sender in `airplay/` (specified in `docs/AIRPLAY_SPEC.md`) is its only AirPlay path. The engine's host code (`AirPlayEngine.swift`, `AirPlayEngineConfig.swift`, `AirPlayImpl.swift`, `AirPlayReconcile.swift`, `EngineLifecycle.swift`, the engine's client, output list and player sources, `PipeMetadata.swift`) and its checks (`Tests/pipestart.swift`, and the logic and fifo suites' checks of the engine) are gone. Clauses that describe only that engine (its process, config file, HTTP API, pipe playback and metadata, the reconcile of its output list, its volume writes and pairing stages, its quit sequence) are marked *Retired with the bundled engine*, or *Retired in part* where some of the clause still stands: they keep their IDs and text as a record and bind nothing, and what the AirPlay sender does instead is in `docs/AIRPLAY_SPEC.md`. Core tests named some of them at first (the core's policy modules for that engine); those went later that day (below). Elsewhere, citations of the deleted files record the evidence a clause was written from, at the 2026-10-18 snapshot.

Later on 2026-10-20 the core's PCM pipe feed went too: once the bundled engine was gone, only the soak harness and the fifo suite still ran it. Its code (`core/src/fifo.rs`, the feed's C interface, `CoreFifoFeed` in `CoreEngine.swift`) and its checks are gone, and so is the rest of the core that served only the bundled engine: its policy modules (`policy::engine`, `policy::reconcile`, `policy::track_info`, and `policy::pairing` but for the drop reasons, which `policy::session` keeps for S136), its volume helpers (`engine_volume`, `adopt_reported_volume`, `LatestValues`, S72 to S74), the session rules' pipe playback step and pipe and engine incidents, and the sync rule's C calls `asc_sync_decide` and `asc_sync_effective_delay_ms`, which no host called. Elsewhere, mentions of the FIFO feed, its queue, its writer thread and the pipe rate in standing clauses (S4, S6, S7, S126, S132 and S186, for instance) are a record; where one names the FIFO feed as the AirPlay leg's input, AudioSync's own sender's input (`NativeAirPlayTap` on the Mac) takes its place.

## Conventions

- **S1** The words MUST, MUST NOT, SHOULD and MAY are normative. Every clause carries a tag; a few carry both, with the Observed part citing its evidence. **Observed** clauses state behavior shown by a test, a doc or an allowed source (cited as `file:line`, a test check name in quotes, or a doc section). **Required** clauses are design requirements where the allowed sources do not show the behavior; an implementation MUST meet them, and where a Required clause gives a concrete algorithm or number, it is the reference choice that satisfies every Observed clause.
- **S2** *Required.* A *frame* is one sample per channel at a given rate. The internal pipeline format is interleaved stereo, 32-bit IEEE float, channel order L, R, nominal range [−1.0, +1.0]. Frame indices are signed 64-bit integers counted from 0 at the start of a session. *Observed:* stereo interleaved float buffers of `frames × 2` (`logictest.swift:164-166`, `fifotest.swift:21-22`, `enginetest.swift:22-23`).
- **S3** *Observed* (`logictest.swift:234`). A delay of `ms` milliseconds at rate `R` is `floor(ms × R / 1000)` frames (2000 ms at 48 kHz = 96 000 frames; 1820 ms = 87 360 frames). *Now covered by the core.*
- **S4** *Required.* Rates: the *capture rate* Rc is the rate of the captured stream for the whole session; the *device rate* is an output device's nominal rate; the *pipe rate* is fixed at 48 000 Hz (S83). Delays, ring indices and lag are always in Rc frames.
- **S5** *Required.* A *session* is the span from a successful engine `start` to the matching `stop`. Every session has its own ring history, reader positions and frame counter.

Clause IDs are assigned in order of addition and never renumbered or reused, so a clause added later sits in the section it belongs to and IDs can appear out of numeric order.

A clause that a later one replaces keeps its ID and its text and starts with *Superseded by Sn*, or *Superseded in part by Sn* followed by the part, so the history stays readable. An implementation follows the newer clause; until it does, the superseded clause still describes the code. Once nothing in the code follows a superseded clause any more, its tests move to the clause that replaced it, and a superseded clause that no test covers is listed in `core/tests/spec_exempt.txt` as superseded.

## 1. Data flow

- **S6** *Observed* (`README.md` "How it works, briefly"; `docs/DEVELOPMENT.md` "Where things are"). Components: one capture stage, one 10-band equalizer, one shared delay ring buffer, one delay reader plus one output renderer per enabled local output, and one FIFO feed that writes 48 kHz PCM16 into a named pipe read by the bundled AirPlay engine, which speaks AirPlay and AirPlay 2 to the speakers.
- **S7** *Observed* (`Equalizer.swift:4-5`, `README.md`). Each captured block is equalized once, in place; the same equalized samples are (a) written to the ring buffer and (b) fed to the FIFO feed. Every output, local and AirPlay, therefore hears identical audio. *Required:* (a) and (b) see bit-identical input; their order within one capture callback is free. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S8** *Observed* (`README.md` intro and "Using it"; `SpeakerState.swift:45-48`). The AirPlay path gets no delay from AudioSync. AirPlay speakers play about two seconds late because of the AirPlay engine's buffer; local outputs are delayed to line up with them.
- **S9** *Observed* (`README.md`; ring suite "F readers independent (different positions)"). Each local output reads the shared buffer through its own reader, at its own delay, with its own drift correction.
- **S10** *Observed* (`AudioSource.swift:16-18`, `:233-252`). The engine never captures its own output: the system source excludes AudioSync's own process, otherwise the delayed local copies would be captured again and echo.

## 2. Capture

- **S11** *Observed* (`AudioSource.swift:15-23`, `:206-285`; tests "processes: …"). Three source kinds. `system`: every process's audio except AudioSync's own. `app(bundleID)`: every Core Audio process object whose PID is the app's PID or whose responsible PID (the process macOS holds responsible, e.g. a WebKit GPU helper's app) is the app's PID, AudioSync's own process always excepted (S10). Without macOS's responsibility lookup only the app's own process matches, and the debug log says so once. `input(uid)`: an input device, by Core Audio device UID. *Required:* the app's process list is resolved at session start and SHOULD be refreshed when the app starts new audio processes.
- **S12** *Observed* (`AudioSource.swift:25-30`, `:43-73`, `:97-192`; tests "source: …", "source apps: …"). Persisted form: `""` = system, `"input:<uid>"` = input device, anything else = an app bundle ID; a missing value means system. Only `app` has a bundle ID. The source picker offers the running regular apps that have a bundle ID, never AudioSync itself, one entry per bundle ID (the first instance's), sorted by name case-insensitively with the bundle ID breaking ties. An app shows the first non-blank, trimmed name of: the running app's name, the installed app's Finder name without a trailing ".app", the bundle ID.
- **S13** *Observed* (`README.md` "How it works, briefly"). System and app sources are captured with a Core Audio process tap that also mutes the tapped processes' original output, so only AudioSync's synced copies are heard. Input devices are read directly.
- **S14** *Observed* (`README.md` "Multi-room audio on macOS"). An input device contributes its first two channels as L and R. *Required:* a one-channel input is copied to both L and R; channels beyond the second are ignored.
- **S15** *Observed* (`AppDelegate+Engine.swift:21-40`). An input source needs the microphone permission: authorized → start; not determined → request, then start or fail; denied or restricted → the start fails with a notice.
- **S16** *Required.* The capture stage delivers interleaved stereo float32 at Rc in blocks of any size ≥ 1 frame. Rc is read at session start: the nominal rate of the device the capture IOProc runs on (the input device, or the tap's private aggregate device), else the tap's own format rate when the device's can't be read, else 48 000 Hz when neither gives a rate above 0. Rates from 8 000 to 384 000 Hz MUST be accepted (the suites exercise 32 000, 44 100, 48 000, 96 000 and size a ring for 192 000).
- **S17** *Observed* (`CaptureCheck.swift:5-9`; `AppDelegate+Engine.swift:120-121`). A process tap delivers no frames until its source actually plays; "no frames yet" is normal. `framesCaptured` is the number of frames written to the ring in the current session and restarts at 0 with each session.
- **S18** *Observed* (`CaptureCheck.swift:18-30`; `AppDelegate+Engine.swift:125-150`; tests "capture: …"). Capture verdict: `framesCaptured > 0` → *flowing*; otherwise, if the source is known to have been playing for at least 6 s (`playingGrace`) → *notWorking*; otherwise → *waiting*. The host checks 3 s after a start, then every 2 s until audio flows or the session ends, posting a notice only when the verdict changes; a paused or unknown source never yields *notWorking*.
- **S19** *Observed* (`AppDelegate+Engine.swift:155-165`). "Known playing" comes only from a live Now Playing reading (Spotify, Music): for the system source any playing player counts; for an app source only that app; for an input source never.
- **S20** *Observed* (`AppDelegate.swift:267-274`; `AppLifecycle.swift:49`). When the capture format changes under a running session, the engine reports an interruption `formatChanged` on the main thread, and the host restarts the session (stop, then start, automatic origin) at the new rate.
- **S157** *Observed* (`CaptureCheck.swift:33-43`; tests "EC4 rate: …"). Capture-rate decision: a rate reported for the running capture rebuilds the session (S20) only when it is a real rate (greater than 0) that differs from the session's Rc, lower or higher (48 000 → 44 100 and 44 100 → 96 000 both rebuild); the same rate, or one that couldn't be read (reported as 0), keeps the session playing. *Required:* the engine reports `interrupted(formatChanged)` only when this rule asks for a rebuild.
- **S242** *Required.* Placeholder, not a rule: the number is taken by the multi-app source clause on another branch, whose text replaces this line when it merges.
- **S244** *Required.* Placeholder, not a rule: the number is taken by the live input clause on another branch, whose text replaces this line when it merges.
- **S21** *Observed* (`AppDelegate.swift:297-302`). When capture fails for good, the engine reports `sourceLost(error)` on the main thread; the host stops the session and posts a "source" notice. Stopping ends every AirPlay speaker's session, but the speakers the user chose stay chosen (`AppDelegate.swift`, `.sourceLost`, and `AppDelegate+Engine.swift`, `stopEngine`; Windows: `windows/src/audio/controller.rs`, `StopReason::CaptureFailed`). Before 2026-10-21 this clause said every AirPlay speaker was deselected, which the bundled engine's host did and neither app has done since that engine went (2026-10-20).

## 3. Equalizer

- **S22** *Observed* (`Equalizer.swift:12`). Ten bands, centered at 32, 64, 125, 250, 500, 1000, 2000, 4000, 8000 and 16 000 Hz (band indices 0–9).
- **S23** *Observed* (`Equalizer.swift:13`; `EqualizerWindow.swift:96`, `:107-118`, `:169`). Band gains and the preamp range from −12 to +12 dB in 0.5 dB steps (the host quantizes and clamps). *Required:* the DSP applies any finite value it is given.
- **S24** *Observed* (`Equalizer.swift:15-19`; `AppSettings.swift:40`). Settings: `enabled` (default false), `preamp` in dB (default 0), `gains[10]` in dB (default all 0). The host persists them as JSON.
- **S25** *Observed* (`Equalizer.swift:106-122`). Each band is an RBJ-cookbook peaking biquad with Q = 1.41 (about one octave). With fs = Rc, G = band gain in dB and f = min(center, 0.45 × fs):
  `A = 10^(G/40)`, `w0 = 2π f / fs`, `alpha = sin(w0) / (2 × 1.41)`, `a0 = 1 + alpha/A`;
  `b0 = (1 + alpha·A)/a0`, `b1 = −2·cos(w0)/a0`, `b2 = (1 − alpha·A)/a0`, `a1 = −2·cos(w0)/a0`, `a2 = (1 − alpha/A)/a0`. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S26** *Observed* (`Equalizer.swift:149-169`). Processing, per channel (L and R keep separate filter state) and per sample, in float64: `x = in × 10^(preamp/20)`; then the 10 bands in series in ascending frequency, each in transposed direct form II (`y = b0·x + z1; z1 = b1·x − a1·y + z2; z2 = b2·x − a2·y`); the output sample is `clamp(y, −1, +1)` stored as float32. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S27** *Observed* (`Equalizer.swift:140-143`; "eq: disabled is bypassed"). Disabled, the buffer is left untouched: no preamp, no filtering, no clamping. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S28** *Observed* (`Equalizer.swift:144-148`). On the first enabled block after being disabled, all filter state is zeroed first (stale state would click). *Now covered by the core.*
- **S29** *Observed* (`Equalizer.swift:161-168`; "eq: recovers from a NaN sample (silence stays silent)"). If a channel's cascade output is NaN or ±infinity, that channel's state in all ten bands is zeroed and the output sample is 0. Silence that follows a NaN stays exactly 0. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S30** *Observed* (`Equalizer.swift:65`, `:88-95`). The design rate defaults to 48 000 Hz. On a rate change (only while capture is stopped) the coefficients are recomputed for the new rate and all state is zeroed; setting the same rate again is a no-op. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S31** *Observed* (`Equalizer.swift:7-9`, `:49-58`, `:97-104`, `:128-137`). A settings change takes effect at a block boundary, atomically for all ten bands, the preamp and the enabled flag together (two coefficient sets, the control thread fills the idle one and flips an atomic index; the audio thread publishes which set it is reading so the control side never rewrites a set in use). Filter state is kept across coefficient changes. The audio thread never waits; the control thread MAY yield briefly until the audio thread leaves the set it needs. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S32** *Observed* (tests "eq: …", sine at amplitude 0.25, RMS over the settled second half of 1 s, 512-frame blocks). Enabled and flat: 0 ± 0.05 dB at 1 kHz. Band 5 (1 kHz) at +6 dB: +6 ± 0.2 dB at 1 kHz at 48 kHz (measured 6.00) and at 44.1 kHz; 0 ± 0.5 dB at 8 kHz (measured 0.04). Bass Boost: more than +5 dB at 40 Hz (measured 6.26). Preamp −6 dB with flat bands: −6 ± 0.1 dB. Disabled: 0 ± 0.01 dB.
- **S33** *Observed* (`Equalizer.swift:22-45`, `:187-189`; "eq: every preset has 10 bands", "eq: one preset per id, in menu order"). Presets, in menu order, as band gains in dB from 32 Hz to 16 kHz. A preset sets the gains only; the settings "match" a preset when all ten gains are exactly equal.

## 4. Delay ring buffer

- **S35** *Observed* (`docs/DEVELOPMENT.md`; `enginetest.swift:16`; ring suite "F …"). One ring per engine, holding interleaved stereo float32 frames, with a capacity C fixed at creation. Exactly one writer (the capture thread) and any number of readers. *Required:* the writer never waits for or checks on readers; once full, each write overwrites the oldest frames.
- **S36** *Observed* (`logictest.swift:219`, `enginetest.swift:16`). The suites create rings of 24 s of audio (48 000 × 24 = 1 152 000 frames; 192 000 × 24 = 4 608 000 frames). *Required:* C ≥ 24 s × Rc, which covers the 20 s maximum delay (S77) plus headroom for blocks and drift correction. Since Rc is known only when capture starts, the ring is either allocated per session once Rc is known (C = 24 s × Rc), or sized for the highest supported rate; a 4 608 000-frame ring (about 37 MB) covers Rc up to 192 kHz, and higher capture rates are then rejected at session start: the core makes no reader or renderer for a capture rate whose 24 s the ring can't hold, so each local output fails to start and says so instead of playing with its delay cut short. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S37** *Observed* (`logictest.swift:243`). `framesWritten` is the total number of frames written since the last reset, a monotonically increasing 64-bit count (W below). *Required:* it is published only after the frames it counts are in the buffer (release store), and readers load it with acquire ordering. After publishing W and before storing any sample of the next block, the writer issues a release fence, so a reader that sees a sample of a later block also sees a W at least as large as that block's start (S40). *Now covered by the core.*
- **S38** *Required.* `write(interleaved, frames)` accepts any count; when `frames > C` only the last C frames are kept, and W still grows by `frames`.
- **S39** *Observed* (`logictest.swift:241-243`, "ring: reset clears history"; `AppDelegate+Engine.swift:120-121`). `reset()` sets W to 0 and makes all history unreadable. Every session starts with a reset, so a new session never plays the previous session's audio. *Required:* reset is called only while no writer is running and no reader exists. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S40** *Required.* Frame k is readable iff `max(0, W − C + M) ≤ k < W`, where M is a safety margin at least the largest block the writer can write in one call (M ≥ 8192 frames). The writer may be storing up to M frames beyond W at any moment, into the slots of frames `[W − C, W − C + M)`. After copying, a reader issues an acquire fence, re-loads W as W2, and keeps the copy only if its lowest copied index is at least `W2 − C + M`; otherwise the copied frames MUST be replaced by silence and the read handled as an overrun (S55). A reader paused mid-copy for a long time therefore never plays audio from a later block.

## 5. Delay reader

- **S41** *Observed* (`logictest.swift:220-237`). Reader interface: `read(into: dst, frames: N, delayFrames: D, toleranceFrames: T)` fills `dst` with exactly N stereo frames; `requestResync()` asks the reader to re-anchor to the requested delay, which the next `read` with unread frames does (S46). A reader is bound to one ring. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S42** *Observed* (`logictest.swift:238`, lag of the block's last frame is `max(D, 512) − 512`; ring suite "B delay=0 not silent (floored to one block)", "B delay=0 near-live, not silent — measured=0"). The effective delay is `De = max(D, N)`: in steady state the first frame of each block is exactly De frames behind W as loaded at the start of the read. A delay shorter than one block is floored to one block. *Required:* De is also capped at `C − M − N`, and a renderer's reader is floored at the renderer's lead too (S206). Every local output converts, at ratio 1 at the capture rate (S85), so its floor is S206's converting lead and never one block alone. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S43** *Required.* Reader state: `P`, the index of the next frame to read (initially 0); `F`, one past the highest frame index ever emitted (initially 0); `anchored` (initially false); an atomic resync flag; atomic `D` written by the control thread.
- **S44** *Required* (normative algorithm; it satisfies every Observed clause in this section). One `read(N, D, T)`:
  1. `W = framesWritten` (acquire); `De = min(max(D, N, Lr) + Jr, C − M − N)`, where Lr is the lead the reader's renderer gives it (S206) and Jr how late its callback came (S209), each 0 for a reader without one; `oldest = max(0, W − C + M)`.
  2. Anchor or correct. The first of these cases that applies decides:
     - *Prime*, when not anchored (the first read, or the first after `resetPrime`, S159): if `W ≤ De`, output N frames of silence, publish `lagFrames = W − P` and end the read, leaving P, F and the resync flag as they are (S45). Else `P = W − De`, `anchored = true`, and clear the resync flag.
     - *Dry*, when `P ≥ W` (P never passes W, so the reader has reached W and the writer has written nothing since): output N frames of silence, publish `lagFrames = 0` and end the read; this is a starved render of N frames (S158). Nothing else changes: no drift correction is made, whatever D is, and a set resync flag stays set for the first read that finds `P < W` (S46).
     - *Resync*, when the resync flag is set (cleared atomically by this read): `P = W − De`.
     - *Drift*, otherwise, with `e = (W − P) − De`: if `P < oldest` while `oldest > 0`, or `e > T`: `P = W − De` (skip forward; a negative P left by a resync early in a session is not an overrun, its frames are masked in step 3). Else if `e < −T`: `P = W − De` (step back), also while W is not moving (S53).
  3. Emit, for i in 0..<N with k = P + i: if `k ≥ W`, output silence for this and every remaining frame of the block and stop advancing; else if `k < F` or `k < oldest` (this includes k < 0), output silence (masked); else copy frame k.
  4. `P = min(P + N, max(P, W))`; `F = max(F, highest copied index + 1)`; validate the copy (S40); publish `lagFrames = W − P` (S56).
  In steady state (no resync, |e| ≤ T, no underrun) this emits frames P … P+N−1 contiguously, with no gap or repeat.
- **S45** *Observed* (ring suite "A delay=4096 not silent", "B delay=0 not silent"; black-box runs of the delay reader). A new reader anchors (primes) on its first read that finds `W > De`, at `W − De` (S44 step 2). Added to a running session that has written more than De frames, it does so on its first read and plays at once at its exact delay. Before that (a fresh session, or a delay longer than the history so far), each read outputs N frames of silence, counts nothing (S158) and publishes `lagFrames = W − P`, which is W while P is still 0; the read that primes starts at frame `W − De ≥ 1`, and the frames before it are never played by this reader. With N = 512, De = 1200 and one 512-frame block written before each read, the reads at W = 512 and 1024 are silent and the read at W = 1536 plays frames 336 … 847, after which `lagFrames = 688`; at W = De exactly a read is still silent.
- **S46** *Observed* ("delay: change to 500 / 3000 / 1820 / 0 ms is exact"; ring suite "L change while frozen: applied after resume"; "J up-then-down: lands on the new delay"; black-box runs of the delay reader). A delay change (new D plus `requestResync`) is applied by the first `read` after it that finds the reader anchored with unread frames (`P < W`, the *Resync* case of S44 step 2), however long until that read happens. That read anchors exactly at `W − De`, so with the writer writing a block before each read the steady-state delay (S42) equals the new De with zero error, not merely within tolerance, and `lagFrames` settles at exactly `De − N` (S56). A newer change supersedes an unfinished older one, also one still waiting on a dry reader, which is then never applied (one resync is counted, for the newer change). A read that primes (S45, S159) uses the current D and clears the resync flag. *Required* (S44 step 2), when the change arrives while the writer has stopped:
  - *The reader is dry* (`P = W`: it reads at the floor `De = N`, as in L, or reached W during the stall, as it always does when `De ≤ T`, S53). The change waits. Each read meanwhile outputs N frames of silence, counts a starved render and leaves P, F and the resync flag as they are, with no drift correction even when the new De is more than T away. The first read after the writer writes again applies the change against that read's W: `P = W − De`. It outputs silence for the frames below F (masked, `De − (W − F)` of them when that is positive) and then continues contiguously with frame F, the first frame it never played; when `W − De > F` it skips forward to frame `W − De` instead. From then on, with a block written before each read, `lagFrames = De − N` exactly: the change is exact, nothing is repeated, and the only silence besides the starved renders is the masked range.
  - *The reader still has unread frames* (`P < W`, for example on the first read after the writer stops whenever the old De is above N). The read applies the change at once against the frozen W, and the reads that follow are an ordinary writer stall from that anchor (S53): each moves P on by N frames (masked below F, buffered frames above it) while W stands still, a step back re-anchors at `W − De` when the error passes −T, and a reader that reaches W is dry. When the writer resumes, the reader keeps the error the stall left; it is inside the dead band, so it is never corrected (S50), and the change lands short: by j × N frames after j more stalled reads without a step back (a step back starts the count again), or by `De − N` (so `lagFrames = 0`) once the reader ran dry. The change is exact in this case only if the writer writes again before the reader's next read.
  - *L, worked through* (N = 512, T = 2400). The ring is filled with 48 000 frames, then 50 times 512 frames are written and 512 read at D = 0. The first read primes at P = 48 000 and every read ends at W, so after the 50th read W = P = F = 73 600 and `lagFrames = 0`. The writer freezes. The 5 reads at D = 0 are dry. After `requestResync`, the 5 reads at D = 1200 are dry as well, so the change waits: 10 starved renders (5120 frames) in all, no resync, no correction, `lagFrames = 0`. The writer resumes: with W = 74 112 the next read finds P < W and applies the change, P = 74 112 − 1200 = 72 912; all 512 frames are below F, so the block is silent, P = 73 424, `lagFrames = 688`, one resync. The next block (W = 74 624, e = 0) holds 176 masked frames (73 424 … 73 599), then frames 73 600 … 73 935. From then on every block is contiguous and the lag, W − 1 minus the index of the last frame read, is exactly 1200 − 512 = 688: 688 frames of silence after the resume, no frame repeated or skipped, no drift correction. With a change to 24 000 frames instead, the first read after the resume anchors at 74 112 − 24 000 = 50 112; 23 488 masked frames follow, audio returns with frame 73 600 in the 46th block, and the lag is exactly 23 488, again with one resync and no drift correction. A reader that took either change during the freeze would anchor at 73 600 − De and play its masked range toward W while frozen; with 1200 it reaches W and resumes with e = 512 − 1200 = −688, inside the dead band, so its lag stays 0 and the change is lost.
  - *A change on the first read after the writer stops*, worked through: steady at D = 4096 (W = 150 400, P = F = 146 816, `lagFrames = 3584`), the writer stops and the next read comes with `requestResync` and D = 5296. P < W, so that read anchors at once at 150 400 − 5296 = 145 104 (a masked block, `lagFrames = 4784`, one resync). Two more reads with the writer stopped move P to 146 640 (`lagFrames` 4272, then 3760). On the first read after the writer resumes (W = 150 912), e = 4272 − 5296 = −1024, inside the dead band: the reader plays 176 more masked frames and then frame 146 816 onward, and `lagFrames` stays 3760, 1024 short of 4784. Had the writer resumed before the second of those reads, the lag would be exactly 4784.
- **S47** *Observed* (ring suite "E increase: never replays audio", "E increase: delay 24000 reached"; comment at `logictest.swift:237`, "let an increase finish its silence"). A delay increase by Δ frames inserts exactly Δ frames of silence (the masked range of S44 step 3), then playback continues contiguously from the frame after the last one emitted. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S48** *Observed* (ring suite "E decrease: applied on the next block"). A delay decrease by Δ frames is applied on the next block by skipping Δ frames forward.
- **S49** *Observed* (ring suite "E increase: never replays audio", "C no replay loop (stays silent once drained)", "D plays FRESH frames (not replayed old)", "G gap: no replayed audio", "H stall: never replays audio", "I faster output: never replays audio", "I slower output: never replays audio", "J up-then-down: never replays audio"). Invariant: over a reader's life, the indices of the frames it emits are strictly increasing. No delay change, drift correction, underrun, stall or overrun ever repeats audio that was already played.
- **S50** *Observed* (`logictest.swift:227`, T = 1680 at 48 kHz; ring suite "K after a stall, drift is still held within ~35 ms"). Drift tolerance (dead band): T = 50 ms, i.e. 2400 frames at 48 kHz. *Required:* at other rates `T = round(0.050 × Rc)`. While |e| ≤ T nothing is corrected. T was 35 ms (1680 frames, the value the ring suite ran with) until S207 trimmed every converting output whatever its hold-back; since drift that is trimmed never reaches T, a correction is left to a stall, a re-time or a clock the trim does not follow, and a wider T means fewer of them. The price: an output that is not trimmed (one without the capture's clock stamps, S207) drifts up to 50 ms off before it is corrected, and two outputs can sit that far apart meanwhile. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S51** *Observed* (ring suite "H stall: resumes after the last frame it had", "H stall: delay restored — measured=23488", "I slower output: lag stays within tolerance"). When the reader is behind by more than T (output clock slower than the capture clock, a stalled output callback, or a long freeze), it skips forward to exactly `W − De`. The first frame after the skip is later than the last frame emitted before it.
- **S52** *Observed* (ring suite "I faster output: lag stays within tolerance", "M stall above the dead band: silence held, no underrun"); *Required* mechanism (S44 step 2). When the reader is ahead by more than T (output clock faster than the capture clock, or a writer stall, S53), it steps back to exactly `W − De`; the frames it already played are masked, so the correction is |e| frames of silence, never a repeat. A dry reader (at W, S44 step 2) makes no correction until the writer writes again (black-box runs: with De = 2048 and the writer stopped, the reader reaches W after three blocks and then outputs starved blocks without a step back).
- **S53** *Observed* (ring suite "C underrun eventually emits silence", "C no replay loop (stays silent once drained)", "M stall above the dead band: silence held, no underrun", which prints 25 drift silences totalling 51 200 frames and no starved render, and "M stall inside the dead band counts underruns", which prints 19 starved renders). Writer stall: when the writer stops, the reader plays buffered frames only while its error stays within the dead band. Once it is more than T ahead of its target, it steps back to `W − De` (S52) although W does not move, and again each time it gets more than T ahead, so it holds silence instead of draining the buffer; with `De > T + N` it never reaches W. A reader that reaches W first (always when `De ≤ T`) underruns: it emits every frame up to W and then silence for the rest of the block (a starved render, S158); P never passes W, and every later block is silent while W does not move. No frame is repeated either way (S49). A device clock that runs faster than the capture's moves a reader toward W the same way, only slowly (about 5 frames a second at 100 ppm), and at `De ≤ T` no step back comes first either; a converting renderer then keeps its reader off W by slowing its converter instead (S206; with clock stamps, at any hold-back, S207), by at most a sixteenth of a callback's reads, which never outpaces a stopped writer: a stall runs such a reader dry as described here.
- **S54** *Observed* (ring suite "D resumes with audio", "D plays FRESH frames (not replayed old) — first=29696 maxOld=13311", "D delay re-established ≈ 4096 — measured=3584"). When the writer resumes after a stall, the reader gets back to its delay without repeating anything. In D, a block played after the resume starts at frame 29 696, past the last frame written before the stall (13 311), and the lag is exactly `De − N` (3584 for De = 4096). *Required* (S44): a reader more than T behind by its next read skips forward to `W − De` (S51); a reader that was holding silence (S53) plays out the rest of its masked range and then continues with the first frame it never played; a reader that ran dry plays the new frames as they arrive; in the last two cases its error stays within the dead band until a correction or resync.
- **S55** *Required.* Overrun: if `P < oldest` (the reader fell more than the ring's history behind, e.g. after a very long stall), the frames it needed are gone; it skips forward as in S51.
- **S56** *Observed* (`enginetest.swift:55-59`, "Lag ≈ delay − one device buffer"; `logictest.swift:223-229`). After each read the reader publishes `lagFrames = W − P` (W from the start of the read, P after it). In steady state `lagFrames = De − N`. It is an atomic 64-bit value readable from any thread. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S57** *Observed* (ring suite, "G gap: delay restored to 1 s (within the dead band) — measured=45952" for a 48 000-frame delay; "I faster output … worst error=2192 frames", "I slower output … worst error=1168 frames", "K … worst error=2192 frames", all with 512-frame blocks at 48 kHz). Acceptance: the worst deviation of the measured lag from `De − N` is at most T + N (2192 = 1680 + 512 at the suite's T; 2912 = 2400 + 512 today) under clock drift in either direction and after a stall; a writer gap shorter than T leaves a residual error inside the dead band (45 952 vs 47 488 exact).
- **S226** *Required* (AudioSync design; `core/tests/drift_hour.rs`, "with stamps a fast speaker clock is trimmed within a millisecond all hour" and "a speaker clock 3 ppm fast loses 11 ms of lag in an hour without a correction"). Clock trim at any delay. A constant offset between the capture's clock and an output's clock is followed continuously, not left to walk across the dead band until a correction at T (S50 to S52). The trim is S207's: every local output on both hosts renders through a converting renderer (at ratio 1 when the rates match) that gets the time of each callback, and the capture stamps its blocks, so the trim runs wherever a local output plays, from the first callback, with no switch of its own. It measures the fill `Ŵ − P` against the reference taken after the reader anchors, and moves the converter by whole capture frames, as S207 says.
  - *Bound.* At most a 256th of a callback's reads per callback (S207), not a fixed rate such as ±20 ppm. A cap at 20 ppm would leave an output on a clock 100 ppm off drifting by the other 80 ppm until a correction at T, and an output held back by less than its lead would run dry as in S53, which S206 and S207 exist to prevent.
  - *Accuracy.* The trim acts only once its running average is more than 15 frames off the reference (S207's dead zone, which filters the hosts' wake jitter), so a clock that drifts one way holds the output near the edge of that zone, about 0.3 ms off, not within a frame. In the test, an output held back by 2 s on a clock 3 or 100 ppm fast, for a simulated hour: with blocks stamped at the exact times, every callback's timing error (the capture's position at the callback's time less the reader's position) stays within 16 frames; stamped at a producer's 2 ms wakes, within 48 frames (1 ms; 35 frames at 3 ppm, 32 at 100 ppm). Either way the converter is trimmed one way only, by what the clock gained within 48 frames (505 of 518 frames at 3 ppm with wake stamps), no drift correction is made and no read runs dry, and an output on the capture's clock is never trimmed. Without stamps the same output loses 10.8 ms of lag in the hour, inside the dead band, with no correction.
  - *With the dead band.* The dead band stays as the safety net: a stall, a re-time, a clock past the trim's bound or an output without stamps is still corrected at T (S51 to S53, S57). Any anchor (a prime, a resync for a delay change, a drift correction) restarts the tracking, which measures a new reference before it trims again (S207, "When it restarts"), so a correction never fights the trim.
  - *Field check.* A 60-minute soak on the Mac at the longest delay, with the drift under 1 ms throughout, is still to confirm the figures on real clocks.
- **S58** *Observed* (ring suite "F reader1 contiguous", "F reader2 contiguous", "F reader1 delay ≈ 4096", "F reader2 delay ≈ 12000", "F readers independent (different positions)"). Readers share no state except the ring: each has its own P, F, delay and corrections, and any number of them can read the same ring at different delays.
- **S59** *Required.* N may differ from call to call (device buffer sizes change); every rule above uses the N of the current call.
- **S158** *Observed* (`docs/BENCHMARKS.md`, counter definitions; ring suite "M steady state: primed once, no corrections, no underruns", "M delay change counts one resync, not a drift correction", "M lag too long counts a skip", "M stall above the dead band: silence held, no underrun", "M stall inside the dead band counts underruns", and the `Counters(…)` values they print; black-box runs of the delay reader). Each reader keeps read-only diagnostic counters, read together as `counters`: `primes`, anchors made because the reader was not anchored (its first read, S45, or the first after `resetPrime`, S159); `resyncs`, anchors that applied a `requestResync` (S46); `driftSkips` and `driftSkipFrames`, skip-forward corrections (S51, S55) and the frames they skipped (e per correction); `driftSilences` and `driftSilenceFrames`, step-back corrections (S52) and the frames of silence they insert (|e| per correction); `starvedRenders` and `starvedFrames`, reads that reached W before their block was full (S53) and the frames of silence that followed in those blocks. Printed values, with the suite's 512-frame blocks and T = 1680: a steady run counts one prime and nothing else; a delay change with a resync counts one resync and no drift correction; a lag 4488 frames too long counts one skip of 4488 frames; the stall above the dead band counts 25 drift silences totalling 51 200 frames (under S44 each comes when the error first passes −T, at −2048 with 512-frame blocks; at today's T of 2400 the same stall counts 20, at −2560, still 51 200 frames) and no starved render; the stall inside the dead band counts 19 starved renders and 9728 frames (19 whole blocks). *Required:* each counter is a monotonic 64-bit count over the reader's life, incremented only inside `read` with relaxed atomic adds (S126, S131) and readable from any thread without blocking the reader; a snapshot need not be consistent across counters. Masked silence (S44 step 3, `k < F` or `k < oldest`) is not starvation. A dry read (S44 step 2) is a starved render of N frames, and the silent reads of a reader waiting to prime (S45) count nothing. A resync is counted by the read that applies it, so a change that waits on a dry reader (S46) is counted after the writer resumes: L counts 10 starved renders (5120 frames) during the freeze, then one resync and no drift correction. An anchor is never also counted as a drift correction, and a read that finds the reader both unanchored and asked to resync counts only the prime.
- **S159** *Observed* (ring suite "M counters survive resetPrime"; black-box runs of the delay reader). A reader also offers `resetPrime()`, and its counters (S158) survive the call. *Required:* `resetPrime()` returns the reader to its initial state (S43: not anchored, F = 0) except for the counters and P, which keeps its value and only feeds `lagFrames` (`W − P`) until the next prime, so the next read that finds `W > De` anchors at `W − De` like a new reader (S45) and counts a prime. That read primes whether or not the writer is running, since only a resync waits on a dry reader (S44 step 2): after `requestResync` with D = 1200 on a dry reader whose writer has stopped, a `resetPrime` makes the next read prime at once at `W − 1200` and count only the prime; the reads after it are the second case of S46 (two more reads with the writer stopped reach W, and the reader resumes with `lagFrames = 0`). It may be called from any thread: like `requestResync`, it sets an atomic flag that the next `read` takes. Because F restarts as well, the no-repeat invariant of S49 holds from the latest prime on: frames played before a `resetPrime` can be played again after it.

## 6. Local output renderer

- **S60** *Observed* (`AppDelegate+Outputs.swift:197-231`; `logictest.swift:89-91`; `enginetest.swift:38-39`). Output devices are enumerated with Core Audio object ID, name, UID and transport type. One renderer runs per local output that is enabled and present, keyed by device UID and bound to that device's object ID, never to "the default output". The capture stage's own private aggregate device (if any) has a fixed name and is excluded from the output list (`AppDelegate+Outputs.swift:170-172`).
- **S61** *Observed* (`enginetest.swift:42-62`). Renderer interface: `start(on: device, captureRate, delayMs)` (throws when the device can't be started), `halt()`, `setGain(linear)`, `setDelay(ms)`, and atomic counters `framesRendered` (device frames delivered so far) and `lagFrames` (S56).
- **S62** *Required.* The renderer reads from its reader in Rc frames. It converts Rc → the device's nominal rate with a continuous-phase resampler, at ratio 1 when the two are equal (S85), sizing each reader request from the converter's needs; delay and lag stay defined in Rc frames.
- **S63** *Required.* Channel mapping: L to the device's first output channel, R to the second, any further channels zero; on a one-channel device, (L + R) / 2.
- **S64** *Observed* (`enginetest.swift:2-3`, `:43-46`, every output at gain 0 is silent). Every output frame is multiplied by the renderer's linear gain. *Required:* gain 0 yields exact zeros; a gain set before `start` applies from the first callback; a gain change applies from the next callback (a ramp of at most one callback is allowed).
- **S65** *Required.* Any NaN or ±infinity reaching the renderer's output stage is replaced by 0 before it is handed to the device.
- **S66** *Superseded in part by S187*: a running output hands a new delay to its reader after a fade-out, up to 20 ms plus one device buffer later than its next callback. *Observed* (`enginetest.swift:56-60`, "<device>: delay 2000 / 500 / 3000 / 150 ms"). `setDelay(ms)` stores the new delay (converted per S3 at Rc) and requests a resync (S46); it applies on the next device callback, or, while capture is stalled and the reader has reached W, on the first callback after capture writes again (S46). On real hardware, 0.4 s after each live change the measured lag (`lagFrames / 48000` s) is within 60 ms of the target (the lag is the delay minus one device buffer, ± one 10 ms producer chunk).
- **S67** *Observed* (`enginetest.swift:51-53`, "<device>: renders audio"). A started renderer renders more than 24 000 frames in its first second (the device's IO cycle runs continuously, even at gain 0 and even while the reader outputs silence).
- **S68** *Observed* (`AppDelegate.swift:258-264`, `:292-299`; `AppDelegate+Outputs.swift:197-225`). When a device disappears or its IO stops for good, its renderer stops and the engine reports `onOutputGone(uid)` on the main thread. The UID stays enabled. The host lists the local devices again at once (S101), so a device that lingers in Core Audio's list without being alive leaves the speaker list, and the reconcile that finds the device listed again (Core Audio's device-list notice, or the 30 s safety net, S101) adds the output again, with its current delay and gain.
- **S69** *Observed* (`AppDelegate+Outputs.swift`, `syncLocalOutputs`; `SpeakerState.swift`, `LocalOutputSync.apply` and `report`; tests "S69 start failure: …"). If adding an output fails, the host removes the UID from the enabled set (so no later reconcile retries it), posts one notice per failed speaker (plus an alert when the user caused it), and stops the session if nothing else is enabled.
- **S206** *Required* (the Mac's debug logs of 2026-10-20: every session on a Bluetooth speaker running at 44.1 kHz at hold-back 0, 26 of 26, counted 1 to 14 starved renders of 3 to 501 frames right after the output started and none afterwards, a 5.5-minute session as many as a 2-second one, while the 48 kHz outputs counted none; core tests "render start: …"). Device callbacks against the capture's blocks. A renderer gives its reader a lead Lr in Rc frames, recomputed at the start of every callback, which S44 step 1 uses as a floor of the effective delay: `De = min(max(D, N, Lr), C − M − N)`.
  - *The callback it counts.* Each callback, the renderer counts Q device frames: this callback's frames, or the device's steady callback when that is larger. The steady callback is the device's period when the host gives it: a Windows endpoint's default period (`GetDevicePeriod`) in frames, rounded up, which each wake refills when it is on time. A wake that comes late finds the device's buffer emptier and refills more, but the capture has written as much more in the meantime, so only the callback in which the reader anchors needs room for the larger fill, and the next one is back at the period. When the host gives no period (the Mac, whose IO cycle asks for the same buffer every time), the steady callback is the largest of the last 8 callbacks, this one included. One large callback therefore raises Lr for itself with a period given and for 8 callbacks without one, never for good.
  - *At the capture rate* the renderer converts at ratio 1 (S85), so the rule with conversion below applies: with 512-frame callbacks fed 512-frame blocks, Lr = 512 + 512 frames, about 21 ms. (Before, such an output had no converter and Lr = Q, S42's floor of one read: on a clock of its own that ran fast it ran dry each time its clock had gained a block, and neither the slow-down below nor S207's trim could reach it.)
  - *With conversion*, Lr is the bound on the capture frames Q device frames can ask the reader for (the frames they span, plus one step and the rounding, the bound S62's sizing stays within) plus B, the capture's block. A block is what one capture callback or packet writes: the Mac writes each IO cycle's buffer as one; the Windows capture writes a packet in pieces (conversion chunks of 1024 frames, then what the resampler hands out) that the ring counts as one block, and the silence it writes while a loopback stream delivers nothing (a gap fill) as a block of its own. B is the largest of the writer's last 8 blocks, the open one included, so a short write (a gap fill's tail, or what a gap fill left of a packet) never cuts the lead short, and at most 25 ms of capture frames, so silence written at once after a stall never raises it by seconds; 25 ms is more than a 10 ms packet or a 1024-frame Mac buffer, about the first silence a Windows gap writes, and well inside the 50 ms dead band. The ring publishes B with each write (S38), as a relaxed atomic the readers load (S190). A converting output's callbacks never line up with the capture's blocks: when a callback begins, the next block may be almost a whole block from being written, and the callback reads what it needs at once. At the floor of one read such an output ran dry after every start, a few frames to most of a block at a time, until the silence of those starved renders had pushed it back by about a block; each one closed and reopened it (S184), which was heard as a crackle at every switch-on. With 512-frame callbacks at 44.1 kHz fed from 48 kHz in 512-frame blocks, Lr = 563 + 512 = 1075 frames, about 22 ms; with a Windows endpoint's 10 ms periods at 44.1 kHz and 480-frame packets, Lr = 485 + 480 = 965 frames, about 20 ms.
  - The lead is a floor of the effective delay, never added to it. S208 gives every reader its hold-back plus a session lead that covers its steady Lr, so the floor bites only in a callback larger than the steady one. (Before S208 an output at a hold-back below Lr, the earliest one without AirPlay, played Lr behind W while the others played their hold-backs, so they sounded that lead, about 22 ms behind a 44.1 kHz Bluetooth speaker, less than the difference of the offsets after it.) A change of Lr moves no reader by itself: Lr moves by at most a few periods or 25 ms of block, which the dead band absorbs (S50), and a late wake's larger Lr comes with W as much further ahead; the next prime, resync or correction lands on the current Lr.
  - *A fast device clock.* The lead is for the start and for the two clocks' phase. A device whose clock runs fast still moves its reader toward W by the difference, about 5 frames a second at 100 ppm, and at a hold-back inside the dead band (the lead is always below T) no step back comes before its reader reaches W again, once the lead's slack is used up (S53); the slack is a few frames to most of a block, by the phase at the start. In the core's runs with a clock 100 ppm fast, a 44.1 kHz output at hold-back 0 ran dry 2.5 s after its start at the worst phase, and again every time the clock had gained a few more frames, each time closing and reopening it (S184). A converting renderer therefore slows its converter just enough to keep its reader off W:
    - At the start of every callback, after taking the control's requests (S190), the renderer asks its reader how many frames it holds for a read that goes on from where the last one ended, `W − P`. The reader answers only when its next read would do that: it is anchored, no prime reset, resync or overrun waits, and when that read may correct drift (S188), its error is inside the dead band. Otherwise the read anchors at `W − De` or finds the reader dry, and nothing is slowed.
    - When the reader holds fewer frames than the converter needs for the callback's device frames, and the shortage is at most a sixteenth of that need, the converter's position falls behind its course by the shortage, in capture frames, spread evenly over the steps between the callback's frames. The callback then asks for no more than the reader holds, so none of its reads runs dry. A larger shortage is left as it is: the reader runs dry as S53 says, which is what a stopped writer does after its first callback.
    - Over that callback the converter runs slower by the shortage against the callback's reads, a sixteenth at most (about a semitone for some 10 ms), and the frames between its usual positions are interpolated with the same kernel at their exact positions. From then on the output plays the shortage later. Nothing is masked, repeated or faded, and the reader counts no correction; the renderer counts the capture frames its converter was slowed by, and the shortages it made up on a counter of their own, apart from S207's trims. The AirPlay sender's input converter (S85) is never slowed.
    - With a clock 100 ppm fast this happens at the low points of the callbacks' phase against the capture's blocks, by as much at a time as the callbacks' phases lie apart. A 44.1 kHz family output's callbacks take many phases against 48 kHz blocks (147 for 512-frame callbacks against 512-frame blocks, 441 for 512-frame callbacks against 480-frame blocks, a pairing the core's check makes up; a real Windows endpoint's 441-frame periods take one, below), a few frames apart, so the reader comes a few frames closer at a time and is slowed by that much: at most a 64th of a callback's reads at once at 100 ppm (4 capture frames in a 512-frame callback at 44.1 kHz), and some 4.8 frames a second in all. The output thus follows the capture's clock and stays within about a block of where it started, closer to W, never later than its lead. In the core's 30-second runs at 100 ppm fast or slow, for every phase, a fresh session and a running one, with 256- to 4096-frame callbacks at 22.05, 44.1 and 88.2 kHz, no read runs dry and no drift correction is made (runs of 300 s at up to 300 ppm, outside the suite, agree); a slow clock falls behind by some 144 frames in 30 s, far inside the dead band, and is corrected at T as before (S51), about every eight minutes at 100 ppm.
    - *Callbacks with few phases.* Callbacks that take only a few phases against the blocks see a fast clock in `W − P` only when one of them crosses a block's write, and then a whole share of a block at once. A Windows endpoint at 44.1 kHz with 10 ms periods fed 10 ms packets takes one phase: its reader holds as much at every callback until the clock has gained a block, about every 100 s at 100 ppm, and then a block less. A 96 kHz output with 512-frame callbacks against 512-frame blocks takes two, half a block at a time; an output at the capture rate whose callbacks match the blocks takes one. Such a drop is far more than a sixteenth of a callback and nothing in `W − P` announces it, so on its own this slow-down can't keep such a reader off W (in the core's runs it ran dry 3 and 5 times in 300 s at 100 ppm). The times at which the capture wrote its blocks show the clock gaining long before that, which S207 uses; this slow-down stays as its backstop.
    - Without those times, a reader that holds enough for its callback never slows its converter: every output on a clock in step or a slow one, whatever its hold-back, so its timing stays exact as above. On a fast clock, an output held back by more than T plus a callback's reads and a block is stepped back (S52) before it gets near W, as before; one held back by less converts like the earliest output and is slowed the same way once its slack is used up. With those times, S207 trims every converting output instead, whatever its hold-back.
  - *Drift is judged once per callback.* When a callback takes several reads (pieces of at most 512 device frames, S62), only its first read may correct drift (S50 to S53). The others read with corrections held, as in S188, and a correction they report as due is ignored, because their error also counts the frames the same callback has already read: with callbacks of more than about T + N capture frames, every callback used to step back and fade out.
- **S207** *Required* (core tests "render start: callbacks with few phases …"; S206's former known limit). Clock stamps. A converting renderer, whatever its hold-back, follows the device's clock against the capture's by the times of the capture's blocks, not only by `W − P`.
  - *Stamps.* The host stamps each block the capture writes, as the writer closes it, with the time on a monotonic clock the whole process shares (the core's: nanoseconds since its first use), and gives each callback of an output the time it reads at, on the same clock. The ring publishes the newest stamp with the W it was taken at, as a pair a reader loads without waiting: the writer marks the pair as being written (an odd sequence number), stores it, and marks it written (the next even number, a release store); a reader loads the number (acquire), the pair, and, after an acquire fence, the number again, and uses the pair only when both loads gave the same even number, else it goes without one for that callback. A block that wrote nothing gets no stamp, and a reset clears the stamp. Both hosts stamp: the Windows capture at the end of each packet's block (a gap fill's silence included) and each render wake; the Mac's core interface stamps each write and each timed render itself. The clock is the system's monotonic one (QueryPerformanceCounter on Windows, the uptime clock on the Mac), read without waiting; its origin is set when the first ring is made, before any audio thread runs, so an audio thread only loads it (S126). Without times the renderer works as S206 says.
  - *The fill.* At the start of every callback, after taking the control's requests and with the reader going on from where it stands (the conditions of S206's slow-down), the renderer estimates where the capture is now, `Ŵ`: the W of the newest stamp plus the capture frames of the time since it, at the nominal capture rate. The fill is `Ŵ − P`. Unlike `W − P`, which moves only when a block is written, the fill moves smoothly: with both clocks in step it stays where it was (within a frame of the converter's rounding), and on a clock that runs fast or slow it falls or rises by the difference, about 4.8 frames a second at 100 ppm, whatever the phases of the callbacks against the blocks. A fixed offset between the times the two sides take (a capture that stamps when a block is published, not when it was recorded) moves the fill by a constant, and only its changes are used.
  - *The reference.* Over the first half second of device frames, or 16 callbacks when those last longer, after the reader anchors (a prime, a resync or a correction), or after the tracking restarts, the renderer averages the fill; that average is the reference, the fill it keeps, and the running average starts at it. Each fill is taken against Jf as it stood at the first of them (S209), so a fall of the anchor floor while the reference is measured is not averaged into it. Every later callback moves a running average of the fill toward the fill, with the same time constant (half a second, or 16 callbacks), which smooths the jitter of the two sides' wake-ups: with large callbacks, half a second would hold too few of them to smooth it.
  - *The trim.* When the running average is more than 15 frames below the reference, the converter is slowed over the callback by the frames it is below that (rounded up), as in S206; when more than 15 above, it is sped up by as much, its position moving ahead of its course evenly over the steps between the callback's frames, so the callback reads that many frames more. Either way at most a 256th of the callback's reads and at least one frame, the frames are interpolated at their exact positions, nothing is masked, repeated or faded, and the reader counts no correction; the running average moves by the frames trimmed, and the renderer counts the capture frames it slowed (with S206's) and sped its converter by. At 100 ppm that is about 4.8 capture frames a second, one at a time: one frame in a 10 ms callback's 480 reads, some 4 cents for 10 ms.
  - *The dead zone.* The 15 frames filter the hosts' wake jitter out of the error before the trim acts on it, as Fons Adriaensen's "Controlling adaptive resampling" (Linux Audio Conference 2012) filters a resampler's error before its loop corrects it. One wake in 33 or 58 that comes 9 ms late, the worst the core's timing matrix gives a Windows endpoint, makes that callback's fill 432 frames larger and moves the running average up to 12.5 frames, slowly back again; with the 4 frames before, such outputs were trimmed back and forth by hundreds of frames. A dead zone rather than a rule that the error keep its sign over several callbacks: one late wake keeps the average off the same way for tens of callbacks, which such a rule lets through. Real drift still passes it within seconds (about 3 s at 100 ppm), and the output then sits at most 15 frames, about 0.3 ms, off its reference.
  - *Who is trimmed.* Every converting output whose reader goes on from where it stands, whatever its hold-back, both ways: on a fast clock a held-back output would otherwise drift toward its target's edge until a step back (S52) at T, on a slow one until a skip forward (S51), about every eight minutes at 100 ppm, and a delay tuned by ear would wander by up to T and jump back. (Before, only an output held back by less than about T more than its lead was trimmed, and one held back further was corrected at T.) An output at the capture rate converts at ratio 1 (S85) and is trimmed the same way. (Before, it had no converter and kept S42's floor of one read: on a clock of its own that ran fast, at hold-back 0, it ran dry for one callback each time its clock had gained a block, about every 107 s at 100 ppm with 512-frame blocks.)
  - *When it restarts.* The tracking restarts (and measures a new reference) after any read that anchors, corrects, runs dry (unless its silence pays in full toward the anchor floor's fall, S209) or has its copy rejected (S40), while the reader's next read would not go on from where it stands, and when the newest stamp is more than 100 ms or four blocks old, whichever is more (the capture stalled, and S53 applies). A callback without a usable stamp changes nothing.
  - *With S206.* S206's slow-down by a shortage still applies, and wins over a trim the other way in the same callback: the running average lags a clock that drifts (some 2.4 frames at 100 ppm), so at the worst phase a reader may still hold a few frames fewer than its callback needs and is slowed by those, or by the trim's own slow-down when that is larger. The running average moves by the frames slowed and the reference by the part of them the trim didn't ask for, each less what of that part pays toward the anchor floor's fall (S209): the output keeps that slack from then on, so no trim the other way takes it back (while the reference is still measured, the fills taken so far move by the frames slowed, less what of them pays toward that fall). In the core's 60-second runs at 100 and 300 ppm fast or slow (300 ppm takes a 441-frame output fed 480-frame blocks past a block's slack inside the minute), with 441-frame callbacks at 44.1 kHz against 480-frame blocks, 512-frame callbacks at 96 kHz and 256-frame ones at 32 kHz against 512-frame blocks, at hold-back 0 and 25 ms, for every phase, a fresh session and a running one, with stamps taken at the exact times, no read runs dry, no drift correction is made, and the converter is trimmed one way only, by no more than the clock gained or lost plus 16 frames; without stamps, those at 96 and 32 kHz ran dry at 100 ppm and all of them at 300 ppm. The same holds over 30 seconds for the many-phase setups (512 and 4096-frame callbacks at 44.1 kHz against 512-frame blocks, 512-frame ones against 480-frame blocks, 88.2 and 22.05 kHz) and 1024-frame callbacks at 44.1 kHz against 480-frame blocks. Real stamps are off by the jitter of the hosts' wake-ups, and the reference by as much: with every stamp up to 0.2 ms off either way, at hold-back 0 in every setup above on a clock in step or 100 or 300 ppm off, the converter is never trimmed the other way; with every stamp up to 1 ms off, the running average crosses the dead zone now and then and the converter is trimmed the other way by at most 32 frames a minute, a frame at a time, but still no read runs dry and no drift correction is made. An output on a clock in step, whatever its hold-back, plays the very same frames with stamps as without. Held back by 100 ms, on a clock 100 or 300 ppm fast or slow over 60 seconds, an output 44.1 or 96 kHz is trimmed one way by at least nine tenths of what its clock gained or lost, with no dry read and no correction; before, it drifted until a step back at T.

With an AirPlay speaker chosen and no offset below −L, every local output is held back by about L, far more than its ℓ, so K = 0: each output sounds exactly at h(x), L plus its offset as P114's timing contract says, now without the converter's half millisecond. When an offset below −L sets m, that output's hold-back is 0, K is its ℓ, and the AirPlay speakers lead the local outputs by −m − L + K.
  - *Joining and leaving.* The renderers publish ℓ and h in the ring, which holds a slot for each of up to 32 renderers: h as soon as the host asks for a delay, before the renderer takes it, so the hold-backs of one re-time (S177) reach K together; ℓ only when the host sets the period or, without one (the Mac), at the first callback, so a renderer counts once its hold-back is set (the Mac sets it right after making the renderer) and its ℓ is final. An output leaves K when its stop's fade-out is asked for (S185). A renderer whose delay `h + K` changes re-anchors as on a delay change (S187), once its lateness floor has formed when it plays (S209): fading out first when it plays, then landing on the delay as it stands after the fade-out's last frame, so the changes of one re-time land together; a rise inserts silence, a fall skips forward. K therefore moves an output whose own hold-back is unchanged only when the output that sets K joins, leaves or changes its hold-back. Such an output is usually the earliest, whose arrival or departure moves m too, so S177 re-times the same outputs in the same fade. An output that joins at a hold-back of at least its ℓ less K, or leaves without setting K, moves no other output.
  - *Limits.* Readers anchor against W (S44), which moves a capture block at a time, so outputs that anchor between different blocks lie up to a block apart (10 to 11 ms), as before; the exact difference holds for outputs that anchor between the same two writes, as outputs re-timed together usually do. ### Fades at stream boundaries

S182 to S191 take the click out of starts, stops, source changes and re-timings. A short click is heard on every source change and every start and stop on the Mac's local outputs, Bluetooth included: an output plays the first frame its reader copies at full level, whatever the source plays at that instant (S45); a stop halts every output in mid-waveform (S115); a source change does both (S141); and a delay change joins two unrelated points of the waveform or cuts to silence (S47, S48). Each of these boundaries gets a short fade. These clauses are design requirements: the Rust core, the Windows app and the Mac app follow them. The Windows app used to fade an output out over whatever one device callback held, which could be a few frames; S185 replaced that with a fixed length.

- **S182** *Required.* Fade shape and length. A fade is a gain w applied alike to every channel of a frame, over F = round(0.020 × R) frames at the rate R of the stream it is applied to (960 at 48 kHz, 882 at 44.1 kHz). For frame i = 0 … F − 1 of a fade, with x = (i + 1) / F: a fade-in has w = ½ − ½·cos(π·x), and w = 1 exactly at i = F − 1; a fade-out has w = ½ + ½·cos(π·x), and w = 0 exactly at i = F − 1. A stream is *open* (w = 1), *fading in*, *fading out* or *closed* (w = 0: exact zeros, whatever the input, NaN and ±infinity included). A fade-out requested while fading in, or a fade-in requested while fading out, continues from the frame of the other curve whose w is closest to the current one, so w never jumps; a request for the state a stream is already in or heading to changes nothing. Reasons: 20 ms is long enough that the ramp itself carries next to no energy above about 50 Hz (1 / 20 ms), so it isn't heard as a click even on bass-heavy material, and short enough to sound like a clean edge rather than a fade, while a stop waits only about that long (S186). A raised cosine has zero slope at both ends, so it has no corner where it meets silence or full level; its largest step between frames is π / (2F) of the signal (0.16 % at 48 kHz, against 0.10 % for a linear ramp of the same length, which has corners at both ends); and starting it a few frames early is inaudible: 16 frames early, the audio enters at 0.08 % (−62 dB) where a linear ramp would already be at 1.8 % (−35 dB), which S184 relies on.
- **S183** *Required.* Where a local output fades. Each local output's renderer applies its fade at the device rate, after rate conversion and channel mapping (S62, S63), as a factor of its own after the volume gain (S64, S71): the device gets the sample × the gain × w, so the last frame of a fade-out and every frame after it are exact zeros whatever the gain or the converter left. F counts device frames (S182 with R the device's rate). The fade never changes the gain or a stored volume; a gain change during a fade changes only the gain factor, with its own ramp (S64) as before, and an output at gain 0 stays exact zeros. NaN and ±infinity are replaced by 0 before the fade multiplies (S65), so w = 0 never turns them into NaN.
- **S184** *Required.* Fade-in when audio starts. A renderer starts closed. A frame its reader fills with silence closes the stream at once (the frame is zero either way, so w may jump there; a fade-out under way ends there as if its last frame had been rendered), and the first frame the reader copies while the stream is closed starts a fade-in, with one exception. While a stop, a delay change or a held correction waits (S185, S187, S188), the stream stays closed after its fade-out until the read that applies the change: every frame is rendered as an exact zero, copied frames included (also those after the fade-out's last frame in the same block), and none of them starts a fade-in. For a delay change or a correction, the first frame copied by the read that applies it (or by the next read, should no correction be due by then) starts the fade-in; after a stop the stream stays closed until the output stops. Without the exception the stream would fade in on the old timeline and jump when the change lands, mid-fade-in: about 1 % of the signal with 512-frame buffers, up to the full level with 4096-frame buffers, against the 0.16 % S191 allows. So an output fades in on the first audio of a session (S45), on the first audio of an output switched on or plugged in during a session (S117, S137, S161), whether its first read primes at once or after silent reads, after the masked silence of a delay increase or a step back (S44 step 3, S47, S52), and after a dry or starved read (S53, S54). For this, each read reports, besides filling its block, how many frames at the block's start it filled with silence and how many it then copied (S44 step 3 orders a block as silence, then copies, then silence; a copy the post-copy check discards, S40, counts as silence). The fade starts where the first copied frame leaves the converter: the device frame its position converts to, plus the converter's fixed latency at the device rate, rounded up; the frames before it stay closed and render as exact zeros. At ratio 1 that is exactly the first copied frame; at other rates it may start the fade up to a frame late (inaudible, S182). Reason: at each of these moments the output jumps from silence to whatever the source plays at that instant, up to full scale; this is the click heard on every start and source change, and on a speaker switched on mid-session. A source that starts from silence by itself loses nothing audible, and a note that begins exactly then loses only 20 ms of its attack.
- **S185** *Required.* Fade-out before an output stops. When the host stops an output whose device still runs (a speaker switched off, S117, S161; every output at a session stop, S186), the renderer fades out over its next F device frames, starting with the first callback that begins after the request, and renders exact zeros after them. It keeps rendering exact zeros after the fade-out and counts as *faded* only once the fade-out's last frame has been played; only then is its device's IO stopped (`stop`, S61). A device plays a frame well after the callback that rendered it: Core Audio renders about one buffer plus the device's safety offset ahead of the output, and WASAPI keeps up to a whole endpoint buffer queued, so stopping the IO as soon as the fade has been rendered, or one buffer later, drops the end of the fade and cuts the sound mid-waveform after all. On the Mac, the renderer publishes the host time at which the fade's last frame is output: its callback's timestamp (the output time of the buffer's first frame) plus that frame's offset in the buffer at the device rate, plain arithmetic on the audio thread. The control thread adds the device's and its output stream's reported latency (`kAudioDevicePropertyLatency`, `kAudioStreamPropertyLatency`, Apple's Core Audio documentation; a Bluetooth device's within S231's window) and counts the output as faded once the host clock passes that time. On Windows, the render thread, whose loop reads the endpoint's padding (`GetCurrentPadding`) anyway, counts the frames it wrote after the fade's last frame and, while stopping, reads the stream's clock (`IAudioClock::GetPosition` over `GetFrequency`, at the device rate) against every frame it wrote since the start, the silent prefill included. The output is faded once the frames written after the fade cover what the clock says is written but not yet played, taken as no less than the padding (only zeros are still queued) and no more than the padding plus 100 ms. Without a clock reading the rule is the padding plus the stream latency the endpoint reports (`IAudioClient::GetStreamLatency`). Reasons: `GetStreamLatency` read 0 on every endpoint of a Windows 11 PC, while the clock put the played position 4 to 30 ms past the queued buffer on the same endpoints (USB headphones about 29 ms), so the old rule could cut that much of the fade's end; the 100 ms bound keeps a clock that stalls, or a driver that counts more than the endpoint's own pipeline, from holding a stop for long, well inside S186's 500 ms. An output whose gain has been 0 for longer than that latency MAY count as faded at once, since nothing it still has queued is audible. An output whose device is gone or whose IO stopped by itself (S68) is stopped without a fade, since nothing can be rendered to it. This replaces the Windows app's fade over one device callback, whose length depended on how many frames that callback held.
- **S186** *Required.* Session stop and source change. `stop()` (S115) first asks every running renderer to fade out (S185) and the AirPlay inputs to fade out (S189), all at once, so any number of outputs costs one fade. It then waits until every renderer is faded, with capture still running: an output at a short hold-back fades out on frames that are still being captured (S42), and with capture stopped first its reader would run dry and cut (S53). Then it goes on as S115 says: capture stops, every renderer is stopped and removed, and the FIFO feed stops. Each renderer is faded within up to one device buffer (until its first faded callback begins), then F, then its output latency (S185: on the Mac about one buffer plus the safety offset plus the device's and stream's latency; on Windows what the endpoint's clock has queued, at most the endpoint buffer plus 100 ms) of the request: about 45 ms for built-in speakers with 512-frame buffers at 48 kHz (10.7 ms until the first faded callback, 20 ms of fade, then 10.7 ms of buffer and a few ms of safety offset and latency), and up to about 350 ms for a Bluetooth speaker, which sounds some 150 to 300 ms late (S172). The host waits for the slowest renderer but never more than 500 ms in all, which covers that Bluetooth range with room for larger buffers; an output that isn't faded by then (its IO stalled, or its latency is beyond the cap) is stopped anyway. A stop on a Bluetooth speaker thus takes about as long as the speaker's own lag, during which it is still playing the end of the audio anyway. The wait happens on the control thread, never on an audio thread or the FIFO writer (S190). Removing outputs while the session goes on (S117, S161) asks every output being removed to fade out at once and then waits once, the same way, for those outputs. A source change (S141, S163) is a stop followed by a start, so every output fades out on the old source and fades in on the new one at its first audio (S184), with silence between them for as long as the rebuild and each output's hold-back take. There is no crossfade: the two sources are unrelated audio, and a crossfade would need both captures running at once, possibly at different rates. A stop after an interruption (S20, S21), the stop at quit (S110, within its 5 s budget) and the stop at an exit signal (S111, within its 4 s bound) run the same way; when capture has already stopped delivering, readers at a short hold-back are dry and their fades run over silence. This supersedes S115 in part: the outputs and the AirPlay inputs fade out before capture stops.
- **S187** *Required.* Delay and hold-back changes. A new delay for a running output (S66, and every re-timing of S177, which happens whenever an output joins or leaves, an offset or the reference changes, the AirPlay condition flips or L changes, S178) is applied between a fade-out and a fade-in. The renderer fades out at its current delay, keeping its device running, and only after the fade-out's last frame stores the new delay and asks its reader to resync (S46), so the reader still lands exactly on the new delay (`lagFrames = De − N`, S46, S56). The audio after the resync fades in (S184): after an increase once the masked silence of S47 has passed (still exactly Δ frames; above 5 s, see large increases below), after a decrease (S48) right after the skip. A change therefore applies up to F plus one device buffer later than S66 says, and a newer change that arrives during the fade-out replaces the waiting one without restarting the fade. A change that arrives while the renderer is closed (no audio yet, or between a fade-out and the next fade-in) goes to the reader at once, with no fade. This supersedes S66 in part: the change reaches the reader after the fade-out, not on the next callback. Reason: a skip forward joins two unrelated points of the waveform and an increase cuts to silence, both a click on every re-timing; under S177 that includes the other outputs whenever a speaker that sets the base is switched on or off. *Large increases* (2026-10-21; this supersedes S47 and S49 in part, for a running output's renderer): when a running output's renderer gives its reader a delay more than 5000 ms (at the capture rate, S3) above the one it gave it last (a change as above, or its first delay after the start), it also resets the reader's prime (S159). The reader then plays at once at the new delay, exactly as an output started there would (S45): it anchors at `W − De` on its first read once more than De frames are written, playing again the frames it already played in the last Δ, instead of the Δ of silence of S47. Before that much is written (a session younger than the new delay) it stays silent until it is, as a new output would. The fade-out before the change and the fade-in after it stay as above, and increases of up to 5000 ms and all decreases are unchanged (S47, S48). Reason: on the Mac a 15 or 20 s delay entered on a running speaker kept it silent for up to 20 s, which reads as an output that stopped, while switching the speaker off and on played it at once; 5 s lies above the usual AirPlay latency L (about 2 s, S178) that every local output gains when an AirPlay speaker is chosen, so that change usually still waits in silence. L may reach 20 000 ms (S178): with an AirPlay delay above 5 s, choosing an AirPlay speaker restarts every local speaker's reader, and each replays up to L of what it just played, as an output started fresh at its new hold-back would.
- **S188** *Required.* Corrections the reader makes by itself. A renderer reads with drift corrections *held*: a read that would make a drift correction (the *Drift* case of S44 step 2: a skip forward, S51, or a step back, S52, S53) leaves P alone, emits its block contiguously as if the error were inside the dead band, and reports that a correction is due. The renderer then fades out from the start of this block, over it and the next ones, still holding, and its first read after the fade-out's last frame is made without the hold, so the correction is made then, against that read's W and exactly as S51 and S52 say; the audio after it fades in (S184). Holding changes only the Drift case: prime, resync and dry reads (S44 step 2) and the masking of step 3 stay as they are, and the counters (S158) count a correction when it is made. A held correction comes up to F plus one block later, during which the error can grow by as much again (a step back while the writer has stopped moves e by N per read). So for a renderer, with F_c = round(0.020 × Rc), the worst deviation of S57 grows from T + N to T + 2N + F_c (4384 frames with 512-frame blocks at 48 kHz; 3664 at the suite's former T of 1680), and in a writer stall its reader never reaches W (S53) only when De > T + 2N + F_c, where S53 says T + N. The ring buffer suite's reader checks read without holding and are unchanged. Two edges stay unfaded: an overrun (S55; the frames a fade-out would play are already overwritten) and the start of an underrun (S53; the reader can't know that the writer stopped before it reaches W, and predicting it would cost every output a block of latency). Both are rare in normal play (a source that pauses usually fades out by itself), and the audio after them fades in (S184).
- **S189** *Required.* The AirPlay leg. Both AirPlay senders' inputs fade too: the FIFO feed (S84) for the bundled engine, and the native sender's input, which takes the same blocks after the equalizer (AirPlay spec P111, P143). Each applies the fade itself to the frames it is given, after the split of S7, so the ring and the AirPlay leg still get bit-identical input. Fade-in: in the FIFO writer over the first F frames it writes after each open of the pipe (S89, S169), which covers every session start (the writer opens the pipe after each `startWriter()`) and every engine restart (a new stream that would otherwise begin mid-waveform); in the native input over the first F frames after each rate set at a capture build (P143). Fade-out: in `feed` and in the native input, over the F frames given after a stop request (S186), then zeros until capture stops. A stop request closes `feed` only until the next session: its `setInputRate` and `startWriter()` (S94, S88) open it again, so the new session's audio passes, faded in by the writer after its open; the native input likewise opens again at its next rate set (P143) and fades in from there. F counts frames at the rate where the fade is applied: 48 000 Hz in the FIFO writer (S83, rounding as S86), Rc in `feed` and in the native input. Nothing in the bundled engine's documented interface or in the AirPlay specification promises a ramp at the start of a stream, so AudioSync doesn't rely on one; a fade costs one multiply per sample. What the speakers do when the stream is cut is not AudioSync's to shape: a session stop stops the engine's player at once (S99) and the native sender flushes or tears down (P100, P129, P143), which drops the audio the receivers hold (about L), so the faded end of the stream is heard only when the engine plays the tail, for example while a switch-on holds the write queue (S101). Whether a receiver clicks when it is stopped or flushed mid-stream is a check on real receivers (test map). Holding a stop back by L so that the faded tail plays would make every stop and source change about two seconds slower, and is not done. A backlog skip (S90) is not faded either: the frames before it are already in the pipe, and a fade-in after it alone would not remove the step.
- **S190** *Required.* Real time. A fade's state (per renderer, per AirPlay input, and in the FIFO writer) is allocated before the audio threads start (S128); w is computed per frame, or read from a table filled before the audio thread starts, with no allocation, lock, system call or logging (S126, S129), in O(N) per callback (S127). The control thread's requests are atomics that the audio thread takes at the start of a callback (a fade-out requested; a delay change waiting for its fade-out, its value stored before its flag as S133 says for a resync); a renderer publishes the output time of its fade's last frame (Mac) or that it is faded (Windows, S185) with a release store that the control thread reads with acquire. The only wait is the control thread's in S186, bounded at 500 ms.
- **S191** *Required.* What "no click" means, as the tests check it. With a constant input of amplitude A (|A| ≤ 1) at gain g on a stream whose fade length is F: (a) frame i of a fade is A × g × w(i) with S182's w, within 1e−6 (within one 16-bit step on the pipe); (b) across every boundary of S184 to S189 other than the unfaded edges they name (a lost device, an output stopped at the 500 ms cap, an overrun, the start of an underrun, a backlog skip, a receiver stopped or flushed), from the last frame before a fade-out to the first frame after the next fade-in, silence between them included, no two consecutive frames differ by more than A × g × π / (2F) plus that tolerance, where a hard edge differs by up to A × g; (c) every frame after a fade-out's last one is exactly 0 until the next fade-in starts; (d) with a sine of amplitude A instead, at any frequency, no step between consecutive frames across those boundaries exceeds the sine's own largest step plus A × g × π / (2F). For timing: (e) `stop()` returns within S186's bound (up to one buffer, F and the output latency of the slowest output, never more than 500 ms), and no output's IO stops before its fade-out's last frame has played unless the cap passed, which a simulated device that plays each buffer one buffer plus a safety offset after its callback must show (a check that only looks at rendered frames can't); (f) a delay change lands exactly on the new delay (S46) once its fade-out is done.

## 7. Volume

- **S70** *Observed* (`Volume.swift:8-16`; tests "volume: …"). Slider position p (0…100) maps to linear gain `g(p) = (clamp(p, 0, 100) / 100)³`; the inverse is `p(g) = 100 × cbrt(clamp(g, 0, 1))`. The curve is strictly increasing over integer positions. Reference values:

  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | g | 0 | 1e−6 | 0.001 | 0.015625 | 0.125 | 0.216 | 0.421875 | 0.729 | 1 |
  | dB | −∞ | −120.00 | −60.00 | −36.12 | −18.06 | −13.31 | −7.50 | −2.75 | 0.00 |

- **S71** *Observed* (`AppDelegate+Outputs.swift:10-12`; `AppSettings.swift:50-51`). A local output's renderer gain is `g(local p) × g(master p)`. New devices start at local p = 60 (≈ −13.3 dB); master defaults to 100.
- **S72** *Retired in part with the bundled engine (2026-10-20)*: a master change still re-applies the gain of every active local output (S71); what reaches the AirPlay speakers is the AirPlay sender's (`docs/AIRPLAY_SPEC.md`), and the rest is a record. *Observed* (`AppDelegate+Outputs.swift:47-52`, `setMasterVolume`; `SpeakerState.swift`, `LocalOutputSync.reapplyGains`; test "S72 master: every running local speaker gets its gain again, and only those"). A master change re-applies the gain of every active local output, and of no other. *Record* (until 2026-10-20: `AirPlayReconcile.swift:395-404`, deleted with the bundled engine; tests "volume: moving the master scales each playing speaker from its own volume, never above it", "volume: a master move while stopped sends nothing, and the next start carries it", removed with it): while streaming, a master change also sent every enabled, listed AirPlay speaker its own volume times the new master (S105), never more than its own and without changing it; while stopped it sent nothing, and the next start carried the master. The engine's master volume was never written (the suites failed if any source sent `PUT /api/player/volume` or a 'pvol' metadata item): the engine's master was its loudest selected speaker's volume, and a master write rescaled every selected speaker relative to it, so it would have set a lone speaker to the master position.
- **S73** *Retired with the bundled engine (2026-10-20); kept as a record.* *Observed* (`AppDelegate+Outputs.swift:58-62`; `AirPlayReconcile.swift:372-385`; engine client `:156-161`; tests "volume: an AirPlay speaker plays at its own volume times the master, never above its own", "volume: after a master move the poll keeps the speaker's own volume"). An AirPlay speaker's own volume (its slider, 0–100, saved across launches) reaches the engine per output as the integer `round(own × master / 100)` on the engine's own 0–100 scale (linear, not the cubic curve), the AirPlay counterpart of S71, so never above the speaker's own volume. A slider change sends that value at once. The volume the engine reports never replaces a speaker's own volume, except for a speaker without one (S105).
- **S74** *Retired with the bundled engine (2026-10-20); kept as a record.* *Observed* (`Volume.swift:19-36`; engine client `:156-172`; tests "volume writes: …"). Volume requests are coalesced per output: storing a value when none is waiting queues one flush; later values replace the waiting one; the flush sends the latest value once; targets never overwrite each other.
- **S75** *Observed* (`AppSettings.swift:44-46`, `:109-113`, `:160-169`; "volume: legacy 3.49% migrates to ≈33", "settings: older linear gains become slider positions (3.49 % → 32.68, 150 % → 100, S75)"). Legacy linear percentages (0…150) migrate as `p(pct / 100)`, clamped (3.49 % → 32.68; anything ≥ 100 % → 100). Local volumes stored that way are converted once at launch, saved as positions and the old values removed; positions already stored win.
- **S76** *Observed* (`AppDelegate+Outputs.swift:51-62`). Host-side volume inputs are clamped to [0, 100] before use.

### Output devices' own volume

S192 to S202 make a turned-down or muted output device visible. Every local output also has its own volume and mute, kept by the operating system per device and applied after AudioSync's renderer gain (S71), so what is heard is AudioSync's gain times the device's. On a Mac only the default output's volume is in the Sound menu and on the volume keys; the others are reached only through Audio MIDI Setup, so a device left at a low volume stays out of sight: at a test on a MacBook, its speakers sounded very quiet with AudioSync's slider at 100 because their own volume was 13 %. Windows keeps a volume and a mute per endpoint the same way. Before these clauses neither app read or set an output device's volume or mute (the Windows app read them only for the double-audio check, `windows/src/audio/wasapi/routing.rs`); both apps follow them now (`windows/src/audio/controller/device_volume.rs` on Windows, `Sources/AudioSync/DeviceVolume.swift` on the Mac). These clauses are design requirements, kept small for the feature freeze: AudioSync reads and shows, and writes only when the user clicks the fix.

- **S192** *Required.* Scope. The *device volume* of a local output is its operating system volume as a scalar from 0 to 1 (the position of the system's own slider for that device) and its mute. AudioSync reads both for every local output row that is switched on, whether or not a session runs, and for no other device: not for AirPlay speakers (whose volume is S73), not for outputs switched off, not for the capture source. It never sets a device volume or mute by itself: not at launch, at a start or stop, when an output is switched on or plugged in, on a source change, at quit, nor to restore an earlier value. The only write is the fix the user clicks (S197). The device volume never changes AudioSync's own volumes, its stored settings or the renderer gain (S70 to S76), and has no part in delay or sync.
- **S193** *Required.* Reading on the Mac (Apple's Core Audio documentation). In the output scope of the device: the volume is `kAudioDevicePropertyVolumeScalar` of the main element when the device has it there; otherwise the mean of that property over the device's preferred stereo channels (`kAudioDevicePropertyPreferredChannelsForStereo`, channels 1 and 2 when the device doesn't say) that have it. The mute is `kAudioDevicePropertyMute` of the main element when the device has it; otherwise the device counts as muted only when every one of those channels that has a mute is muted. Each part counts as *known* only when its property is present and settable (`AudioObjectIsPropertySettable`): a volume that can't be set can't be fixed, so it is treated as absent. Many HDMI and DisplayPort outputs, some USB devices, and aggregate and multi-output devices have neither; S196 then shows nothing. Reading happens on the control or main thread, never on an audio thread.
- **S194** *Required.* Reading on Windows. The volume is `IAudioEndpointVolume::GetMasterVolumeLevelScalar` of the endpoint and the mute is `GetMute`, as the double-audio snapshot reads them today. Windows applies the endpoint volume in software where the hardware has none, so both parts are known whenever the endpoint's volume control can be activated and read; when activation or a read fails, both are unknown. Channel volumes are not used: the master scalar is what the Sound settings slider shows.
- **S195** *Required.* Following changes. When an output's row is switched on (including the rows switched on at launch) or its device appears again (S137, S161), the host reads its device volume once and registers for changes: on the Mac a property listener on each address S193 read (`AudioObjectAddPropertyListenerBlock`, on a serial queue, results passed to the main thread); on Windows `IAudioEndpointVolume::RegisterControlChangeNotify`, whose callback runs on a system thread and passes the new mute and level to the UI thread. The row then shows the latest value; several notifications before the UI thread runs collapse into one update. The host removes the listener or unregisters the callback when the row is switched off, its device goes away, or the app quits, and holds no listener for any other device. A notification never touches an audio thread or the renderer.
- **S196** *Required.* The decision, a pure function of the two parts. A known volume v becomes the whole percent `round(100 × clamp(v, 0, 1))` (half away from zero); a NaN or infinite reading makes the volume unknown. The output is *muted* when the mute is known and set, *low* when the volume is known and 25 % or less, both when both hold, and *fine* otherwise; with both parts unknown it is *unknown*. Muted, low, and muted and low show a hint (S198); fine and unknown show nothing. Reason for 25 %: it catches a device left near the bottom (13 % in the case above, which sounded very quiet at AudioSync's 100) and leaves alone users who keep a device moderately low on purpose, such as a laptop at a third of its volume at night. The bound counts as low itself, so a device the system shows at 25 % gets the hint, as "a quarter or less" reads. A volume of 0 % without mute counts as low. The hint stays for as long as the decision does: it has no dismiss button and doesn't time out, and it goes only when the volume or the mute changes (or the row is switched off). This is intended: a user who keeps headphones at 15 % on purpose keeps seeing it, and it is small enough to live with.
- **S197** *Required.* The fix. One click writes, once each: the volume, set to 0.75 only when the output is low; and the mute, cleared only when it is muted. The fix never lowers a volume, so unmuting a device at 90 % leaves it at 90 %. On the Mac it sets the same addresses S193 read: the main element's scalar, or each preferred stereo channel's scalar to 0.75 (a channel balance set on such a device is lost, which is rare and stated here), and the main mute or each channel's mute to 0; on Windows `SetMasterVolumeLevelScalar(0.75)` and `SetMute(false)`. The row is then updated from the change notification (S195), not from the values written, so a write the device ignores or refuses leaves the hint and the button exactly as they were, and only the log says so (S201). This is intended: the row always shows what the device reports, and a second click simply tries again. No dialog, alert or confirmation is shown. Reason for 75 % rather than 100 %: it is loud enough that AudioSync's own slider spans its useful range again, and short of the device's maximum, so one click doesn't put headphones or a nearby speaker at full scale; anyone who wants more raises it in the system's settings or with AudioSync's slider.
- **S198** *Required.* Where the hint shows. In the main window's row of a switched-on local output (Mac `SpeakerRow`, Windows `SpeakerRow` in `widgets.slint`), on its own line under the volume slider: a small speaker glyph (decorative, hidden from VoiceOver and Narrator), the hint text in the secondary text style with at least 4.5:1 contrast in light and dark appearance, and the fix as a borderless, link-style button after it. It appears and disappears with the decision (S196) without moving focus, raising a window or showing a notice, and never on an AirPlay row or a row switched off; a click on the fix while it has keyboard focus first moves focus to the row's volume slider, so focus doesn't vanish with the button once the fix works. ## 8. AirPlay sync rule (local delay policy)

- **S77** *Superseded by S175 and S178* (local delay model, below). *Observed* (`AppSettings.swift:41-43`, `:54-55`, `:81-82`, `:106-107`; `AppDelegate.swift:129-130`; `AppDelegate+Outputs.swift:100-106`; `README.md` "Using it"). Each local output has a configured delay in ms, range 0…20 000, default 2000 (seeded from a legacy single delay when present, clamped to the range). Non-finite input is ignored; other input is clamped.
- **S78** *Superseded by S176.* *Observed* (`SpeakerState.swift:49-52`; tests "delay: AirPlay chosen → configured delay", "delay: no AirPlay → plays at once", "delay: zero stays zero"). Effective delay = the configured delay while an AirPlay speaker is chosen, otherwise 0. The configured value is kept either way. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S79** *Observed* (`SpeakerState.swift:54-58`; tests "delay: a chosen speaker not listed today doesn't count", "delay: chosen and listed counts", "delay: listed but not chosen doesn't count"). "An AirPlay speaker is chosen" means at least one AirPlay output ID is both enabled by the user and present in the AirPlay engine's latest output list (this Mac's own receiver is never in that list, S100). *Now covered by the core.*
- **S80** *Superseded by S177.* *Observed* (`AppDelegate+Outputs.swift:110-136`; `AppDelegate.swift:83-88`, `:136-138`). Whenever that condition flips (the enabled set or the listed set changed), every running local output gets its new effective delay live through S66: a flip to 0 skips forward (S48), a flip back inserts silence (S47).
- **S81** *Observed* (`AppDelegate+AirPlay.swift:24-29`). A poll that finds the AirPlay engine unreachable leaves the listed set unchanged, so an engine restart doesn't flap local delays.

### Local delay model (offsets relative to a reference output)

S172 to S181 replace the rule that local outputs play at once unless an AirPlay speaker is chosen (S77, S78, S80). Under that rule the delay fields did nothing with only local outputs, although a Bluetooth speaker sounds some 150 to 300 ms behind a wired one, and every row, the built-in speakers included, carried an absolute delay of about two seconds that only meant something next to AirPlay. In this model the built-in speakers are the reference and have no delay setting; every other local output has an offset relative to them that always applies; the host turns the offsets of the outputs taking part into hold-backs and adds the AirPlay latency while an AirPlay speaker is chosen. The Rust core, the Mac app and the Windows app follow these clauses; builds before them behave as the superseded clauses say. S79 (what "an AirPlay speaker is chosen" means) and S81 still apply.

- **S172** *Required.* Terms used by S173 to S181:
  - A *local output* is an output the host renders to itself (S60), keyed by device UID on the Mac and by endpoint ID on Windows.
  - The *reference* R is at most one local output, chosen by S173 on the Mac and by S174 on Windows. It defines time 0 and has no delay setting of its own.
  - The *offset* o(x) of every other local output x is how much longer, in ms, x is held back than the reference: positive for an output that sounds early next to the reference (or that the user wants later), negative for one that sounds late (a Bluetooth speaker is typically 150 to 300 ms late, so about −150 to −300). The reference's offset is 0 by definition, whatever value may be stored for that output.
  - The outputs *taking part*, T, are the local outputs the user switched on whose devices are listed now (enabled ∩ available, S161), the reference included when it is one of them. An enabled output whose device is missing (unplugged, a Bluetooth device out of range) takes part again once its device is listed.
  - The *hold-back* h(x) is the delay in ms the host gives the renderer of x (`attachOutput` and `setDelay`, S61, S112), what S78, S117, S137 and S144 call the effective or configured delay a renderer gets. Hold-backs are computed by S176; they are never stored or shown.
- **S173** *Required.* Mac reference. The candidates are the listed output devices (S171) whose transport type is built-in (`kAudioDeviceTransportTypeBuiltIn`, Apple's Core Audio documentation). The reference is the first candidate in Core Audio order whose first output stream reports the speaker terminal type (`kAudioStreamTerminalTypeSpeaker` for `kAudioStreamPropertyTerminalType`); when no candidate reports it (the property can't be read, or reports another type, as an Intel Mac's single built-in device may while its headphone jack is in use), the first candidate in Core Audio order; with no candidate, there is no reference. So on an Apple silicon MacBook the built-in speakers stay the reference while the built-in headphone output, also of transport type built-in, is listed beside them, and a Mac mini, iMac, Mac Studio or Mac Pro uses its own built-in speaker. A built-in output that isn't the reference (that headphone output) has an offset like any other output. The choice is made afresh from every device list and doesn't depend on whether the reference is switched on: switched off, it is still the reference (its row still has no delay field, S181, and the other offsets keep their meaning), it just doesn't take part (S176). With no reference, every local output has an offset, and the offsets are relative to each other only (S176 needs nothing more).
- **S174** *Required.* Windows reference. The reference is the listed output of kind BuiltIn, the PC's own audio hardware (an endpoint whose form factor is Speakers or LineLevel and whose device enumerator is not USB, Bluetooth or ROOT, as `output_kind` in `windows/src/audio/outputs.rs` decides), with the smallest endpoint ID in byte order (endpoint IDs are ASCII); with no BuiltIn output listed, there is no reference, as in S173. Reasons: BuiltIn is the Windows counterpart of the Mac's built-in speakers, the onboard codec with the shortest and steadiest output latency (a laptop's speakers, a desktop's line out); headphones on the PC's own jack are of kind Headphones and have an offset like any other output, usually 0; endpoint IDs stay the same across restarts and reboots, so on a PC with more than one BuiltIn endpoint the reference doesn't move between launches; Windows' default output is not used, because the user changes it freely and every offset would change meaning with it. Switched off, the reference stays the reference, as in S173.
- **S175** *Required.* Offsets. An offset is a finite number of ms from −2000 to +20 000; an output that never had one set has offset 0. Input that isn't finite is ignored and other input is clamped to that range (for local outputs this replaces the 0 to 20 000 ms of S77 and S124). Offsets are stored per output (device UID or endpoint ID) and kept across launches whether the output is connected, switched on or the reference at the moment; a stored offset of the reference is kept but not used while it is the reference (S172). The window edits whole ms (S181). The lower bound covers Bluetooth and other wireless outputs that sound up to two seconds late; the upper bound is S77's maximum.
- **S176** *Required.* Hold-backs. With o′(x) = 0 for the reference and o(x) for every other output, the base is
  `m = min({ o′(y) : y ∈ T } ∪ { −L, only while an AirPlay speaker is chosen (S79) })`,
  and every x in T gets `h(x) = min(o′(x) − m, 20 000)` ms, with L from S178. No hold-back is negative. Without AirPlay, the output with the smallest offset among those taking part plays at once (at the session lead, S208) and the others wait for it by exactly the difference (S208). Adding the same number to every offset therefore changes nothing, and a lone output plays at once whatever its offset, since there is nothing to line it up with. With an AirPlay speaker chosen, in the usual case where no offset in T is below −L (L is about two seconds, a Bluetooth offset a few hundred ms), m = −L and h(x) = L + o′(x): the reference sounds when the AirPlay speakers are taken to sound (S178) and every other output by its offset. An output taking part whose offset is below −L sounds later than the AirPlay speakers themselves; it then sets m, the local outputs still line up with each other, and the AirPlay speakers lead them by −m − L ms, because AudioSync never holds the AirPlay path back (S8); under the native sender, raising every AirPlay speaker's d_s by that much, where the window offers d_s, makes up for it. The cap at 20 000 ms (S77's maximum, which the ring covers, S36) only bites when a large offset meets a large L or a negative base; that output then plays early by the excess. Examples with the built-in speakers R, a USB speaker at +150, a Bluetooth speaker at −250 and L = 2000 (hold-backs in ms):

  | Taking part | AirPlay chosen | m | h(R) | h(USB) | h(Bluetooth) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | R, USB | no | 0 | 0 | 150 | – |
  | R, Bluetooth | no | −250 | 250 | – | 0 |
  | R, USB, Bluetooth | no | −250 | 250 | 400 | 0 |
  | USB, Bluetooth (R switched off) | no | −250 | – | 400 | 0 |
  | Bluetooth alone | no | −250 | – | – | 0 |
  | R, USB, Bluetooth | yes | −2000 | 2000 | 2150 | 1750 |
  | Bluetooth alone | yes | −2000 | – | – | 1750 |

  The first row is the reported bug: under S78, with no AirPlay speaker chosen, the USB speaker plays at 0 like the built-in speakers and its delay does nothing.
- **S177** *Required.* Re-timing. The host recomputes every hold-back whenever T changes (a speaker switched on or off, a device listed again or lost, S161, S68), the offset of an output taking part changes, the reference changes (S173, S174), the AirPlay condition flips (S79), or L changes (S178). Each running renderer whose hold-back changed gets the new one through `setDelay` (S66): an increase inserts exactly that much silence (S47), a decrease skips forward (S48), and nothing is ever played twice (S49); an increase of more than 5 s instead restarts the reader and plays at once at the new hold-back, replaying up to that increase (S187, large increases). A renderer whose hold-back is unchanged gets no call, so it gets no resync. An output being attached starts at its current hold-back (S117, S45). When m changes, the hold-back of every output already taking part whose own offset didn't change moves by that same change: a Bluetooth speaker at −250 switched on next to the built-in speakers and a USB speaker at +150 makes those two wait 250 ms longer (250 ms of silence each, then in step with it), and switched off again, they skip 250 ms forward; editing that Bluetooth speaker's offset to −300 leaves its own hold-back at 0 and moves the other two 50 ms later. A poll that finds the AirPlay engine unreachable leaves the listed set as it was (S81), so it re-times nothing. This replaces S80, where only the AirPlay flip re-timed outputs and every hold-back went to 0 or came back from it.
- **S178** *Required.* The AirPlay latency L of S176, the time from a frame's arrival to the moment the AirPlay speakers are taken to sound it, depends on the AirPlay sender and only counts while an AirPlay speaker is chosen. Each sender has one stored host setting for it, kept across launches, which the window shows as the *AirPlay delay* (S181); the user tunes it to line the reference up with the AirPlay speakers. Input that isn't finite is ignored and other input is clamped.
  - *Retired with the bundled engine (2026-10-20); kept as a record.* *Bundled engine (the Mac's AirPlay sender before AudioSync's own).* The engine reports no latency, so the setting is absolute: L is the stored AirPlay delay, 0 to 20 000 ms, 2000 by default (S77's default; `README.md`: AirPlay speakers play about two seconds late). It takes over the role the reference's own delay had under S77 (S179). In the usual case h(x) = L + o′(x), which is S78's "configured delay" with configured = L + offset.
  - *Native sender* (AirPlay spec P98, P113, P114). The sender reports its base latency L_s, counted on the sender clock from c(n) as P114 says (before its first report, the configured Lf, P98, 1750 ms by default), and the setting is an adjustment A on top of it: −2000 to +20 000 ms, 0 by default, with `L = clamp(L_s + A, 0, 20 000)`. A is relative, so it stays right when L_s changes; no absolute latency is ever stored for this sender, since it would go stale with the next Lf change or timeline restart. A covers receivers that sound later (or earlier) than L_s says. This replaces P114's L + d_local with d_local = clamp(configured − L, 0, 3000): the trim is now the offset, which may be negative down to −L and isn't capped at 3000 ms (only h is capped, S176), and P114's one-time migration is replaced by S179 and S180. An AirPlay speaker's own delay d_s (P99) is unaffected and still moves that speaker alone.
  - *Retired with the bundled engine (2026-10-20); kept as a record.* The two settings are separate. The Mac, which could use either sender, kept both, and switching senders switched settings; neither was ever converted into the other. The Mac now reads only the native sender's adjustment; S179 still stores the bundled engine's value once, for older builds.
  - A change of L (the user edits the AirPlay delay; the native sender reports a new L_s after an Lf change or a timeline restart, P98, P143) re-times every output taking part (S177).
- **S179** *Required.* Mac migration, once. Before this model the Mac stored an absolute delay per device UID (S77, the `localDelayMsByUID` defaults key) that applied only while an AirPlay speaker was chosen, and played outputs without one at D0: the legacy single delay (the `delayMs` key) clamped to 0 to 20 000, else 2000. The first time a build with this model has both its stored values and a device list, it takes R from S173 and D_R, the reference's stored delay, or D0 when it has none or there is no reference, and stores:
  - the bundled engine's AirPlay delay (S178), L_new = D_R when D_R ≥ 1000 ms, a value tuned against AirPlay; else 2000 ms, since a smaller value can only have been meant to line local outputs up with each other;
  - for every other UID x with a stored delay D_x: o(x) = clamp(D_x − D_R, −2000, 20 000);
  - for every other UID without a stored delay that is switched on (the `enabledLocalUIDs` key) or in the device list of that moment (S171): o(x) = clamp(D0 − L_new, −2000, 20 000) when D0 ≥ 1000 ms, which keeps its D0 sound with AirPlay (with D_R ≥ 1000 that is D0 − D_R), and o(x) = clamp(D0 − D_R, −2000, 20 000) when D0 is below 1000 ms, which keeps its place relative to the reference (so untuned outputs that all played at D0 keep playing together, also when the reference is one of them); nothing for any other UID (offset 0);
  - the native sender's adjustment A (S178) = 0: L_s isn't known at this moment, and no stored delay was ever read for that sender except through P114's clamp;
  - nothing for R: its old delay became the AirPlay delay, or is dropped when below 1000 ms.

  The new values go under new keys (`localOffsetMsByUID`, `airPlayDelayMs`, `nativeAirPlayAdjustmentMs`), and the migration counts as done once a separate flag, `localDelayModelMigrated`, is set; it is written last, so a migration cut short runs again from the same old values. The old keys are left as they are, so an older build still finds its own values and a newer one never reads them again. Effect, with the bundled engine: with an AirPlay speaker chosen and D_R ≥ 1000, every output with a stored delay, and every switched-on or listed output that played at D0, plays exactly as before as long as its offset needed no clamping (h = D_R + D_x − D_R = D_x, with D_x = D0 for one without a stored delay, S176); without AirPlay, outputs now line up by their offsets where they all used to play at once. Under the native sender (the Mac's only one since 2026-10-20) the offsets apply with the reported L_s (S178) instead of P114's clamp. Worked examples, in ms, hold-backs listed in the order of the first column:

  | Stored before | D0 | Stored after | h with AirPlay chosen | h without AirPlay, all on |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | R 2000, USB 2100, Bluetooth 1800 | 2000 | AirPlay delay 2000; USB +100, Bluetooth −200 | 2000, 2100, 1800 (as before) | 200, 300, 0 (before: 0, 0, 0) |
  | R none, HDMI TV 2400 | 2000 | AirPlay delay 2000; TV +400 | 2000, 2400 (as before) | 0, 400 |
  | R 2300, USB none (listed) | 2000 | AirPlay delay 2300; USB −300 | 2300, 2000 (as before) | 300, 0 |
  | R none, USB none (listed) | 0 (legacy `delayMs` 0) | AirPlay delay 2000; USB 0 | 2000, 2000 (before: 0, 0, about two seconds ahead of AirPlay) | 0, 0 |
  | R 2500, Bluetooth 2250 | 2000 | AirPlay delay 2500; Bluetooth −250 | 2500, 2250 (as before) | 250, 0 |
  | R 250, Bluetooth 0, USB 400 | 2000 | AirPlay delay 2000; Bluetooth −250, USB +150 | 2000, 1750, 2150 (before: 250, 0, 400, about two seconds ahead of AirPlay) | 250, 0, 400 |
  | R 2500, USB 0 | 2000 | AirPlay delay 2500; USB −2000 (clamped from −2500) | 2500, 500 (before: 2500, 0) | 2000, 0 |
- **S180** *Required.* Windows migration, once. Before this model the Windows app stored per output a trim from 0 to 20 000 ms (`delay_ms` in its settings file, 0 by default) that applied with or without AirPlay, as L_s + min(trim, 3000) while an AirPlay speaker was chosen (L_s as in S178), and per AirPlay speaker its own delay d_s from 0 to 3000 ms (P99). When it loads a settings file of an older format version, the first time it has both that file and an output list, it takes R from S174 and t_R, the reference's stored trim (0 when it has none or there is no reference), and stores:
  - for every other output x with a stored trim t_x: o(x) = clamp(t_x − t_R, −2000, 20 000);
  - the native sender's adjustment A (S178) = t_R, so the reference keeps sounding t_R after L_s;
  - nothing for R; every AirPlay speaker's own delay d_s stays as it is.

  It then writes the file in the new format version (an older build keeps a newer file aside, as it does today). Without AirPlay the outputs keep their alignment (S176 subtracts the smallest offset taking part, so only a delay common to all of them goes). With AirPlay every local output plays exactly as before, h = L_s + t_R + t_x − t_R = L_s + t_x, as long as its old trim was at most 3000 ms (the old cap) and its offset needed no clamping, and the AirPlay speakers keep L_s + d_s, so a user who lined a late AirPlay speaker up by giving R a trim keeps that alignment, now in the AirPlay delay. Where t_R is 0, the default, every offset equals the old trim and A is 0. Example: R 250, Bluetooth 0, TV 120 and an AirPlay speaker with d_s 0 become Bluetooth −250, TV −130 and A = 250; without AirPlay, all on, the hold-backs stay 250, 0 and 120; with AirPlay and L_s = 1750, L = 2000 and they are 2000, 1750 and 1870 with the AirPlay speaker at L_s + 0 = 1750, all as before, and the window shows an AirPlay delay of 2000 (S181).
- **S181** *Required.* What the windows show (UI facts; the wording goes through `UIStrings` on the Mac and the Windows app's own strings):
  - The reference's row has no delay field, whether the reference is on or off.
  - Every other local output's row shows its offset with its sign: "+150 ms", "−250 ms", and "0 ms" for zero. The field takes whole ms with an optional leading "+" or "−" (a hyphen-minus counts as "−"), clamped to S175's range; other input is ignored and the field shows the stored value again.
  - One short hint beside the delay fields says what they are relative to, naming the reference (the built-in speakers on the Mac, the reference's name on Windows), and which way to move a value for a speaker heard first or late; the Mac adds that a negative value is for a speaker that sounds late, such as Bluetooth. With no reference, it says that the speaker with the lowest value plays first and the others wait by the difference.
  - The AirPlay delay (S178) is one field in the AirPlay part of the window, not on any local row, with a hint that the local speakers wait that long for the AirPlay speakers. With the native sender, the only one since 2026-10-20 (with the bundled engine it showed and edited the stored value), it shows L = clamp(L_s + A, 0, 20 000) in whole ms and follows L_s when that changes; entering v stores A = clamp(v − L_s, −2000, 20 000) with the L_s of that moment, so the field then shows v (unless clamped). An AirPlay speaker's own delay d_s (P99), where a window offers it (the Windows app doesn't today), stays a plain 0 to 20 000 ms value.
  - Hold-backs (S176) are not shown.

### Microphone calibration of one output's offset

S232 to S234 let the host measure one local output against the reference with a microphone and fill in that output's offset (S175), instead of the user finding it by ear. The core makes the test signal and does the estimate; the host plays the signal, records, and stores the result. AudioSync's own design from DSP textbook methods (a matched filter, that is a cross-correlation with the known signal); no other project's calibration was studied.

- **S232** *Required.* The measurement. The host plays the test signal (S233) on the reference alone and records it with the microphone, then plays the same signal on the chosen output alone with the microphone where it was, and passes both recordings to the estimate: mono, at the signal's rate, the two outputs at the hold-backs they have while both take part (S176), so the only things that differ between the two runs are the output and its offset. Frame 0 of each recording is the moment the signal enters that output's path, before its hold-back, so the hold-back is part of the arrival; the host trims each recording to that moment, or starts it at the same distance before it in both runs. A gap from recording start to play start that differs between the runs goes into d unseen and can flip the result's sign. Each recording lasts at least the largest hold-back taking part (about 2 s while an AirPlay speaker is chosen, S178, up to 20 s with large offsets), plus the output's latency, plus the 0.75 s signal, plus a margin of about half a second. The host records at 48 kHz: S234's limit of 2^21 frames is about 44 s there but only about 10.9 s at 192 kHz, which a 20 s hold-back would pass. The Mac host records at the input device's own rate and refuses before anything plays, as too long, when the recording would pass that limit there, so a long hold-back at a high microphone rate is never cut short. The estimate finds the signal's arrival in each recording (S234), a_R in the reference's and a_x in the chosen output's, in ms. Only the difference d = a_x − a_R is used, so the microphone's input latency and the time between the signal's start and the recording's start cancel out as long as they are the same in both runs. The new offset is `o_new = clamp(round(o_old − d), −2000, +20 000)` ms, whole ms in S175's range, where o_old is the output's stored offset read as S175 reads it (0 when it isn't finite, else clamped). A result the clamp changed is still accepted but says it was clamped, so the host can tell the user the output stays off by the part cut away. The sign follows S172: a chosen output heard d ms later than the reference sounds late, so it is held back d ms less, and one heard earlier is held back more. A difference larger in size than the whole offset range, 22 000 ms, which no two offsets in range explain, is refused as implausible. A refused measurement, for this reason or one of S234's, never changes the offset. Core tests "calibrate: ...". The signal needs no music: on the Mac a process tap whose source plays nothing delivers no block, so from the moment a calibration fades the music out until it gives the outputs back, the host writes silence in its place, at the capture rate, whenever the capture has given nothing for 40 ms, and the signal enters every path on that silence (logic tests "calibration fill: ..."); the Windows capture's gap fill keeps its ring moving the same way at all times.
- **S233** *Required.* The test signal: a linear sweep from 300 Hz to 8 kHz over 0.75 s, peaking at −18 dBFS (a linear amplitude of 0.125), mono, with a raised-cosine fade of 20 ms at each end so neither end clicks (the first and last millisecond stay under 1 % of the peak). It is defined in continuous time and sampled at the rate asked for, so it is the same sound at any rate: the host may play it at the output's rate and give the estimate the signal made at the microphone's rate. Rates from 22 050 to 384 000 Hz are made (below 22 050 Hz the sweep's top would pass the Nyquist frequency); its length is `round(0.75 × rate)` frames. Why a sweep: its autocorrelation has one sharp peak (the main lobe is about 1 / 7.7 kHz, some 0.13 ms, wide, and outside 3 ms its sidelobes stay near 1 % of the peak), its energy is spread evenly over a band that small speakers and built-in microphones cover, and unlike a noise burst it is the same at every rate. The estimate needs far less than −18 dBFS at the microphone (S234), so the level is chosen to be quiet rather than for the estimate.
- **S234** *Required.* Arrival and confidence. The arrival in a recording is the lag of the largest magnitude of the cross-correlation of the recording with the signal, over every lag where the whole signal lies inside the recording. The correlation runs through a radix-2 FFT of the recording's length rounded up to a power of two, which never wraps those lags: for two 3 s recordings at 48 kHz the whole estimate takes about 45 ms in a release build on the PC, where the plain sum would take seconds. A recording is refused, naming which one, when:
  - *Too short:* it is shorter than the signal plus 100 ms (fewer lags would not show a noise floor), or the signal passed is not the one for the rate.
  - *Too long:* it holds more than 2^21 frames (about 44 s at 48 kHz), which bounds the estimate's memory: at that length it peaks near 96 MB (two 2^21-point complex buffers of 32 MB each, their twiddles and the correlation).
  - *Clipped:* 3 samples in a row have a magnitude of 0.99 or more (the flat top of a clipped waveform; a single full-scale sample, a click, is let through), or a sample isn't finite.
  - *Too quiet:* the correlation is 0 everywhere (silence), or its peak is less than 12 times the median of its magnitude over all lags. Noise alone reaches 6.4 to 7.8 in the core's runs, so it is refused with room to spare; white noise 20 dB louder than the signal (per-sample RMS) still gives about 29; by the matched filter's gain, the square root of the signal's 36 000 frames at 48 kHz (about 190), the threshold lies near −27 dB.
  - *Ambiguous echo:* another lag more than 3 ms from the peak reaches over 0.7 of it. Then a reflection or a second sound is nearly as strong as the arrival and either may be the direct one. Within 3 ms, about 1 m of extra path, a reflection is taken as part of the arrival itself. The signal's own sidelobes outside 3 ms stay near 0.01, and noise alone mostly reaches 0.8 to 0.99, so the check also backs up the one above. A reflection stronger than a blocked direct path is measured as the arrival and is not refused; the host asks for a clear line from the speaker to the microphone.

  The thresholds are constants of the core (`core/src/calibrate.rs`), checked by tests that put noise alone, a signal under noise, and echoes of known strength through them.

### Calibrate All Speakers

S235 to S241 measure every speaker that is on in one run, local outputs, Cast speakers and AirPlay speakers alike, each against the reference as S232 to S234 measure one output, and show the results on a sheet before anything changes. The core plans the run, works out how long a Cast speaker takes, judges each speaker's two sweeps, sets the shared AirPlay delay and lists what Apply writes (`core/src/calibrate_all.rs`); the host plays, records, shows the sheet and stores the changes. AudioSync's own design; no other project's calibration was studied.

- **S235** *Required.* The run. The host names the reference, a local output, and every speaker that is on, each with its class (local output, Cast speaker or AirPlay speaker), its stored delay or none, and its expected arrival: when its sweep should appear in a recording whose frame 0 is the moment the sweep enters its path before its hold-back (S232), so the hold-back plus the latency as the host estimates it, the reference's included. The run plays the reference's sweep first, then each other speaker's two sweeps, one speaker at a time with the others silent, in the order given. Each recording lasts the expected arrival plus the 0.75 s sweep plus 1 s, at least the sweep plus 100 ms and at most 2^21 frames (S234); an expected arrival that isn't a finite number counts as 0. A Cast speaker without a stored offset has no estimate: its first window is the 2^21-frame limit, marked so the host can say it is searching, and its second is the first sweep's arrival plus the sweep plus 1 s. A run needs a supported rate (S233), a reference among the speakers, unique IDs, and at least one speaker besides the reference; a run in which any window but a searching Cast speaker's would pass the 2^21-frame limit at that rate is refused as too long before anything plays, since a window cut short loses the sweep and reads as too quiet (at 192 kHz that is an expected arrival past about 9.2 s). Microphone calibration measures external speakers only: the Mac host leaves out of a run, and of the single-speaker calibration, each local output the microphone can't hear, namely one with a virtual transport; a Bluetooth output whose name says headphones; and any other output whose output data source is the headphone jack or whose first output stream's terminal is headphones (or the USB headphones, head-mounted or headset terminal). Over Bluetooth only the name decides: Core Audio documents no kind for Bluetooth outputs, and macOS reports Bluetooth speakers with the headphones terminal as well, so a Bluetooth speaker takes part whatever its terminal or data source. The sheet lists them with the reason and never sweeps them; Include Anyway puts one in the runs of that sheet, for a speaker that reports the wrong kind, until the sheet closes. With the reference on the headphone jack nothing can start (logic tests "calibration: ..." and "calibrate all: ...").
- **S236** *Required.* Two sweeps and the uncertainty. Each sweep's arrival is found as S234 finds it, and its difference from the reference's arrival is how much later the speaker was heard. A difference beyond the 22 000 ms any two offsets in range can span is refused as implausible (S232). A row's difference is the mean of its two sweeps, and its uncertainty U is half their difference plus the 3 ms guard of S234; a row with U over 10 ms is uncertain, shown but never applied. Any refused sweep refuses the row with that reason (too quiet, which includes a peak under 12 times the noise floor, clipped, an ambiguous echo, too short, too long, implausible) and is never averaged with the other; a refused first sweep skips the second, and a refused reference sweep refuses every row and ends the run. The new value, rounded to whole ms: a local output's offset (S175) moves down by the difference, clamped to −2000 to +20 000 ms; a Cast speaker's measured offset and the AirPlay delay (S178) move up by it, since the other outputs wait for those speakers, clamped to 0 to 20 000 ms, the largest hold-back (S176). The Cast sender stores offsets up to 120 000 ms, but a stored Cast offset above 20 000 ms is measured from 20 000 ms, the largest value in use, so one stored at 30 000 ms and heard 40 ms before the reference gets 19 960 ms. A row says it was clamped only when the range cut its value; a value that lands exactly on an end of the range is not clamped.
- **S237** *Required.* The AirPlay delay. AirPlay speakers share one delay (S178), so every AirPlay speaker is measured and the new delay is the old one plus the median of the certain AirPlay rows' differences (the mean of the middle two for an even count). The spread, the largest minus the smallest of those differences, is reported; over 10 ms the sheet says that the AirPlay speakers differ by that much and that only one AirPlay delay can be set, and the median still applies. A new delay the 0 to 20 000 ms range cut says it was clamped, as a row does (S236). Without a certain AirPlay row the delay stays. Reason: AirPlay speakers keep in step among themselves, so a large spread points at the room or the microphone's position rather than at the delay, and averaging it silently would hide that.
- **S238** *Required.* The sheet, Apply and Undo. Nothing changes during the run. The sheet lists every speaker in the order given: the reference, the old value, the new value ± U, or the reason it was refused or not measured. Apply lists one change per certain local or Cast row and one for the AirPlay delay when an AirPlay row is certain, each with the value it replaces (none when the speaker had no stored value); it is refused while no row is certain. One Apply is one change with one Undo, which restores exactly those replaced values. An applied sheet is spent: a second Apply and Measure Again are refused, since its rows describe offsets that are no longer stored and measuring or applying again would undo the correction.
- **S239** *Required.* Measure Again. Any row but the reference's can be measured again: a new run of the reference's sweep and that speaker's two, from the values the sheet started with, whose steps name the sheet's rows. Its result replaces that row alone, and the AirPlay delay is worked out again. A Measure Again run that is cancelled or ended by a change (S240) is dropped: the sheet and that row stay exactly as they were.
- **S240** *Required.* Ending a run. A cancel, a change of the speaker list, a speaker taking part turned off or a switch of the source stops the run: no more steps, and its sheet ends with that reason, keeping the rows measured so far for display, with nothing to apply. A run finished with steps still waiting ends as cancelled.
- **S241** *Required.* A Cast speaker's wait. The expected arrival the host gives a Cast speaker (S235), which is also how long the music already sent to it plays out before the first sweep, comes from one rule both hosts call: its stored offset, counted up to 20 000 ms, plus the Cast feed's hold-back plus 500 ms; with no stored offset (none, or 0 ms, which the sender keeps after Undo) the hold-back plus a starting guess by the format the session plays, 3000 ms for WAV and 6000 ms for FLAC or a format not reported yet (CAST_SPEC C77a; the FLAC figure is derived, not measured). A negative offset, or one that is not a number, also counts as no stored offset, here and for the searching window of S235. The guess never delays another output. Reason: a guess too short lets music overlap the first sweep, one too long only costs time, and leaving the hold-back out made a host wait too little while AirPlay holds the Cast feed back.

- **S203** *Retired with the FIFO feed (2026-10-20); kept as a record.* *Required* (`docs/EDGE_CASES.md` EC3; maintainer's ear test of 2026-10-18: an AirPlay TV and the Mac's speakers were in step at a 500 ms delay before a sleep, and after the wake the delay still read 500 ms but AirPlay was far behind; core test "after_a_stall_the_open_pipe_resumes_as_close_to_live_as_a_fresh_start", which failed with "first frame 250 ms behind live after the stall, 10 ms after a fresh start" before the core's writer followed this clause; FIFO check "fifo: resumes at the live edge after a stall (S203)"). *Live edge after a stall.* The pipe *stalls* when its reader keeps it open but stops reading (the engine pauses its player, for instance while its speakers reconnect after a wake), so the pipe fills, writes are refused (`EAGAIN`, or a write that takes nothing, S93) and the unwritten audio grows past the backlog cap: the pipe took nothing for longer than the 250 ms of S90. No reopen follows, so S169 never fires, and S90 alone would leave the newest 250 ms waiting for the reader, which would then play them first: AirPlay would come back up to 250 ms later than before the stall and stay that far out of step with the local outputs at their saved delays (S80). So from the step where a stall begins until the pipe takes data again, the writer keeps only the newest audio, at most one write's worth (128 frames, under 3 ms, S93), and drops everything older: one backlog skip for the whole stall, however long it lasts, with every frame it drops in `backlogSkipSamples` (S160); the first frame the pipe takes after the stall is then as close to live as after an open (S169), no more than 50 ms further behind live than after a fresh start (in the core test: 2.7 ms after the stall, 10 ms after a fresh start). Audio dropped: what waited when the stall began, up to the cap's 250 ms, and what is fed during it, except the newest write's worth; the rest of a partly written frame is still completed (S93), and nothing is written twice or out of order (S90). A pipe that takes nothing while less than the cap piles up (a short stall the cap absorbs) loses nothing: the audio that waited follows on without a gap. A backlog past the cap while the pipe keeps taking data (a writer that wasn't scheduled for a while) is not a stall: S90 alone governs it. The audio already in the kernel's pipe buffer when the writes began to fail (up to 64 KiB, about a third of a second, on macOS, S93) is beyond the writer's reach and reaches the reader first. *Required* detail: a stall begins at a step that finds the last write refused and more than `BACKLOG_CAP_FRAMES` frames unwritten (counting a partly written frame as one), and ends once the pipe has taken a whole write's worth (512 bytes) since it began, or at a disconnect (S92) or an open (S169). A pipe its reader left nearly full can still take a few shorter writes, less than 512 bytes in all, and they must not end the stall: the backlog would grow back towards the cap before the next refusal began a new one. A write interrupted by a signal changes nothing. The writer thread does this with no allocation (S129), and the capture side is unchanged (S95, S126). *Unverified risk* (review of 2026-10-19): an engine that kept the pipe full during normal playback, refusing writes now and then, would let clock drift push the backlog slowly past the cap; S90 alone trimmed a few frames there, and this rule drops about 250 ms at once. The 60-minute soak (`Tests/soak.sh`) reports the backlog skip counter, which should stay flat during steady playback; one that climbs points here.

## 12. Non-finite values

- **S123** *Observed* ("eq: recovers from a NaN sample (silence stays silent)", "fifo: NaN/±inf samples do not crash"). NaN and ±infinity can arrive from capture. ## 13. Real-time constraints

- **S126** *Required* (project rule; the equalizer's design follows it, `Equalizer.swift:7-9`). The audio path is the capture callback (equalizer, ring write, FIFO `feed`) and every renderer callback (reader, conversion, gain). On it, nothing may allocate or free memory, take a lock (mutex, read-write lock, semaphore, dispatch sync, runtime calls that may lock), block or sleep, make a system call, log or build strings, or touch the file system. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S127** *Required.* Work per callback is bounded and linear in the block size: O(N) per reader, O(20·N) biquad steps for the equalizer, O(N) for conversion.
- **S128** *Required.* Values shared with audio threads are lock-free atomics only (an implementation SHOULD enforce this at compile time where its language allows); all buffers (ring, FIFO queue, equalizer state, converter buffers sized for the largest device buffer, at least 8192 frames) are allocated before the audio threads start and freed only after they stopped.
- **S129** *Retired with the FIFO feed (2026-10-20); kept as a record.* *Required.* The FIFO writer thread does not allocate after `start`, shares no lock with the audio path, does no logging inside its loop, and performs only `open`, `write`, `close` and short sleeps.
- **S130** *Observed* (`Equalizer.swift:97-104`, `:128-137`). Control-to-audio updates never make the audio thread wait: scalars are atomic stores; multi-value updates are double-buffered and flipped atomically; the control side MAY spin or yield briefly. *Now covered by the core.*
- **S131** *Required.* Logging about audio-path events (underruns, corrections, drops) happens only from non-real-time threads, from counters the audio path increments atomically. The debug log (`AUDIOSYNC_DEBUG=1`, `docs/DEVELOPMENT.md`) is never written from the audio path.

## 14. Threading

- **S132** *Required; Observed* in part (`Equalizer.swift`, `AppDelegate*.swift`). Threads and ownership:

  | Thread | Runs | Writes | Reads |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | main | control interface, callbacks, polls, equalizer settings | reader targets and resync flags, gains, equalizer idle set and active index, FIFO start/stop | counters, `lagFrames`, `framesCaptured` |
  | capture (one Core Audio IO thread) | equalizer, ring write, FIFO `feed` | ring frames and W, equalizer state, FIFO queue tail, `samplesQueued` | equalizer active set, FIFO queue head |
  | one renderer IO thread per output | reader, conversion, gain | its reader's P, F, `lagFrames`, `framesRendered` | ring frames and W, its target, resync flag, gain |
  | FIFO writer (one) | dequeue, backlog cap, pipe writes | FIFO queue head | FIFO queue tail |

- **S133** *Required.* Atomics and orderings: W release/acquire, plus the writer's release fence after publishing W and the reader's acquire fence before re-loading it (S37, S40); reader target delay, gain and FIFO run flag relaxed (a target is stored before its resync flag, and the flag is set with release and taken with an acquire exchange); `framesRendered`, `lagFrames` and `samplesQueued` relaxed; FIFO queue indices release/acquire; equalizer `active` and `reading` indices sequentially consistent (S31). `framesCaptured` is W.
- **S134** *Required.* Teardown order: stop a device's IO (Core Audio returns after the callback in progress finished) before freeing what that callback uses; stop capture before resetting the ring; stop the FIFO writer before freeing its queue.
- **S135** *Required.* Ring samples are stored as relaxed atomic 32-bit words holding the float bit patterns (e.g. `AtomicU32` in Rust), so a reader racing the writer's overwrite is not undefined behavior; on mainstream targets these compile to plain loads and stores. Ordering comes from the W release/acquire pair, the writer's release fence (S37), the reader's acquire fence and the post-copy check (S40). No locks.

  | output device lost | `onOutputGone(uid)` | keep enabled, re-add when back (S68) |
  | pipe has no reader / reader gone | none (writer retries) | none (S89, S92) |
  | AirPlay engine down, crashing or unsupported | lifecycle state | restart, give up, Retry (S98) |

  Notices are capped at 4, newest first (S153).

- **S205** *Required* (AudioSync design; core tests "a failure after a caught panic says where it happened and never what it said", "… a panic in an input write …", "… the driver panicked says where the latest panic happened"; CI's panic probe). *Where the bug that stopped AudioSync's own sender is.* When a bug (a panic) stops the AirPlay sender, the failure event the host takes (`ASC_AIRPLAY_EVENT_FAILED`) says in its detail where the panic happened: `panic at <file>:<line>`, the source file from its crate's folder on (`airplay/src/sender/driver.rs`; the core's own files as `src/…`), with forward slashes and nothing above that folder, so no home folder or account name. The panic's message never goes there: it may hold anything, a receiver's reply included. A panic inside a call on the sender or its input is named exactly. When one of the sender's own threads ended instead (its driver, or discovery), no call caught anything, and the detail names the most recent panic in the process since that sender was created as `latest panic at <file>:<line>`, which, with one sender, is usually the one that ended it (a session thread may have recovered from it, so the wording doesn't claim more); with no panic since, a discovery that ended for another reason included, the detail is empty. The core notes the locations with a panic hook it installs once, when the first sender is created, and that hook then runs the one that was there before (the default hook still prints to standard error). Noting a location allocates only while a panic is under way, which allocates anyway (S126). The host writes the detail to its debug log with the failure (the Mac's `AUDIOSYNC_DEBUG` log) and never shows it to the user. The release build keeps unwinding, and CI checks that in the library the app links: it builds the same prelinked library with an extra entry that panics inside a sender call's guard (a build option the app's library never has), links it into a small C program and expects the call's failure value, the failure event with a location, and the program's normal exit.

## 16. Edge cases

- **S137** *Device unplug and replug.* *Observed* (S68). The renderer stops, `outputLost` fires, the UID stays enabled; on replug the reconcile that Core Audio's device-list notice brings (S101) re-adds it with its configured delay and gain. *Required:* the new reader anchors at `W − De` (S45), so it is in sync on its first callback, and nothing from before the unplug is replayed.
- **S138** *Bluetooth drop.* *Superseded in part by S175*: a Bluetooth device that sounds late gets a negative offset instead of "a little less delay". *Observed* (S68; `README.md`: Bluetooth adds its own lag, so users set a little less delay). Handled like an unplug. *Required:* a device that returns at a different nominal rate (e.g. a headset profile) is rendered through S62; its extra latency is not compensated automatically.
- **S139** *Sleep and wake.* *Retired in part with the bundled engine (2026-10-20)*: the Observed part (the output poll, the wake grace, pipe playback) describes that engine and is a record; the Required part still stands for the capture and the local outputs, all but its sentence on the FIFO feed, which went with it. *Observed* (`AirPlayReconcile.swift:140-191`; `AppDelegate+AirPlay.swift:9-20`, `:234-262`; tests "EC3 sleep: …", "EC3 wake: …"). The host follows the system's sleep and wake notices; the displays waking also counts as a wake while the host still takes the Mac to be asleep, in case the wake notice is missed. From going to sleep until waking it sends no output poll, and an answer or a failure that comes back meanwhile decides nothing and doesn't count toward reachability (S102). A wake polls at once and opens a wake grace of 20 s, counted from the engine's first answer after the wake, however long that takes; failed polls don't start it. During the grace a wanted `open` speaker the engine reports off is selected again instead of being dropped, at most once per poll (S103, S104), and pipe playback is requested again with each such selection (S99). A speaker the user switched off stays off, one that isn't `open` follows S106–S107, and a new engine run is a restart (S102, S143). After the grace the usual rules apply (S104, S106–S109): an `open` speaker still reported off is dropped as unreachable with a notice (S148). *Required:* nothing else needs dedicated handling, and nothing may crash: a callback gap of any length is a stall (S51, S55: resume at the exact delay, no replay); a capture format change after wake goes through S20; a device that vanished goes through S68; capture that never resumes surfaces through S18; the FIFO backlog cap (S90) keeps stale audio from reaching AirPlay, and a pipe the engine kept open but stopped reading across the sleep resumes at the live edge (S203), so the writer's own backlog adds no lag after the wake; what sat in the kernel's pipe buffer still plays first.
- **S140** *Sample-rate change.* *Observed* (S20, S30). A capture rate change restarts the session: new ring history at the new Rc, equalizer coefficients recomputed and state zeroed, FIFO resampler re-rated, renderers re-added. *Required:* an output device's rate change is absorbed by its renderer's converter (S62) with the reader position untouched; if the renderer can't reconfigure, it reports `outputLost` (S68).
- **S141** *Source switch.* *Retired in part with the bundled engine (2026-10-20)*: the engine's playback, stopped and requested again, is a record; the rest stands. *Observed* (`AppDelegate+Outputs.swift:149-163`). Stop without deselecting AirPlay (deselecting would race the reselect), then start a user-origin session with the new source: the ring is reset and renderers are re-added; AirPlay speakers stay selected, while the engine's playback is stopped with the old session and requested again shortly after the new one starts (`AppDelegate+Engine.swift`).
- **S163** *Source switch decision.* *Observed* (`AudioSource.swift:77-93`; `AppDelegate+Outputs.swift:149-162`; tests "EC5 source: …"). Choosing the source already in use changes nothing. Choosing another one while not streaming only stores it for the next start. Choosing another one while streaming, an input device included, rebuilds the capture as in S141, with the speakers kept on and the AirPlay speakers kept selected. Any real change clears the old source's "source" and "capture" notices.
- **S142** *Source app quits.* *Observed* (`AppDelegate+Engine.swift`, `sourceAppQuit`; `AppLifecycle.swift`, `sourceQuit`). With S227's setting at Stop, its default: When the app whose bundle ID is the source terminates during a session: stop, post a "source" notice (no modal alert). Stopping ends every AirPlay speaker's session, but the speakers the user chose stay chosen, and the source stays that app, so the next start plays to the same speakers from the same app (`AppDelegate.swift`, `appQuit`, and `AppDelegate+Engine.swift`, `stopEngine`; Windows: `windows/src/audio/controller.rs`, `StopReason::SourceQuit`). Before 2026-10-21 this clause said every AirPlay speaker was deselected, which the bundled engine's host did and neither app has done since that engine went (2026-10-20).
- **S164** *Source app quit decision.* *Observed* (`AudioSource.swift`, `SourceQuit`; `AppLifecycle.swift`, `sourceQuit`; core `policy/source.rs`, `depends_on_app`; tests "EC6 app quit: …"). Only an app source ends with a quit, and only when the quitting app's bundle ID equals the source's. Another app quitting, an app without a bundle ID, and any quit while the source is the whole system or an input device change nothing.
- **S227** *When the source app quits.* *Required* (AudioSync design, 2026-10-21; core tests "source quit: …"; the Windows app follows it, `windows/src/audio/controller.rs`, `SourceQuitWatch`, tests "an app that quits gives way to the whole system …"; the Mac app asks the core through `asc_source_quit_decide`, `AppLifecycle.sourceQuit` and `AppDelegate.sourceAppQuit`, logic tests "source quit: …" and "EC6 app quit: …"). A setting chooses what a running session does when the app it captures quits: *Stop* (the default) or *Switch to whole-system audio*. It is stored as `stop` or `system`; a missing or unknown value reads as Stop, so a copy that never stored it, or an older copy reading a newer value, behaves as S142 says. Whether a quit concerns the session stays S164's decision: only an app source, and only its own app quitting (on Windows, where the capture itself reports that its app ended, the host passes the source's own app ID). With Stop the session does what S142 says: it stops and posts the "source" notice, the AirPlay speakers the user chose stay chosen, and the source stays that app, so the next start plays to the same speakers from it again. With Switch the session goes on with the whole system as its source, the way a source change does (S141, S163, S186): every output fades out on the app and in on the system audio, the AirPlay speakers stay selected, the stored source becomes the whole system (S12), and an informational "source" notice names the app that quit and says that whole-system audio plays now. Edge cases: (a) a quit while no session runs changes nothing under either setting, and the source stays the app; (b) while the source is already the whole system, or an input device, no quit concerns it (S164), so nothing stops and nothing switches; (c) the app starting again after a switch changes nothing: AudioSync stays on whole-system audio and never switches back by itself, since other apps may be playing by then, and only the user picking the app again makes it the source; (d) changing the setting while a session plays does nothing at that moment: the setting in force when the quit arrives decides, and changing it after a switch doesn't undo the switch; (e) a device change doesn't hold the decision back: an output device coming or going (S161) doesn't rebuild the session, so the quit is handled as in any running session, and a session being rebuilt when the quit arrives (after a capture format change, S20 and S140, or on Windows a capture starting again after Windows audio restarted or its device went away) counts as running: Stop stops it and drops the rebuild, Switch lets that one rebuild start with whole-system audio instead of the app, never a second rebuild after it; (f) the same quit reported again (the system's notice and the capture's own report, or a repeated notice) changes nothing the second time, since by then the session has stopped or the source is the whole system. *Windows:* the setting is the check item File > Switch to All System Audio When the Source App Quits (on means Switch), kept in the settings file as `when_source_quits`; the notice reads "<app> quit, so AudioSync switched to All System Audio." With Switch, a capture of an app that quit before it could start (one waiting for the last session's fade, or a capture starting again after a loss) starts the whole system instead, which is the rebuild of (e); with Stop such a start ends streaming as before, with the notice that the capture stopped. *Mac:* the setting is the check item AudioSync > Switch to All System Audio When the Source App Quits in the app menu, next to Launch at Login, kept in the user defaults as `whenSourceQuits`; the notice reads the same. The quit reaches the Mac three ways, each through the same decision: macOS reporting an app that terminated, the capture finding its app gone (`.sourceLost(.appNotRunning)`, which stopped the session, so Switch starts the whole system), and the rebuild after a capture format change failing because the app is gone, which is the rebuild of (e); with Stop each ends as S142 says.
- **S143** *AirPlay engine restart.* *Retired with the bundled engine and the FIFO feed (2026-10-20); kept as a record.* *Observed* (S98, S102, S105, S81, S169, "fifo: reader closing mid-stream does not kill the process", "EC7 engine restart: the reopened pipe starts as close to live as a fresh start"). The pipe reader disappears; the FIFO writer survives and reopens (S92) at the live edge, so AirPlay comes back with the latency it had before and the local delays still line up (S169); the host restarts the engine after 2 s within the crash-loop limits; while it is unreachable the AirPlay list and local delays stay as they were; when it answers again, wanted speakers are re-selected at their own volume, and each one the new engine plays by itself gets it back, at the first answer that lists it (S105); pipe playback is requested at once (S99). Local outputs keep playing throughout.
- **S144** *All speakers off, then on.* *Observed* (S118, S119). Switching the last speaker off stops the session and deselects AirPlay; switching any on starts a new session (ring reset, S39) with the saved per-speaker delays and volumes.
- **S145** *Default output change.* *Required; Observed* basis (S60; `README.md` "Using it": keep the macOS output on a local device). Renderers are bound to device UIDs, so changing the macOS default output never moves, stops or re-times them. System capture taps processes, not a device, and continues; if the change alters the capture format, S20 applies. macOS's own AirPlay output is hidden from the list by default (S151).
- **S146** *Six or more speakers.* *Required.* There is no fixed limit on outputs: each local output costs one reader and one renderer, all reading one ring without coordination (S58); the engine MUST sustain at least 16 local outputs at 192 kHz within S127. Every AirPlay speaker is fed by the single pipe stream (the engine fans it out). *Observed:* reconcile, pairing and coalesced volume writes are per speaker with no cap (S74, S104, S108); notices are capped at 4 (S153).
- **S165** *Many speakers, as tested.* *Retired in part with the bundled engine (2026-10-20)*: the local half (eight local speakers attached together) still stands; the engine's eight AirPlay speakers are a record. *Observed* (tests "EC10 many speakers: …"; `AirPlayReconcile.swift:257-273`; `SpeakerLayout.swift:67-85`). No host decision caps the number of speakers. One built-in output plus eight USB and eight AirPlay speakers are all listed: the built-in one under This Mac, the sixteen others in one connected list in name order, numbers in numeric order (S152). Eight wanted AirPlay speakers that appear in the same poll are all selected by that poll's reconcile, which returns one action per reported speaker in the engine's order (S104). Eight enabled local speakers whose devices are present are all attached by one output sync (S161).
- **S147** *Long names.* *Required; Observed* basis (S101, S149). The engine keys everything by device UID or engine output ID, never by display name; names are display-only, may be arbitrarily long Unicode, and never pass through fixed-size buffers or the audio path. IDs are percent-encoded in API paths.
- **S166** *Long names, as tested.* *Observed* (tests "EC11 long names: …"). Names are compared whole and never shortened: two 257-character AirPlay names that differ only in their last character sort correctly after a short one and come out unchanged (S152), and a 263-character engine output name with an emoji and non-ASCII letters is parsed unchanged (S100).
- **S148** *AirPlay network loss.* *Retired with the bundled engine and the FIFO feed (2026-10-20); kept as a record*: what AudioSync's own AirPlay sender does when a speaker drops off the network is in `docs/AIRPLAY_SPEC.md`, the local hold-backs follow S177, and S118 stops streaming once nothing is left enabled. *Superseded in part by S177*: when no chosen AirPlay speaker remains, the local hold-backs lose the AirPlay latency L instead of switching to 0. *Observed* (S102–S109, S78–S80). Speakers that lose the network are reported off; after the settle rules they are dropped as unreachable, removed from the enabled set, and noticed; if no enabled, listed AirPlay speaker remains, local delays switch to 0 live (S80); if nothing at all remains enabled, streaming stops (S118). Speakers that stop advertising leave the engine's list (`CHANGELOG.md` 0.4.0). An engine that doesn't answer polls is not a network loss (S81, S102). A pipe nobody reads is bounded by S90, and resumes at the live edge once it is read again (S169, S203).
- **S167** *AirPlay network loss, as tested.* *Retired in part with the bundled engine (2026-10-20)*: the engine's list and its reconcile are a record; the Local Network hint for an empty list still stands (S154). *Observed* (tests "EC12 network loss: …"; `AirPlayReconcile.swift:257-273`; `AppDelegate+AirPlay.swift:33-46`). A wanted speaker missing from the engine's list gets no reconcile action and stays enabled, while the speakers the engine still reports get theirs (S104). While it is missing it isn't a chosen AirPlay speaker, so local outputs stop waiting for it (S79, S80). When the engine lists it again it counts as appeared, since appearance is judged against the previous poll's list, and is selected again (S104). A listed, wanted, open speaker (S106) that the engine reports off with nothing pending is dropped as unreachable with a notice (S106, S109). An empty list while Local Network access is allowed never shows the permission hint, however long it stays empty (S154).

- **S204** *Required* (the maintainer's decision for the switch to AudioSync's own sender, the one change of saved IDs it may make; tests "speaker IDs: …"). *Saved speaker IDs after the switch to AudioSync's own sender.* An AirPlay speaker is saved by its ID alone (switched on: `enabledAirPlayIds`; its volume: `airPlayVolumeByID`; the `ap:` keys of groups and of auto-connect), never with its name or address. Both senders take their ID from the receiver's device ID, the MAC-style address in its announcement (docs/AIRPLAY_SPEC.md P8, P9): the bundled engine writes that 48-bit number in decimal, AudioSync's own sender writes 12 uppercase hex digits behind `native:` (P10). So when the native sender's discovery reports a receiver (found or changed) whose receiver ID is 12 hex digits, every saved ID that is the same number in the engine's form (decimal digits only, no sign, space or leading zero, 1 to 2^48 − 1) becomes `native:<receiver ID>`, as an ID and inside `ap:` keys, before the receiver's row appears, so auto-connect and a speaker that was on act on the new ID at once. A volume already saved under the new ID wins over the old one; a group that already lists the new key drops the old one and otherwise keeps its order; a set never holds an ID twice. Every other ID and key stays as it is: local keys, IDs of receivers discovery hasn't listed, receivers without a device ID, anything not in the engine's form. An ID therefore moves once, when its receiver is first seen, and a second run changes nothing. The old ID is gone afterwards: back on the bundled engine (the hidden option), a moved speaker is off and outside its groups, like any speaker that engine never saw. The debug log has one line per moved ID. *Unverified* (no receiver here): that the bundled engine's output ID is the device ID in decimal; if it isn't, nothing matches, nothing moves, and the saved speakers stay off under the native sender, as before this clause. To check on a Mac with a receiver: a group saved under the bundled engine keeps its AirPlay speaker after the switch.

- **S225** *Observed* (`Tests/soakrules.swift`, `SoakRules`; soak rules suite, every check). The soak harness's memory rule. A run's memory growth is the last window's footprint minus the level the run settled at: the median of the last three windows inside the warm-up (of those there are, the mean of two when only two), the warm-up being the first 2 minutes, or the first half of a run shorter than 4 minutes.
